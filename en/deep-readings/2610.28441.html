Stratified Sparse Attention: Reducing the Sparse-to-Dense Gap at Extreme Sparsity

Sahil Joshi, Prithvi Dixit, Aryan Katiyar, Agniva Chowdhury, Joseph E. Gonzalez, Anshumali Shrivastava, Aditya Desai, Kumar Krishna Agrawal
24 minread4.30score

In long-context decoding, every new token reads the whole KV cache from HBM, so attention cost grows linearly with context. Sparse attention reads only a fraction of it, but at aggressive settings such as 50× sparsity, current methods remain far from dense attention: on RULER-HARD at 32K, Llama-3.1-8B scores 88.76 with dense attention and only 72.51 with PQCache-style top-k. This paper proposes Stratified Sparse Attention (SSA): keep high-scoring tokens deterministically, importance-sample the remaining tail by proxy scores, and correct the weights by inclusion probabilities. At the same 50× sparsity, Llama-3.1-8B rises to 83.67; on AIME 2025 with Qwen3.5-9B, SSA scores 59.8 against 36.7 for PQ. With optimized CUDA kernels, SSA's decode attention is 2.96× faster than FlashInfer at 1M context.

1. The problem: top-k drops the tail, uniform sampling wastes the budget

There are three existing ways to spend a sparse-attention budget k. Deterministic retrieval (Quest, PQCache, and Tactic for top-p) picks the highest-scoring tokens by proxy score. It works when attention is concentrated, but when attention is diffuse or long-tailed, all mass outside the set is lost, especially when proxy errors push genuinely important tokens just below the cutoff. Pure sampling (MagicPig) uses LSH-based sampling with importance correction; coverage is broader, but variance is high at small budgets. Hybrid methods (vAttention) keep the top-k and sample the rest uniformly. This beats both, yet a clear gap to dense attention remains at aggressive sparsity.

The authors' preliminary study pins down the flaw in uniform sampling: residual attention mass is very unevenly distributed, and uniform sampling spends as much budget on tokens that contribute almost nothing as on the rest. Figure 1 shows that score-guided importance sampling has consistently lower relative L2​ error than uniform sampling.

2. Method: stratify, sample, then correct

First, recall importance-sampled sparse attention. Let si​=q⊤ki​/d​ and wi​=exp(si​). If each token enters the set S with probability πi​, weight it by 1/πi​:
o=∑i∈S​wi​/πi​∑i∈S​(wi​/πi​)vi​​
This is inverse-probability weighting: a token that was unlikely to be chosen gets a larger weight when it is chosen, standing in for similar tokens that were not. SSA has three steps.

Step 1: allocate the budget from proxy scores. Apply softmax to the proxy scores s~i​ (from PQCache's product quantization in the experiments) to get qi​, and find λ such that ∑i​min{1,λqi​}=k. Tokens with λqi​≥1 form the heavy set H and are kept deterministically; the remaining budget m=k−∣H∣ goes to sampling the residual set R. The heavy-set size is not hand-set; the proxy distribution decides it, and a sharper distribution yields a larger heavy set.

Step 2: score-guided sampling of the residual. Renormalize qi​ over R into a proposal pi​, draw m times with replacement, and deduplicate to get S. A residual token's probability of being drawn at least once is πi​=1−(1−pi​)m; heavy tokens have πi​=1.

Step 3: exact scoring and correction. Compute exact scores ℓi​ only for tokens in H∪S, weight them by exp(ℓi​)/πi​, and take the normalized weighted sum. Because repeated draws count once, at most k tokens are read.

An illustrative walk-through (numbers are illustrative): a 1M-token context at 50× sparsity gives k=20,000. Suppose 8,000 tokens satisfy λqi​≥1; they enter the heavy set directly. The other 12,000 draws are made over about 990,000 residual tokens according to pi​. A token with small pi​ that is drawn carries a large 1/πi​, representing a broad tail of similarly low-scoring tokens whose combined attention mass is still significant. Pure top-k drops this tail entirely; uniform sampling wastes most draws on tokens with near-zero weight.

The framework contains earlier methods as special cases: a heavy set alone is top-k or top-p; an empty heavy set is pure sampling; a uniform proposal gives the vAttention-style hybrid.

Error bound. Let AR​ be the true attention mass in the residual set, and ρ=maxi∈R​aˉi​/pi​ measure how well the proposal covers the true residual mass (ρ≥1, with equality when the two match). Theorem 2 shows that with probability at least 1−δ, the output error ∥o−o∥2​ scales as AR​ρ/m​, with deduplication contributing only a constant factor. This states the allocation trade-off directly: keeping more heavy tokens lowers AR​, leaving more sampling budget raises m, and a proxy that underestimates an important residual token raises ρ and the error.

3. Core contribution

The main contribution is unifying retrieval and sampling in one framework, in which a single proxy score decides who is kept, who is sampled and how weights are corrected. It is orthogonal to the choice of approximate retrieval: any top-k method that yields proxy scores (PQCache, Quest and others) can serve as SSA's proposal, so improvements in retrieval carry straight over to SSA. The second contribution is engineering: sampling is often assumed too slow, and the authors' kernels show it can beat dense attention at long context.

4. Implementation: keeping sampling off the critical path

For efficiency, the real kernel uses a fixed heavy fraction h instead of adaptive allocation; the authors report that this fixed-budget variant closely matches the theoretical one. A decode step has four stages; the first three scan the whole context but read only a few bytes per token.

  • Proxy scoring: each key is a 16-byte PQ code (16 subspaces, 256 centroids), versus 1 KiB for its fp16 KV pair at head dimension 256. A lookup table computes proxy scores for all tokens, and the same kernel masks sink and local tokens.
  • Heavy-set selection: radix top-k returns an unordered index set, since the estimator does not depend on order.
  • Residual sampling: a hierarchical sampler picks a 1024-token chunk, then a 32-token sub-tile, then one token among at most 32. A per-row hash table removes duplicates. πi​ is clamped at 10−4 to avoid huge weights, at the cost of a small bias. A counter-based RNG keeps samples fresh across replays while remaining compatible with CUDA graph capture.
  • Sparse decode: the selected K and V are gathered from the paged KV cache and evaluated with online softmax, splitting each query head across thread blocks and merging with log-sum-exp. At 1M context and batch 1, 64 splits are 1.58–1.71× faster than 32; 128 or 256 splits are slower.

5. Experiments

Baselines are Oracle-top-k (exact scores for top-k selection; it cannot accelerate anything and serves as an upper reference for deterministic retrieval), PQCache and vAttention (PQ), all with 128 sink and 128 local tokens. Models are Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen3.5-4B/9B and Ministral-3-3B/8B, plus Qwen3.5-27B on LongBench.

On Ministral-3-8B, SSA recovers 94% and 85% of dense performance at 32K and 128K. Oracle-top-k is still stronger at 128K on Llama and Ministral-3-3B, and at every length on Ministral-3-8B, which suggests proxy-score quality remains the bottleneck. On Qwen3.5, every sparse method stays within two points of dense.

AIME 2025 (32K-token generations). SSA's lead grows with sparsity. On Qwen3.5-9B (dense 66.3) at 50×, SSA scores 59.8 against 48.3 for Oracle-top-k, 36.7 for PQ and 35.8 for vAttention (PQ); at 100×, SSA still has 51.5 while vAttention (PQ) falls to 3.5. Low sparsity is different: at 5×, vAttention (PQ) is better (65.4 vs 61.0). An ablation on the heavy fraction h shows that hybrid splits with h∈[0.2,0.8] reach 61.0–64.0, against 40.2–42.5 for pure sampling ($h=0$) and 49.4–53.8 for pure top-k ($h=1$).

LOFT and LongBench. On LOFT, SSA has the lowest attention error among non-oracle methods at every length and sparsity, but the task metric (subspan-EM) differs by at most 0.04 across methods. On LongBench with Qwen3.5-27B, all sparse methods are within one point of dense. Lower error does not always turn into a task-score difference.

Decode efficiency. Measured on one H200 (16 query heads, 4 KV heads, head dimension 256, fp16 KV). At batch 1 and 128K, SSA runs at only 0.60–0.74× FlashInfer's speed; as context grows the overhead amortizes, and at 1M it is 1.79×, 1.39× and 1.07× faster at 50×, 20× and 10× sparsity. Gains grow with batch size, reaching 2.96× at 1M, batch 16 and 50×. Kernel optimization matters most at batch 1: an unoptimized reference runs at 0.37–0.47× FlashInfer, and the optimizations improve it 2.95–3.80×. The two-level sampler is 1.21–3.16× faster than chunk-only sampling, keeping sampling at 0.30–1.18 ms at 1M context and $h=0.4$.

6. Limitations and what it means in practice

There are several limitations. The real kernel uses a fixed heavy fraction, and an efficient adaptive implementation is future work. Clamping πi​ introduces a small bias. Sampling makes outputs stochastic, so two decodes of the same request do not produce identical attention, and settings that need reproducibility should fix the seed. Below 128K at batch 1, SSA is slower than FlashInfer. Where sparsity barely hurts anyway, as with Qwen3.5 or LongBench, SSA shows no clear advantage. The authors' stated next step is to make SSA verifiable, with user-set guarantees on approximation quality.

For industry, the fit is clear: million-token contexts, decode serving at larger batch sizes, and sparsity pushed beyond 20–50×, such as long-horizon agent histories, very long document QA or long reasoning chains. If your system already runs a top-k retriever such as PQCache or Quest, adopting SSA only means moving part of the budget from top-k to proxy-guided sampling and adding the 1/πi​ correction; the retrieval index can be reused as is. Before deploying, sweep h on your own tasks: the AIME results show h anywhere in 0.2–0.8 works well, while pure top-k and pure sampling are clearly worse. If your sparsity is only 5–10×, existing top-k or vAttention may already be enough; SSA's gains come mainly in the aggressive regime.
