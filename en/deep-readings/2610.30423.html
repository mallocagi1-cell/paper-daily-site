When Agentic Retrieval Fails Silently: Framework and Production Evidence from a Creative AI Assistant

Yan, Liang, Anuj Agarwal, Aditya Reddy Cheruku
26 minread4.55score

On Adobe Express, one retrieval infrastructure serves both a template gallery search and an agentic assistant, for the same user population. Intuition says assistant users, who never inspect result lists, should reformulate less; in fact the chat entry point reformulates 44.4% of the time against 33.1% in the gallery, 11 points higher. The two "reformulations" are different behaviors: in the gallery it means "these results were not relevant, I will try another query"; in chat it mostly means "I see the generated output and want to change it" ("make it bluer", "try a bolder font"). More surprising still, the assistant actually fired retrieval on only 0.31% of prompts. The three Adobe authors call this the attribution gap: CTR, MRR and behavioral NDCG assume the user sees ranked results and acts on them, and in an agentic assistant that assumption fails in three directions at once. The paper proposes LARA, a layered assessment framework that restores retrieval attribution with three independent measurement layers plus four logging fields, and no new modeling.

1. The problem: the agent reads first, the user arrives later

The authors distil the failure into three structural shifts and stress that none is a tuning problem.

Who reads. In gallery search a user types "birthday invitation", scans 40 templates and clicks one, and that click grades retrieval. In the assistant, the user asks for a birthday invitation, the search subagent retrieves templates, and the agent picks one and builds the design; the user never sees the other 39. There may be no click at all, and whatever the user does see has already been filtered and reordered by the agent.

When the user acts. In one conversation the same asset can be retrieved in turn 1 and again in turn 3, then exported in turn 5 or in another session. Without an identifier linking the action to a specific retrieval event, timing cannot say which retrieval earned it.

What caused it. A bad outcome cannot be split into retrieval failure versus generation failure, and a good outcome can hide a retrieval failure the model quietly compensated for.

Without per-component instrumentation, teams rely on session-level proxies such as retry, abandonment and task completion. The authors note that retry alone fails both ways: it fires on generation failures unrelated to retrieval, and stays silent when the model covers for a retrieval failure. The third shift is the most dangerous, because nothing in the session-level signal distinguishes it from success.

2. Method: three measurement layers

LARA measures each stage at the earliest point where its failure becomes observable.

Layer 1: retrieval quality. Did the subagent return the right assets in the right order? Metrics are null rate, precision@1, precision@K and NDCG@K, computed from server-side logs against offline relevance labels (human or LLM-as-judge) at the moment retrieval fires, independent of what the user or agent does next. CTR becomes a secondary directional signal. Only this layer can detect a silent retrieval failure the model compensated for.

Layer 2: engagement and outcome. Did anyone use what retrieval returned, and did anything signal that retrieval was wrong? This layer keeps positive and failure evidence separate and reports both by "retrieval role", a tag written at retrieval time recording what retrieval was for: direct retrieval, retrieve-then-inspect, retrieve-then-act, reference input for generation, an embedded step in a larger task, or context-dependent. Its four metrics are:

  • Genuine Positive Rate (GPR): the share of retrievals followed by user-initiated use of a returned asset after the response is shown, such as a follow-up prompt that continues with it, or selecting, editing or exporting it.
  • Agent Consumption Rate (ACR): the share of retrievals where the agent uses a returned asset within the same request, before the user has a result (e.g., finding a logo and removing its background in one prompt). It is positive evidence too, but tracked separately, because the agent using an asset is the agent's judgment while a user's later action is independent confirmation.
  • Observed Retrieval Failure Rate (ORF): the share of retrievals followed by one of three retrieval-specific signals: Search Correction (the user says the retrieved object or route was wrong), Search Reformulation (retrying retrieval for the same unresolved need), or Agentic Rejection (the workflow was expected to use a returned asset but used something else). A Downstream Correction that keeps the asset and fixes an edit or generation is not a retrieval failure. 1−ORF is reported alongside GPR and ACR but never read as satisfaction.
  • Unobserved Outcome Rate (UOR): the share of retrievals with neither use evidence nor a failure signal, reported as its own category, not as abandonment or success.

Layer 3: product success. Did the user achieve the goal? In creative tools the clearest signals are export, share and download. Task completion is defined per retrieval role, and goal fulfillment needs LLM-as-judge or human annotation. This layer becomes a retrieval signal, not just a product-health signal, only when the success event links back through the lineage fields to the retrieval that supplied the asset.

3. Cross-layer diagnosis and lineage fields

Reading Layer 1 against Layer 2 locates the failing stage:

Layer 1Layer 2Diagnosis
LowORF highRetrieval returned the wrong assets
HighORF low, many Downstream CorrectionsAssets fine, generation failed
HighORF high (Agentic Rejection)Agent bypassed good results (parametric blending)
LowORF near zeroSilent retrieval failure masked by the model

The last row is the most dangerous: invisible without Layer 1, and detectable with it at fire time, before complaints accumulate.

Four lineage fields. In current production logs, retrieval events and action events share no field. The authors close the gap with four: a unique retrieval_id on every retrieval event; returned_asset_ids, the full ranked list at fire time, before the generation layer filters or reorders it, which alone enables Layer 1 and is the highest-value single change; consumed_asset_id on every action event, whether a user action or an agent tool call; and source_retrieval_id on the same event, naming the retrieval that supplied the asset, inherited from the placement event for assets already in the document. All four are logging additions, not predictive models. Because source_retrieval_id names a retrieval rather than a session, it resolves cross-turn recurrence and keeps exports in a new session attributable; joined, the fields recover the retrieval rank of the asset actually used, independent of display order, letting RAG frameworks such as RAGAs and ARES use direct attribution instead of timing heuristics.

Illustration: a user in chat says "find a birthday invitation template and change the title to Xiaoming's 6th birthday". The retrieval event logs retrieval_id=r1 and 20 candidates; the agent picks rank 4 and edits the title, an agent event carrying consumed_asset_id and source_retrieval_id=r1, which counts toward ACR; two days later the user exports in the editor, and the export inherits r1, counting as a retrieval-attributed export in Layer 3. Without these fields, the export lands in another session and the chat session is scored as abandoned.

4. Production evidence from Adobe Express

The authors analysed production sessions from August 2026. LARA's lineage fields are not yet implemented, so every engagement figure below is timing-based; session, user and query counts are withheld under Adobe's disclosure policy, and the gallery runs at about 8–9× the assistant's volume.

The agent reads first. Assistant-wide CTR is near zero because 99.7% of interactions never invoke retrieval, so no denominator exists. Retrieval fired on only 0.31% of in-scope prompts; where it fires it works, with a 1.51% null rate and about 1.05 clicks per returned grid. The direct test needs ACR, which cannot yet be computed.

Action is delayed. Assistant sessions average 2.04 prompts (chat: 2.65), so the same asset reappears in later turns. Exports started from chat land in a different session_guid once the user leaves the chat surface; session-level attribution counts these users as abandoning, inflating chat abandonment (59.5%) and deflating engagement.

Retrieval and generation blend. This is the reformulation reversal from the opening: one behavioral signal mixes retrieval failure with generation refinement, and the aggregate means nothing on its own. LARA splits it into Search Reformulation, counted only on retrieval turns, and Downstream Correction, counted everywhere else.

What can be measured today. Null rate and clicks per grid come from existing logs; precision@K and NDCG@K need returned_asset_ids plus a labeled sample; GPR, ACR, ORF and UOR need the other three fields and the retrieval role, and today require manual trace annotation. The team can confirm retrieval works when it fires, but not yet whether the user or agent used what it returned.

5. Unexpected finding: retrieval nearly vanishes

Gallery search is retrieval by definition, while the assistant invoked retrieval on 0.31% of prompts, and about 82% of assistant activity is generative editing with no gallery-search analog. The authors suspect the bigger risk is missed retrieval: the planner routes "find me a birthday invitation template" to generative editing, no retrieval event fires, and Layer 1 has nothing to score. Table 3's 10.7% chat retrieval-intent figure is directional; if it reflects intent rather than fired retrieval, its gap to 0.31% is a first estimate of missed retrieval. They name this Layer 0 (planner routing), an extension of LARA rather than a fourth layer, with two measures: mis-routing rate (retrieval calls whose prompt is out of retrieval scope, to be excluded from Layer 1) and missed-retrieval rate (prompts with retrieval intent that never triggered retrieval, which needs ground-truth intent labels). In a 273-prompt sample, roughly 5–7% contained two or more distinct retrieval intents.

6. Limitations and industry relevance

The authors' limitations are concrete: all evidence comes from one deployment, and the 0.31% rate and the reformulation reversal may be specific to creative assistants dominated by generative editing; none of the lineage fields is implemented, so all Layer 2 figures are timing-based inferences and retrieval role is inferred post hoc rather than tagged at retrieval time; chat abandonment is overstated by cross-session exports; the data has not passed schema or value-range validation, synthetic traffic was removed manually, and latency and tokens per successful task are not instrumented per stage. LARA starts at the retrieval component; how the agent interprets intent, generates queries or decomposes multi-intent requests needs separate instrumentation. It is a short industry paper, and the framework has not been validated across deployments or by online experiments.

The authors' three lessons for practitioners are practical. First, do not report assistant-wide CTR or reformulation as retrieval metrics; restrict retrieval metrics to turns where retrieval fired and split reformulation into Search Reformulation and Downstream Correction. Second, log the full ranked list at fire time before anything else; it is the only field that unblocks Layer 1. Third, define user-initiated use once and publish the definition: Express chat's meaningful-action rate moved between 7.6% and 40.5% depending on which actions qualified. For teams embedding search into agents, the main value is the reminder that when retrieval fires on 0.31% of prompts, the question is not only whether it ranked well but whether it was called at all.
