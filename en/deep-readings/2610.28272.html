How Language Models Learn Taste: Brand Preference in LLMs

Tapan Parikh
22 minread4.00score

Ask a language model to "name a running shoe brand" and it says Nike; ask it to "recommend one" and it says Brooks. The author tested 102 models across 42 brand categories. Under a two-turn "recommend, then pick one" question, the panel's brand differs from the "name one" brand in 26 categories, and models from different labs move together to the same brand: Delta becomes Singapore Airlines, Chrome becomes Firefox, Google becomes DuckDuckGo. The Named brands are mostly market leaders; the Picked ones are mostly niche or specialist. Using three open pipelines that release a checkpoint after every training stage, the paper traces where this "taste" forms: pretraining already ties the recommended brand to advice contexts, SFT mostly leaves the pick unchanged, and DPO moves the pick by 11 to 24 points in all three pipelines, even though DPO's preference data contains almost no brands. The shift is not limited to brands: when OLMo 3 7B is asked to "choose a sauce", the share choosing pesto rises from 5% at base to 67% after DPO.

1. The problem: where do a model's recommendations come from?

More and more people simply ask an AI assistant to "recommend a such-and-such". Existing audits examine which brands production assistants surface and how stable those answers are across rewordings, but few ask at which training stage those preferences form. This paper starts from a simple contrast: "name one" asks what comes to mind first, while "recommend one" asks for a judgment. When the answers differ, the model is using a different set of associations to judge.

2. Experimental design

Questions. There are 42 categories, from soda to cloud providers. Each is asked with three verbs, all requiring a single name: Name ("Name a running shoe brand"), Choose ("Choose one") and Recommend ("Can you recommend one?"). A two-turn question first asks "Can you recommend a running shoe brand?" and then "Which one would you pick?". The four answers are called the Named, Chosen, Recommended and Picked brands.

Panel. 102 models are queried through OpenRouter at temperature 1; one-name questions run 8 times per model and category, the two-turn question 4 times, and 25 hybrid reasoning models are also run with reasoning off. Data was collected from 2026-09-30 to 2026-10-10. Perplexity Sonar, which answers from live search, is excluded.

Scoring. A one-name reply must be a single name, with no list or lead-in. A two-turn reply is reduced to the brand it commits to; a list, a question back or a refusal counts as no pick, which happens in 17% of two-turn replies. On three hand-checked samples, the rules agreed with a human reading on 87% to 97% of replies.

3. Finding one: the panel names one brand and picks another

Each step toward judgment changes the panel's brand in more categories: 6/42 for Choose, 16/42 for Recommend and 26/42 for the two-turn pick. Meanwhile, a model's own Named brand falls from 89% of its answers to 31% of its picks. Each question has its own consensus: under Name the most common brand takes 76% of answers, and between 60% and 69% for the others.

The Named brands are market leaders such as Google, Walmart, McDonald's, Facebook and Verizon. The Picked brands are mostly smaller ones with a specialist or insider following, such as DuckDuckGo, Aldi, Sierra Nevada, Reddit, Signal and Brooks, plus a few premium brands, such as Hermès over Chanel and Hyatt over Marriott.

Models do not reject their default; they demote it. In the two-turn pick, a model's own Named brand appears in its recommendation list 83% of the time but is picked only 31% of the time; when listed, it comes first in 60% of lists and is picked in 37%. With a median list of 4 brands, a random draw would pick it 30% of the time. In each of the 12 labs with at least three models, the two-turn pick changes the lab's consensus brand in 55% to 74% of categories, so every major lab shares the pattern.

Two side observations. Labs favour their own AI assistant: asked to recommend one, Anthropic's models answer Claude 92% of the time, other labs' models 15%. But labs do not favour their other products; Google's models recommend Google search and Chrome less often than other labs' models do. And in a spot check, Claude Opus 5.5 answered the two-turn question for all 42 categories with web search available, searched in none, and left the Named brand in 25 of the 26 categories where the panel does.

4. Finding two: taste forms when a model learns which answer is better

OLMo 3 7B, OLMo 3.1 32B and Tulu 3 8B release checkpoints for every stage: base, SFT, DPO and RL. Nemotron 3.5 Lightning releases only a base and a final model. Scoring uses log-probabilities, so every stage and category can be measured without sampling.

Pretraining supplies the candidates. A base model cannot be asked for advice, but it can complete an advice sentence. Given "If you want a good web browser, I recommend", Tulu's base model continues with Firefox in 13 of 20 samples; given "A well-known web browser is", it continues with Chrome in 10. Over the 16 categories where the Recommended brand differs from the Named one, advice phrasing raises the Recommended brand's share in 7 to 10 categories, depending on the base model, and lowers it in at most one.

SFT leaves the pick; DPO moves it. To compare the same choice across stages, every stage gets the same three-item list (Named brand, Picked brand and a third recommended brand) with "Which one would you pick?", the reply starts with "I'd pick", and the probability of each brand is read off. Each list is shown in both orders because base models favour the first-listed brand.

The figures are the probability of picking the Picked brand over the Named brand across the 26 categories. After SFT all sit near base; the DPO step raises all three significantly, and RL keeps what DPO set. Nemotron rises from 58% at base to 79% in its final model.

The move does not depend on which brands the panel picks. Across all 42 categories, a brand's base-model log-probability under Name correlates negatively with how much DPO raises it under Recommend (-0.16, -0.14 and -0.23 across the three pipelines, each interval below zero): DPO lifts the brands the base model found less obvious. Yet DPO does not lower obvious answers everywhere: in the 16 categories where the panel keeps the Named brand, its share does not fall at DPO in any pipeline. Repeating the test on the SFT stage's own recommendation lists, the DPO step remains at +14, +9 and +14 points.

5. Finding three: the preference data holds almost no brands

OLMo 3's and Tulu 3's training data are public. The author searched for requests that pair one of the 26 categories with an advice word such as "recommend", "best" or "which". In 260,000 OLMo 3 DPO pairs, the chosen answer names the Picked brand 3 times and the rejected answer once; Tulu 3 is just as sparse. DPO did not learn these picks from brand examples, though indirect associations elsewhere in the data cannot be ruled out.

The same move appears outside brands. Using Name and Choose questions on everyday categories (a tree, a smell, an animal and so on), the author measures the same log-probability shift on the 23 categories where the panel's answers differ. Base models barely separate the two verbs, and DPO pushes Choose toward the panel's answer in all three pipelines, with SFT→DPO increments of +0.39, +0.11 and +0.17 nats. When OLMo 3 7B chooses a sauce, pesto goes from 5% at base to 27% after SFT and 67% after DPO, while ketchup falls from 72% to 6%.

Who defines "better"? Half of OLMo 3's DPO pairs are judged by an LLM; the other half pair a Qwen 3 32B response as chosen with one from a much smaller model as rejected. Tulu 3's pairs are rated by GPT-4o. The author therefore suspects the taste comes from the judge models used in preference training. Tulu 3 has a single judge, which allows a test: in categories where GPT-4o recommends a different brand from Tulu's base-model favourite, DPO moves the pick toward GPT-4o's brand by 8 points (90% interval 2 to 14). Note that the GPT-4o queried is the current release, not the 2024-08-06 snapshot that labelled Tulu's data, so this is an approximation.

6. Limitations and what it means in practice

The paper lists its limitations: the questions are short and carry no personal context, and measures on short prompts may not predict real use; the three staged pipelines come from one lab and share much of their recipe, so the formation path is shown only on them, though the 102-model panel shows the phenomenon itself extends beyond them; Nemotron has no intermediate stages; the 32B model and Nemotron ran at 8-bit precision; for OLMo 3 7B, sampled picks place the change at RL rather than DPO; and the panel is a snapshot of late September to early October 2026. In addition, "judge models transmit taste" is the author's conjecture, and the 8-point Tulu result is partial evidence. The AI-usage note states that Claude (Opus 5.5) was used to run probes, build the analysis and draft the text, while several Claude models are also study subjects.

There are direct implications for two groups. For post-training teams: preference optimization changes a model's judgments in domains its preference data never covers, likely via the judge models. If your product involves recommendation or choice (shopping assistants, local services, travel planning), run a "name vs. recommend" probe set at every training stage, monitor the shift around DPO, and examine the judge model's own preferences. For brands and marketers: the author concludes that whether a brand gets recommended depends less on what a model knows about it than on whether it fits the model's implicit taste. In marketing terms this is a matter of brand associations and positioning, but how a brand can build such associations for itself is left to future work.
