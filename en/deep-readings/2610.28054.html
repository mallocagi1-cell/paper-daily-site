AutoSim: A Self-Improving User Simulator for Conversational Search Evaluation

Saber Zerhoudi, Jelena Mitrovic, Michael Granitzer
26 minread4.30score

Ask a strong LLM to play a search user and it writes 37.5 words per message where real users write 12.8; a held-out discriminator catches its utterances at AUC 0.88, and its queries retrieve almost none of the passages a real user retrieves (overlap@5 of 0.01). This SIGIR-AP 2026 paper studies AutoSim, a conversational-search user simulator whose relevance judgments rank retrieval systems in agreement with NIST across three TREC iKAT years (Kendall's τ of 0.66, 0.91, 0.91), yet whose user behavior is far from human. The authors let the simulator self-improve under a held-out signal from real users, and the conclusion is clear: editing the prompt cannot push discriminator AUC below 0.85; editing the weights drives it to 0.45, where even a person reading the full dialogue cannot tell. More importantly, that realism changes the evaluation: where real users retrieve poorly, a prompted simulator over-estimates nDCG by 0.136, enough to change a deployment decision.

1. The problem: can simulators be trusted, and what should be edited to make them human

User simulation lets conversational-search systems be compared reproducibly, when tuning a ranker or a clarifying-question policy, without a human study for every design change. But a simulator is fixed at design time as personas, prompts and rules, and does not change as the systems it evaluates do. Prior work also finds it an unreliable proxy: changing only the underlying user model swings the evaluated agent's task success by about nine percentage points, and general-purpose LLM simulators systematically over-estimate success. Nor is a more realistic simulator necessarily a more accurate evaluator.

The authors split the question into three: does AutoSim rank IR systems as NIST does (RQ1); can self-improvement close the behavioral gap to real users, and should it edit the prompt or the weights (RQ2); and does closing the gap make the simulator a better evaluator (RQ3)? The diagnosis in Section 3 sets the direction: the default simulator's relevance judging already agrees with NIST, the gap is in "what the user says and asks for", and editing the generation prompt does not close it. So the design leaves the relevance judge alone, targets the utterance generator, and edits it at the weights.

2. Method: AutoSim and the self-improvement loop

Simulator state. AutoSim keeps an ordinary LLM generator and wraps it in a state St​={P,It​,Ht​,Cs,t​}: persona P (background statements in a personal text knowledge base, PTKB), information need It​, dialogue history Ht​, and a latent cognitive-affective state Cs,t​ such as satisfaction, frustration and patience. An orchestration policy picks which component acts each turn: deterministic simulators for routine behavior such as query reformulation, clicks and stopping, or LLM functions for answering a clarifying question, giving feedback, expressing satisfaction and generating the next utterance.

Two editable components. The first is the relevance-judging instruction, which sets the simulator's satisfaction and thus the ranking it induces; the second is the utterance generator, the model that writes what the simulated user says. The generator can be edited at the prompt or at the parameters, the central variable of the paper.

Gap metric. The authors combine system-ranking agreement and behavioral-distribution match into CSG. Communication style (D1) is measured two ways: summary statistics such as mean words, politeness rate and short-message rate, which are easy to read off and easy to write into a prompt; and a held-out real-versus-simulated discriminator reported as ROC AUC, where 0.5 means indistinguishable. The latter is an adversarial realism criterion that cannot be met by matching a few numbers, and the difference between the two measures is itself a result.

The loop. Each cycle records structured traces (each turn's policy choice, retrieval, click and utterance), clusters the differences between simulated and real behavior, builds per-cluster evaluators with a judge from a different model family than the generator, proposes edits by reflective population search, accepts an edit only if it does not regress on a calibration split, and monitors a rolling held-out set. The weight-level edit warm-starts Qwen2.5-3B with a LoRA adapter (r=16, α=32, on q/v projections) on real iKAT utterances, then runs 4 rounds of adversarial rejection sampling: sample several utterances per held-out turn, keep those a frozen discriminator rates most human, fine-tune on them, refresh the discriminator.

Testbed. iKAT 2023 (176 judged turns), 2024 (116) and the multi-session 2025 (45), all over ClueWeb22-B with NIST passage qrels. Each year has 12 constructed retrieval systems: BM25, SPLADE and RRF first stages over raw, context-resolved and PTKB-expanded queries, with or without a cross-encoder reranker; 2023 adds two real organizer runs for 14. The reference ranking uses condensed nDCG@5 on the NIST qrels, and AutoSim's ranking uses its persona-conditioned mean satisfaction with each system's top-5 passages. The validity pipeline uses two open-weight instruction models from different families (an 80B-class Qwen and a 31B-class Gemma) at temperature 0, with no proprietary API.

3. RQ1: it is a valid evaluator

On the 12 iKAT 2023 systems τ=0.66 (p=0.002, system-level bootstrap 95% CI [0.30, 0.90]); leave-one-out τ ranges 0.59–0.73, and five re-scorings give 0.72±0.01. With the two real organizer runs added τ=0.65, and AutoSim ranks the weak automatic-rewrite run last, as NIST does; its largest disagreement is placing the strong manual-rewrite run 10th of 14 where NIST places it 3rd.

iKAT 2024 and 2025 both reach τ=0.91 (p<0.001). On TREC CAsT 2020 (MS MARCO corpus, no personas, 10 systems) τ=0.56, and 0.58 over 15 systems with a neural reranking tier added. Two key controls: a persona-free relevance judge gets τ=0.79, on par with the persona-conditioned 0.72; and a different-family assessor (Gemma against Qwen) reproduces the 2024 ranking unchanged at τ=0.91. Ranking validity thus comes from the relevance-assessment ability strong LLMs already have, not from the persona. The weak spot is close systems: on the 6 closest-quality systems τ falls to 0.33 in 2023 (not significant) and is 0.87 in 2024.

4. RQ2: the prompt has a ceiling, the weights do not

At the prompt. Across 12 seeds, the full loop with the calibration check raises held-out style fidelity (CSG-D1) to 0.76±0.10 against 0.56±0.07 for the static baseline (paired t-test p=0.0002); the "blind" variant without calibration reaches only 0.63±0.07, not significantly above static (p=0.08). Mean words drop from 37.5 to 11.0, but politeness overshoots to 39% (humans 12%), and the 5% of very short human messages are missed. A sharper comparison: a one-line hand-written prompt, "write a short message, about 12 words, and skip pleasantries", scores 0.80 on the summary statistics, above the loop's 0.68; few-shot prompting with real terse utterances matches length (12.3 words) yet the discriminator still scores AUC 0.94.

At the weights. Discriminator AUC:

Self-improvementDiscriminator AUC
None (default prompt)0.88
Prompt-level (best of four methods)0.84
Weight-level, warm SFT on real data0.45
Weight-level, + adversarial rounds0.42–0.50

The authors check two objections. Is the discriminator too weak? A fine-tuned XLM-RoBERTa separates the default almost perfectly (0.94) yet cannot separate the weight-level simulator (0.51±0.06), while detecting a lightly politeness-perturbed real utterance at 0.99, so it is strong. Is it one model only? On Qwen2.5-7B the extremes are 1.00 vs 0.50, on Mistral-7B 0.97 vs 0.40. Two frontier LLMs asked to pick the human beat chance on the default (0.59, 0.89) but not on the weight-level one (0.50, 0.48); three human annotators reading full dialogues catch the default on 28–30 of 30 trials but the weight-level one on only 4–11 of 30, all below the 15/30 chance level. Query-need alignment matches too: humans 0.56, weight-level 0.51, default 0.23, and the tersest prompt (9.6 words) is worst at 0.19.

The relevance judge is a third case: editing the assessor prompt under the check yields no accepted edit, and unchecked edits move τ by up to about 0.1, within sampling noise. Self-improvement has room to change how the user behaves, not how it judges.

5. RQ3: realism changes the evaluation

System-ranking τ does not move; the ranking was already valid. The effect appears on a quantity the training never optimized: the absolute retrieval effectiveness implied by the simulator's own queries. On 157 held-out judged turns, BM25 is run on each simulator's queries and nDCG@5 compared with the real user's:

Query sourceWordsiKAT 2024 bias2023 held-out bias
Default (verbose)28+0.136+0.009

Weight-level12+0.014+0.015

On the harder, fully held-out 2024 collection, real users' mean nDCG is only 0.016; the default over-estimates by 0.136, and the terse prompt and length truncation still over-estimate by 0.087 and 0.071, while only the weight-level simulator's interval includes zero. Length explains just 2% of the variance in retrieved nDCG. On the easier 2023 held-out set (real mean 0.080), no simulator is detectably biased: the over-estimate appears exactly where real users struggle and evaluation most needs to be trusted.

This is not a harmless shift that calibration can remove. Real users retrieve nothing relevant on 86% of these turns; on exactly those turns the default simulator still returns a relevant passage 57% of the time, the weight-level one only 14%, failing where real users fail. In decision terms: on 2024 an evaluation with the default simulator reads 0.15 nDCG, well above a modest 0.05 deployment threshold, where real users get 0.02 and the weight-level simulator reports the same failure (0.03).

6. Limitations and industry relevance

The authors' limitations: the pool is constructed plus two organizer runs, not the full participant field; the behavioral reference is organizer-authored iKAT utterances, a proxy for logged end users; the human evidence rests on three annotators; validity holds for systems that differ clearly in quality, near-tie resolution on 2023 is only marginal, 2025 rests on 45 judged turns, and the borderline tests (p=0.02–0.03) should be read as descriptive; behavior covers four axes across D1–D3, leaving clicks, satisfaction and stopping for later; and the cross-corpus step is limited by about half corpus coverage. Also note that the weight-level "self-improvement" relies on SFT warm-starting from real utterances, so it is not fully self-bootstrapped.

For teams evaluating search or conversational products offline, there are two directly usable reminders. First, do not judge a simulator by summary statistics like word count and politeness, nor by system-ranking correlation alone: a simulator with high τ can still over-estimate absolute effectiveness by a wide margin, so add a learned discriminator and an effectiveness-bias test. Second, with a batch of real user utterances in hand, weight-level fine-tuning of a small LoRA model makes simulated users far more human than polishing prompts; the relevance judge can simply be a strong model without much tuning. The framework, protocol, LoRA adapters and human-discrimination kit are released.
