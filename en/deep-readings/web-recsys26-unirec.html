UniRec: A Unified Expressive-Aligned Generative Recommendation Framework for E-commerce

RecSys 2026
Ziliang Wang, Gaoyun Lin, Xuesi Wang, Shaoqiang Liang, Yili Huang, Weijie Bian, Li Zhang, Mingchen Cai, Jian Dong, Guanxing Zhang
34 minread4.65score
Original paper ↗

Generative recommendation encodes each product as a few layers of discrete semantic IDs (SIDs), and the model generates these IDs layer by layer to perform retrieval and ranking. This Shopee paper points out one gap between it and discriminative ranking models: when a discriminative model scores a candidate it sees the product's category, shop and brand directly and crosses them with user features, while a generative model has seen no product-side information at all before it generates the first SID layer. UniRec's core technique is Chain-of-Attribute (CoA): the model first generates the product's category and then generates the SID conditioned on that category, which puts back into the decoding path the product features a discriminative model can use. Alongside it come an SID construction that caps codebook load by exposure, CDC that injects scenario and combination features into decoding, and business alignment via RFT plus DPO. Offline HR@50 is 22.6% higher (relative) than the strongest baseline OneRec-V2; launched on Shopee's main feed and landing pages, orders rose 4.76% and GMV 5.60%, with 110ms end-to-end latency versus 266ms for the original multi-stage pipeline.

The paper's arXiv title is "UniRec: Bridging the Expressive Gap between Generative and Discriminative Recommendation via Chain-of-Attribute"; this write-up is based on the arXiv full text.

1. Where generative recommendation falls short

A discriminative ranker estimates p(y∣f,u), the probability that user u engages (y) given product features f, with all product features visible at scoring time. A generative model generates the SID layer by layer as ∏l​p(sl​∣s<l​,u), and when generating the first layer it sees only the user, no product features.

The authors use Bayes' rule to show where the gap comes from:
p(y∣f,u)∝p(f∣y,u)p(y∣u),p(f∣y,u)=k=1∏n​p(fk​∣f<k​,y,u)
p(y∣u) does not vary across products, so ranking by the discriminative score is equivalent to ranking by p(f∣y,u), and the latter, expanded by the chain rule, is exactly generating product features one by one. The conclusion: if a generative model generated all of a product's features one by one, its ranking ability would equal the discriminative model's. Real systems cannot generate all features one by one because latency would be too high, so they compress product semantics into a few SID layers. Structured attributes such as category, shop and brand, which discriminative models cross directly, are folded into the SID during compression and no longer appear explicitly at decoding time, and that is where the gap comes from.

2. Method

2.1 Chain-of-Attribute: generate the category first, then the SID

CoA prepends m attribute tokens a to the SID sequence, so generation goes attributes first, SID second:
p(s∣u)=p(a∣u)⋅l=0∏L−1​p(sl​∣a,s<l​,u)
In the production configuration the attributes are the product's second- and third-level categories (categories have three levels L1, L2, L3, getting finer going down), so a product's decoding path is "L2 category → L3 category → s0​ → s1​ → s2​", two steps longer than before.

Why generating the category first helps: products in the same category sit close together in SID space, so once the category is known, the candidate range for the next SID layer shrinks. The authors write this as the reduction in conditional entropy ΔHl​=H(sl​∣s<l​,u)−H(sl​∣a,s<l​,u), which equals the conditional mutual information between the attributes and that SID layer and is greater than 0 whenever the two are correlated. With a lower error probability at each layer, the probability of getting the whole path right goes up, and beam search becomes more stable.

2.2 Capacity-constrained SID: balance the codebook by exposure, not product count

Existing balanced quantization (such as the RQ-KMeans used in OneRec) gives each codeword a similar number of products, but product popularity is long-tailed, and an even product count does not mean even exposure. The authors measured exposure concentration on Shopee production data. At the first layer alone, the 10% of codewords with the most exposure take a third of it, which is still mild; at combinations of the first two layers, the top 10% already take nearly 90% of exposure (87.90%). This means the deeper the layer, the more uneven the codeword allocation, with a few popular combinations taking almost all the traffic.

High-exposure token paths appear often in the training data, so the model keeps generating those few paths during beam search; the authors call this a Matthew effect inherited layer by layer. Capacity-constrained SID adds one constraint to the residual clustering at each layer: the sum of exposure weights Vk​ of the products in each cluster may not exceed τ times the average load.
{zi​},{μk​}min​i=1∑N​∥xi​−μzi​​∥22​s.t.Vk​=i:zi​=k∑​wi​≤τCcap​
xi​ is product i's residual vector at the current layer, zi​ the cluster it is assigned to, μk​ a cluster center, wi​ its historical impression count, Ccap​ total exposure divided by the number of clusters, and τ=1.05 in production. Solving this exactly is NP-hard, so a two-step greedy procedure is used: assign each product to its nearest cluster, then move products from overloaded clusters to the nearest cluster that is not full, repeating until reconstruction error stops changing.

2.3 CDC: tell the model which task it is on, then add combination features

One generative model has to serve objectives such as click, purchase, add-to-cart and cross-border, and scenarios such as main feed, search, similar items and flash sale. Task-Conditioned BOS replaces the fixed start token with a learnable vector looked up by "objective × scenario", steering decoding toward that scenario's distribution from the first step.

Content Summary deals with the combined meaning of tokens: the same s1​ following different categories may stand for completely different products. Giving every token pair its own embedding would mean about 16 million combinations for two layers with 4,000-token vocabularies. CDC instead uses 3 hash functions ($x+y$, x⋅y, p1​x+p2​y, with p1​, p2​ primes) on the tokens already generated. A fixed set of pairs is chosen in advance, such as (L2 category, s0​), (L3 category, s1​) and (s0​, s1​). At step t, for each available pair, the two token indices x and y go through the 3 functions to get 3 indices. Each index, modulo the table size, looks up a vector in one shared embedding table, and the 3 vectors concatenated form that step's combination feature ct​. The paper's formula hashes all earlier tokens and does not say how results from several pairs are merged. Different combinations may collide on one index, but rarely on all three at once, much like a Bloom filter.

2.4 Decoder and business alignment

The decoding-side sequence [BOS, attribute tokens, SID tokens] is Q. K and V come from the item vectors of the user's latest 200 clicks plus 100 multimodal vectors. Each multimodal vector corresponds to one item in the history and is made by concatenating the embeddings of its three SID levels and passing them through a linear layer. The paper only says these vectors are aligned in time with the behavior sequence, not which 100 items they are. These pass through 3 layers of cross-attention with a learnable gating coefficient and an MMoE-FFN. Each step has its own Rank Head, which concatenates the attention output, the embedding of the prefix generated so far, ct​ and the aggregated user representation, passes it through SENet (which learns an importance weight for each input segment) and MaskNet (which uses the input itself to build an element-wise mask on hidden layers), and outputs that step's token distribution.

Next-token prediction only fits the impression distribution and does not optimize GMV directly. RFT computes a business reward for each sample by combining several predicted metrics for it (such as conversion probability) according to business priorities; the paper gives no combining formula and describes it as weighting by business value (GMV). It then subtracts the batch mean, divides by the root mean square and clips to get A~i​, and multiplies the sample's loss weight by (1+λA~i​). DPO builds preference pairs from items impressed under the same request, with purchase > click > impression only, and when computing the pair log-probabilities, the terms for earlier SID layers are computed but get no gradient; only the last layer back-propagates. All layers share one decoder, so this only reduces the disturbance to prefix predictions and cannot keep them fully unchanged. The two are trained together:
L=LRFT​+λDPO​LDPO​
In production the RFT to DPO loss weight ratio is 20:3, and DPO's β=0.1.

3. Experiments

3.1 Main results

This experiment is meant to show that UniRec's retrieval quality is higher than existing generative and discriminative methods. The data is 9 consecutive days of Shopee main-feed production logs, billions of interactions. HR@K generates the top K candidates with beam search and checks whether the target product is among them; "order samples" counts only samples that ended in a purchase. Baselines: SASRec, a self-attention sequential recommender that does nearest-neighbor retrieval over the SID codebook with its learned representation; TIGER, generative retrieval with an encoder-decoder architecture; OneRec-V2, decoder-only generative retrieval with cross-attention. The finer the category, the higher the first-layer hit rate; generating L2 → L3 in sequence works best.

Removing any one of the three components drops HR@50 by 4 to 6 points. After switching back to RQ-KMeans, the authors also compared the exposure concentration of the two SIDs: on three-layer combinations, the top 1% of tokens take 57.33% of exposure under RQ-KMeans and 26.04% with the capacity constraint.

Task-Conditioned BOS also got a multi-scenario experiment on samples from three production scenarios. HR@100 is 0.470 when each scenario trains and evaluates its own model on its own samples, 0.486 for one model trained on all three without being told the scenario, and 0.510 when that joint model gets a scenario-specific BOS (the paper reports one aggregate number per setting, not per scenario). Mixed training helps by itself, and adding the scenario condition improves all three cutoffs a bit more.

Increasing model width dmodel​ from 64 to 512 raises HR@50 from 0.397 to 0.557, without saturating.

4. Limitations

The paper has no separate limitations discussion. Two points stand out from the experimental setup. First, all offline comparisons are on Shopee's internal data, with baselines also limited to 0.05B parameters, and there are no results on public datasets. Second, the online control is the entire discriminative multi-stage system, so the A/B gain includes the combined effect of the generative architecture, CoA, capacity-constrained SID, CDC and alignment; the paper gives no per-component online contribution.

5. Practical implications

  • If the first-layer SID hit rate of your generative retrieval is low, try CoA first: put one or two category levels in front of the SID, at the cost of only two extra decoding steps; in Table 2 the first layer's Top-3 hit rate rose from 0.314 to 0.682.
  • When building SIDs, check exposure concentration, not just whether product counts are even. A codebook balanced by product count can still let a few popular combinations take nearly 90% of exposure, and beam search will keep walking those few paths; adding an exposure-based capacity constraint only changes the clustering assignment step, so the cost is low.
