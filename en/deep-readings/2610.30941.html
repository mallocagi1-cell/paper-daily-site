PAIR: Preference-Aligned Intent Retrieval for Premium People Recommendation at LinkedIn

Zhongkai Shangguan, Yanbin Kang, Yihan Cao, Ruijie Jiang, Qiannan Yin, Chang Yue, Zijian Zhou, Girish Kathalagiri, Changshuai Wei, Zhengming Xing
24 minread4.00score

When a software engineer starts job hunting on LinkedIn, the clicks shift from fellow engineers to recruiters, hiring managers and HR. In professional networking, relevance is often complementary rather than symmetric, so general-purpose embeddings trained on profile similarity misrank candidates. In LinkedIn's offline evaluation, the production member embedding Holistic-171 reaches only 0.288 MAR@100 on Premium user retrieval, and an off-the-shelf 0.6B general LLM embedding does worse at 0.192. A LinkedIn team proposes PAIR (Preference-Aligned Intent Retrieval): build preference supervision from production exposure logs, who was clicked and who was not, and add a click-history tower that can be updated cheaply. The 0.6B model reaches 0.450 MAR@100, a 56% relative gain over Holistic-171 and 2.3× the off-the-shelf base. In an online A/B test on LinkedIn Premium Browsemaps, click-through rate rises 7.5% and Premium retention 2.1%, with no measurable latency regression.

1. The problem: professional recommendation is not about finding similar people

Premium Browsemaps is LinkedIn's people-recommendation module for Premium subscribers, shown on member profile pages. The profile visits it generates feed notifications such as "Who viewed your profile", which shape users' sense of Premium's value and their renewals. The system has two stages: attribute filters (location, industry, seniority, language) narrow the Premium member pool, embedding-based retrieval (EBR) pulls about 100 candidates, and a ranking model picks the 4 shown to the user.

Retrieval defines every candidate the ranker can see; intent-matching candidates missed at retrieval cannot be recovered by ranking. Yet general embeddings cluster people with similar backgrounds: job seekers actually want recruiters or hiring managers, business developers want prospective clients or partners, and recruiters want candidates with complementary skills. The authors frame the problem as preference ranking: for a query user q, a clicked candidate d+ should score above an unclicked d−.

Deploying at LinkedIn scale adds two engineering constraints. First, intent is dynamic: someone actively job-hunting this week may shift to casual networking next month, and re-encoding hundreds of millions of profiles with an LLM whenever history changes is infeasible in production. Second, recommendation surfaces have different latency, memory and storage budgets, so a single model must provide embeddings at several dimensions.

2. Method I: exposure-conditioned preference learning

Training data comes from historical Premium Browsemaps production logs. In each recommendation impression, the clicked candidate is the positive; each positive gets 3 negatives, preferring candidates exposed in the same impression but not clicked (hard negatives) and, when fewer than 3 exist, filling the rest with random active Premium members outside the exposed set (easy negatives).

The score is temperature-scaled cosine similarity s(q,d)=cos(eqfinal​,ed​)/τ, and the objective is a multi-negative softmax loss:
LPAIR​=−logexp(s(q,d+))+∑i=1K​exp(s(q,di−​))exp(s(q,d+))​
The authors state plainly that this has the same computational form as multi-negative contrastive learning (InfoNCE). The idea is inspired by DPO's preference-learning perspective, but it is not a direct embedding-space instance of DPO: there is no reference policy and no token-level probability ratio. The difference lies in what the supervision means: exposed-but-unclicked candidates are options the live retriever actually considers, not ANN-mined neighbors. PAIR therefore learns "whom users prefer within the live exposure distribution", and needs no extra reference-model forward pass. The authors acknowledge the cost: preferences are only observed inside exposed sets, so PAIR inherits selection bias from the historical retrieval policy, which future exploration-aware sampling could mitigate.

The authors note that initial fine-tuning with standard contrastive learning beat the off-the-shelf model, but the embedding space stayed similarity-oriented: a job-seeking engineer was still matched to other engineers. That is what motivated exposure-based negatives.

3. Method II: a cheaply updatable triple-tower intent architecture

To separate stable identity (who the user is) from short-term intent (what they need now), PAIR uses three towers. The query profile tower and document tower share encoder parameters (base Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B), and the history tower aggregates precomputed profile embeddings. Profiles are serialized as structured text: headline, summary, positions, education, skills, certifications, patents, honors, publications and languages.

History aggregation with positional attention (P-ATT). Take the $K=10$ most recently clicked profiles, each with an embedding ehi​​ precomputed by the same encoder. Attention weights are αi​∝exp(w⊤(ehi​​+PE(i))), where PE(i) is a learned positional encoding and the latest click is $i=1$. The history vector is eH​=∑i​αi​ehi​​. The attention is deliberately query-agnostic: it learns global recency and item-importance weights, so the history vector can be refreshed independently of the query profile tower. Query-conditioned attention would be more expressive but adds joint query-history computation and complicates serving.

Normalized additive fusion. Adding the vectors directly causes scale mismatch, so each is L2-normalized first and then combined as eqfinal​=L2Norm(e~q​+γe~H​), with a learnable scalar γ. Profile embeddings are computed in offline batch jobs, and a new click needs only $O(Kd)$ vector operations over a fixed window, with no LLM re-run, cheap enough for CPU.

Dimension-flexible embeddings. A Matryoshka loss trains jointly over prefix dimensions {200,256,512,1024}; at serving time embeddings are truncated to the target size without a separate model. The 200-dim prefix approximates the storage footprint of the 171-dim production embedding.

4. Experimental setup

Training uses DeepSpeed, bf16 and 8 GPUs, with temperature τ=0.02. The authors note that fp16 mixed precision caused gradient overflow under DeepSpeed, so every reported model uses bf16. Data is split by time: the training set has 209K query-level instances involving 4.4M unique member profiles; the test set has 11.8K instances involving 93K profiles. There are two metrics: ACC checks whether the positive ranks first among 4 candidates (1 positive, 3 negatives; random is 0.25), and MAR@k checks whether clicked candidates land in the top k of a fixed 100-candidate pool. ACC saturates quickly, so MAR@k is the main signal.

A few readings. Preference fine-tuning is the main source of gain: the two-tower model without history already reaches 0.436, 51% above the production baseline. History adds a moderate extra gain: P-ATT lifts 0.436 to 0.450, a 3.2% relative improvement, with GeM close behind (0.447). Notably, max pooling, mean pooling and position-free attention all fall slightly below the no-history model, so history signals must be fused carefully, not added indiscriminately. The authors therefore position the history tower mainly as a deployment-friendly mechanism for dynamic intent. Every PAIR variant exceeds 0.96 ACC, showing the 4-way task is nearly saturated.

Dimensionality does not explain the gain. Truncating the same Matryoshka model gives 0.421 at 200 dims, 0.432 at 256, 0.444 at 512 and 0.450 at 1024. At 256 dims, with 25% of the 1024-dim storage and about 0.40× relative latency, MAR@100 is still well above the 171-dim production embedding. The intended uses are 200 dims for candidate pre-filtering, 256 for latency-sensitive channels, 512 for standard retrieval and 1024 for Premium Browsemaps.

Online A/B test. The treatment uses 1024-dim PAIR embeddings with P-ATT history and the control uses production Holistic-171; both arms share the same downstream ranker to isolate the retrieval embedding. Traffic is split by user and stratified by subscription tier and activity level.

The authors discuss a gap: the offline relative MAR@100 gain is large, the online effect much smaller. That is expected in a multi-stage system: better retrieval improves the candidate set, while final engagement also depends on ranking, presentation and user context.

6. Limitations and what it means in practice

The paper lists its limitations: the A/B test used a fixed downstream ranker, and joint optimization of retrieval and ranking may add more; the experiment mainly measures short-term engagement and retention, while longer subscription effects need longer observation; the mixed-negative construction is a practical compromise, and a systematic comparison of random-only, exposure-only and ANN-mined negatives is still to do; and the test covered only Premium Browsemaps, so generalization to email recommendations, job matching and similar surfaces is unverified. Using exposure logs for negatives also carries the historical policy's bias into the model, as the authors themselves note.

For teams building recommendation retrieval, the paper offers several directly usable lessons. First, if your setting is two-sided or complementary matching (hiring, dating, B2B matchmaking, mentor matching), the "similar means relevant" assumption of general semantic embeddings may not hold, and fine-tuning with exposed-but-unclicked negatives is the cheapest, highest-yield step; in the paper it accounts for most of the gain. Second, to add real-time intent without running an LLM online, follow PAIR and make history a weighted sum of precomputed embeddings: query-agnostic positional attention, with $O(Kd)$ updates. First confirm that your history aggregation actually beats no history; three common choices in the paper did worse. Third, when several surfaces share one model, Matryoshka training lets a single model cover multiple storage and latency budgets instead of maintaining several models.
