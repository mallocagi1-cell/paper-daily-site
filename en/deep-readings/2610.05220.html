Ranking Bandits for Carousel Interfaces with Observable Browsing Depth

Takuma Yasuda, Atsuyoshi Nakamura, Dai Hai Nguyen
24 minread4.00score

Classic ranking bandits have an unavoidable blind spot: when a user does not click an item, the learner cannot tell "saw it and passed" from "never saw it". Cascade models and position-based models (PBM) treat examination as latent, and PBM must also separate position bias from item attractiveness, which costs statistical efficiency. In a horizontally scrolling carousel, however, client events can tell the system directly how far the user browsed. This paper formalizes that signal as a ranking-bandit problem and proposes three algorithms, OD-UCB, OD-TS and OD-DMED-L, that update only from confirmed exposures. Over 500,000 rounds in an environment calibrated from RecGaze eye-tracking logs, OD-TS reaches cumulative regret 856.1, comparable to PBM-TS given the true position biases (876.9), while PBM-UCB reaches 10,958.5. OD-TS needs neither the position biases nor the rejection sampling from a non-conjugate posterior that PBM-TS uses. In theory, OD-DMED-L's asymptotic regret upper bound meets the lower bound as its parameter α→0, making it asymptotically optimal.

1. The problem: turning "was it seen?" from a latent variable into an observation

Ranking bandits are the standard framework for balancing exploration and exploitation in recommendation and search: each round shows a ranked set of items and updates attractiveness estimates from clicks. Most methods build on click models:

An example shows the difference (illustrative). Suppose an item has been shown 1,000 times at position 5 and never clicked. Under PBM, the learner only knows that about 1000×P5​ of those showings were seen on average, and its attractiveness estimate must be corrected by the position bias. If P5​ is only 0.08, the effective sample is about 80, and the algorithm must first know or estimate P5​. If the logs record how far each session actually scrolled, you can count only those roughly 80 confirmed exposures and get a clean Bernoulli sample mean directly.

Client events in carousels and scrollable lists can record the farthest position actually displayed, which the authors call browsing depth. At or above that depth, both clicks and confirmed non-clicks are observed; deeper positions are left out. Unexposed items are therefore never mixed in as negatives.

2. Problem setting

There are K items, and each round t shows a ranking a(t) of $L<K$ distinct items. The user always sees the first, keeps going down, and stops after the $V(t)$-th. $V(t)$ is i.i.d. and independent of the display order. Let Pj​=Pr{V(t)≥j} be the probability that position j is seen, with 1=P1​≥⋯≥PL​>0. Each seen item i is clicked independently with unknown probability θi​, a simple multi-click model chosen to isolate the value of depth observation itself.

The reward is the number of clicks, with expectation μ(a)=∑j=1L​Pj​θaj​​. Assuming θ1​>θ2​>⋯>θK​ (unknown to the learner), the optimal ranking puts the top L items in descending order of attractiveness. Regret is RegA​(T)=∑t​Δa(t)​, the sum of per-round gaps to the optimal expected reward.

3. Three algorithms, all updating only from confirmed exposure

All three share one update subroutine: given the user's depth V and clicks on the first V positions, update the exposure count ni​ and click count si​ of only those V items. Because updates happen only after confirmed exposure, θ^i​=si​/ni​ is a direct sample mean of a Bernoulli parameter, with no position-bias correction.

  • OD-UCB: score θ^i​+αlogt/ni​​, infinite for never-exposed items; show the L highest.
  • OD-TS: a Beta(1,1) prior per item gives posterior Beta(1+si​,1+ni​−si​); each round, sample from the posteriors and show the L highest samples. Because exposure is confirmed, the posterior stays conjugate and sampling is very cheap.
  • OD-DMED-L: adapted from DMED. It first rotates through items so each is exposed at least once. Afterwards, when the exploration queue is empty, it shows the L items with the highest sample means and checks every item i outside the current top L against ni​d(θ^i​,θ^aL​​)<(1+α)log(t−1), where d is the Bernoulli KL divergence; items that pass get a ranking enqueued for exploration (first $L-1$ unchanged, last slot replaced by i). Intuitively, it asks "are we confident enough that i is worse than the L-th item?" and, if not, gives it another try in the last slot.

The three differ mainly in how they explore. OD-UCB explores optimistically with upper confidence bounds and is the simplest to implement; OD-TS explores through posterior randomness and performs best at finite horizons in the experiments; OD-DMED-L explores only items with insufficient information, allocating exploration by KL divergence, which is why it can be asymptotically optimal. In all three, exploration competes with the optimal set only at position L: to decide whether an item belongs in the top L, what matters is its gap to the L-th item, Δi​=θL​−θi​.

4. Theoretical results

Lower bound (Theorem 1). For any strongly consistent learner, liminfT→∞​E[Reg(T)]/logT≥∑i=L+1K​Δi​/d(θi​,θL​), where Δi​=θL​−θi​.

Finite-time upper bound for OD-UCB (Theorem 2). For α>1/2, regret grows as logT, with a leading term containing ∑i>L​1/(PL​Δi​). The authors compare it with Lagrée et al.'s bound for PBM-UCB with known position biases: in comparable limits, PBM-UCB's leading term carries an extra factor of PL−2​. When the last displayed position is rarely seen (small PL​), PBM-style bounds degrade sharply, which depth observation avoids. The authors also note that, by Pinsker's inequality, the logT coefficient of the OD-UCB bound is not asymptotically optimal.

Asymptotic upper bound for OD-DMED-L (Theorem 3). For any α>0, limsupE[Reg(T)]/logT≤(1+α)∑j>L​Δj​/d(θj​,θL​). As α→0 this meets the lower bound, so the algorithm is asymptotically optimal in that limit.

5. Experiments

The setup is $K=50$, $L=5$, T=500,000, with three environments. The shallow synthetic environment has exposure probabilities 1.00, 0.55, 0.30, 0.15, 0.08; the deep one has 1.00, 0.80, 0.70, 0.60, 0.50. The RecGaze-calibrated environment comes from a dataset recording eye tracking, clicks, cursor movement and explanations for carousel interactions (87 participants, 3 movie-selection tasks, 3,477 interactions). Processing yields 23,075 usable observations; the 50 most-exposed items (85–87 confirmed exposures each) and the first 5 positions are kept, giving exposure probabilities 1.0000, 0.9031, 0.8529, 0.7720, 0.6879, with Laplace-smoothed click rates. Baselines are PBM-UCB and PBM-TS (given the true position biases Pj​ but not the realized depth) and LastClick-UCB (positions up to the last click count as exposed; with no click, the whole list counts as exposed). Within each seed, all methods share the same depth and click random draws.

OD-TS and PBM-TS have overlapping confidence intervals in all three environments, but PBM-TS needs the true marginal exposure probabilities. All three observable-depth methods beat PBM-UCB and LastClick-UCB in every environment. The shallow-versus-deep comparison suggests that the faster exposure decays with position, the more depth observation helps: PBM-UCB's regret is about 3.1× OD-UCB's in the shallow environment and about 1.4× in the deep one.

Asymptotic behavior. To test the theory, the deep environment is run to T=107. For this instance the Theorem 1 coefficient is 218.45, and the OD-DMED-L reference is (1+α)×218.45=240.30 (α=0.1). OD-DMED-L's Reg(t)/logt peaks at 291.78 near t≈5.09×105 and falls to 261.30 at 107, moving toward 240.30. The median information allocation is 1.102 near 106 (close to the threshold 1.1) and 1.053 at 107, between the threshold and the lower-bound target of 1.

6. Limitations and what it means in practice

The authors state the limitations clearly. Clicks are assumed independent, and browsing depth is assumed independent of display order. The RecGaze experiment is a model-driven simulation calibrated from logs, not an online deployment, and does not establish regret under real user behavior. The high click rates of a few items may reflect task-specific intent rather than context-free attractiveness. RecGaze's exposure is even deeper than the deep synthetic environment, so its differences cannot be attributed to browsing depth alone. Future work includes relaxing the assumptions, online validation, a sharper OD-UCB analysis, and finite-time guarantees for OD-DMED-L and OD-TS.

In practice, the assumption that depth is independent of order may not hold: very attractive content near the front might make users scroll farther, or click and leave. The paper does not handle this dependence.

For recommendation teams, the most direct lesson is to check whether your logs already contain exposure depth. Many carousel, horizontal-scroll and feed clients already report visible positions or scroll depth, yet offline training and online exploration still treat shown-but-unseen items as negatives. In bandit or cold-start exploration, updating only from confirmed exposure and running Thompson sampling with a conjugate Beta posterior is simple to implement and needs no position-bias estimate; in the paper it matches PBM-TS given the true position biases. A rollout can go in three steps. First, store the farthest visible position of each impression as a log field aligned with click events. Second, compute empirical exposure probabilities per position offline and see how fast they decay; the faster the decay, the more confirmed exposure pays off. Third, swap the update rule on exploration traffic first and compare cumulative clicks against the existing PBM or last-click rule. Also note that the paper assumes depth is independent of order, so before launch check that the depth distribution is stable across rankings. The signal is worth most on interfaces where exposure decays quickly with position, such as mobile horizontal scrolling.
