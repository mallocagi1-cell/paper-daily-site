A Cue's Hidden Price: Auditing Seller Influence on AI Shoppers

Felix Buecheler, Gerardo Berbeglia, Mingyu Guo
26 minread4.00score

At equal price, GPT-4o chose the headphones carrying "Our best-selling model, chosen by over 10,000 customers" in all 144 trials. Its price had to rise by about 13% (roughly $17 on a $130 item) before its selection share fell to one half. A plain neutral sentence was also chosen almost 100% of the time at equal price, but it was abandoned at a premium of about 2%. The University of Adelaide authors argue that the usual choice-rate audit has a ceiling: strong cues all sit near 100% and cannot be told apart. The paper proposes price-equivalent (PE) auditing, which prices a seller cue in money: raise the cued product's price until it is chosen only half the time, and that premium is the cue's PE. Across 7 models, 2 categories and 113,472 trials, six conditions all chosen at ≥95% at equal price span PEs from 1.6% to 21.4%, and the choice-rate ranking can even flip.

1. The problem: how much more will content make a shopping agent pay

LLMs are moving from answering questions to acting for users, browsing, comparing and buying. Once the decision-maker is a model, the seller's lever shifts from persuading a person to steering an agent. A "best-seller" badge, an expert endorsement, or a different phrasing of a spec can all tilt the agent toward a product. The authors treat such a cue as a hidden discount: like a price cut it raises the product's chance of being chosen, but the price is unchanged and the buyer cannot see it. This is the core question of generative engine optimization (GEO): not whether seller content moves agents, but by how much.

Prior work mostly reports influence as a change in choice probability, a scale that misleads where it matters. Strong cues push the choice rate to nearly 100% and become indistinguishable; after position balancing, primacy is averaged into 50%, which looks like indifference. Knowing a cue "raised the choice rate" does not tell you whether it is worth pennies or a large share of the price.

2. Method: price-equivalent auditing

Task. A product pair is matched on price, star rating, rating count and specs; only one carries an appended cue sentence. In a two-alternative choice the authors record the equal-price probability of choosing the cued product, then raise its price in 8 steps: 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 18, 26%, denser near zero where most crossings fall. The PE is the premium, as a percentage of the base price, at which the cued product's selection share falls to one half.

Formal definition. Given a model and prompt environment M and a pair distribution Q, the auditor fixes a presentation policy π that sets which product carries the cue and whether it is listed first or second; the paper uses a balanced policy with each of the four states at 1/4. With SQ​(g) the cued product's selection probability at premium g averaged over π,

The authors stress that PE depends on the cue contrast, products, presentation policy, model, prompt and decoding configuration, and in general cannot be read as an individual indifference point or willingness to pay.

Estimation and censoring. The primary estimator is model-free: linear interpolation between the two neighbouring price points where the pooled curve crosses 0.5. Because the equal-price choice is often 1, a global logistic fit would be distorted. A cue already dispreferred at equal price (choice rate below 0.5) is reported as PE ≤ 0; a curve that never falls to one half within 26% is right-censored to a lower bound. Confidence intervals use a bootstrap resampled by product pair; with only 6 pairs, intervals may be too narrow.

Position control. In every (pair, price-point) cell the cued product is first in half the trials and second in the other half, and the cue sits on A and B equally often, with 6 calls in each of the four states.

Setup. A cue is one sentence appended to a neutral description. The set covers four intensities of social proof ("A popular choice." up to "The #1 best-selling model, chosen by over 100,000 customers."), scarcity ("Only a few left in stock."), authority ("Recommended by audio experts."), information-preserving rephrasings (percent vs fraction, a rescaled rating, and gain/loss framing "9 out of 10 buyers keep it." vs "1 out of 10 buyers returns it."), plus neutral, vacuous, length-matched and presence controls. Products are 6 headphone pairs ($59.99–$199.99) and 6 coffee-machine pairs ($49.99–$299.99). Models are strong/weak pairs from four providers: GPT-4o/GPT-4o mini, Claude Sonnet/Haiku, Nova Pro/Micro, Llama-3.3-70B/3.1-8B. Nova Pro is excluded for a high non-response rate (21%), and Llama-3.1-8B, which picks the first option in about 97% of trials, is excluded from cross-model claims. Temperature is 0.7 with 24 repetitions per (pair, price point), so one condition is 6×8×24=1152 trials.

3. Core finding: price separates what choice rates cannot

On GPT-4o, each cue condition is plotted on two axes: the equal-price choice rate a conventional audit would report, and the PE.

The sharpest case is scarcity. At equal price its choice rate is 0.95, higher than gain/loss framing's 0.72, yet its PE is only 1.6% against 22.3% for gain/loss, a fourteenfold gap with disjoint intervals. A choice-rate audit ranks the two the wrong way round. The authors note this is not a precision problem that more trials would fix: the two quantities measure different things, how often a cue wins when price is tied and how much price it can absorb. A cue can be "broad and shallow" (scarcity) or "narrow and deep" (gain/loss framing), and only the latter is what a seller can convert into money.

A few readings. The four social-proof intensities give a monotone, sharply nonlinear PE: weak and moderate sit near zero and the premium switches on only at "strong", and on headphones the three lower tiers have non-overlapping intervals, evidence that the instrument tracks persuasive strength. The length-matched control at about 10.6% shows the premium comes from the sentence's meaning, not from an extra sentence. The model is indifferent to number-preserving rewrites (rating rescale, percent to fraction) yet strongly swayed by a gain/loss framing of the same fact: it responds to the form of a statement, not to its information. The whole pattern replicates on coffee machines; authority reaches about 21.6% when domain-matched, but "recommended by audio experts" on a coffee machine is only about 1.9%, so the model cares whether authority is relevant. A length-matched 23 factorial over social proof, authority and scarcity detects no interaction, consistent with additivity, though with only 6 pairs moderate interactions cannot be ruled out.

4. Across models, position bias and a native decision model

Across models. On the six task-engaged models social proof keeps its step pattern: the weak and moderate tiers stay at or below 4%, and at least one strong tier lies clearly above; scarcity commands at most 2% on any model. Yet at equal price GPT-4o and GPT-4o mini prefer the scarcity product (0.95, 0.88) while Claude Sonnet finds it aversive (0.19). Raw authority (1.6–19.8%) and gain/loss (0–22.3%) PEs look wildly different, but PE combines cue pull with price sensitivity: at +10%, GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet and Llama-3.3-70B almost never keep the pricier identical product, while Nova Micro does about half the time. Claude Sonnet's 1.6% authority PE reflects a steep price response, not an unmoved model; Nova Micro keeps choosing the cued product at more than double the price, so its PE is unidentified and its apparent susceptibility is price-insensitivity. Raw PE should not be ranked across models.

Position bias. GPT-4o picks the first option in 56.3% of all trials, rising to about 65% in low-signal conditions. After balancing, the pooled PE is the crossing of the averaged curves, not the mean of the two slot crossings: scarcity is 6.1% when first, 0.9% when second, and 1.6% pooled. The authors also price "first slot" itself: at most 3.2% on price-sensitive models, 14.0% on GPT-4o mini, and beyond 80% on the price-blind Nova Micro and Llama-3.1-8B. This is a warning to any practice that reads preferences off LLM choices, including LLM-as-judge.

How much wording matters. Twelve truthful, LLM-generated rewrites of one full description (all weaving in strong social proof and scarcity) move GPT-4o's PE between 2.3% and 8.0%. Wording is a real lever, but several times smaller than the choice of cue (0.7–21.4%). Under four equivalent task phrasings the social-proof ordering holds, but strong-cue premia drift by up to about 8 points. Selecting the top-PE cue among 9 on 5 headphone pairs, its out-of-sample PE is 13.3 points above the mean of the nine.

Native decision model Jev. Jev returns a probability per option rather than generating text. The ceiling replicates: four cues are chosen in every equal-price trial yet span 1.5–16.6% once priced (headphones). Its profile differs from every text model: social proof switches on only at "very strong" (15.4%), authority is worth 1.5% (19.8% for GPT-4o), and gain/loss framing is worth 0; the off-domain "audio experts" sentence on a coffee machine is never chosen at equal price in 144 trials. Input format also changes Jev's prices: passing cards as a structured object raised strong social proof from 2.0% to 10.8%.

5. Five rules for auditing in practice

The authors distil their results into reusable operating rules: counterbalance position and cue target within every cell; read the equal-price rate as a two-sided validity gate, where every cue near 0.5 signals a model not engaging the task and exactly 1.00 signals an axis with no resolution left; always run a cue-free price-only control, since PE is the ratio of cue pull to price sensitivity; mark censoring explicitly, because a censored value is a bound and must not enter a ranking; and read PE as ordinal-to-interval, where rankings and coarse bands are trustworthy but exact percentages are not.

6. Limitations and industry relevance

The paper states its limits plainly. It is a controlled synthetic two-alternative choice that measures susceptibility under clean conditions and does not predict a live shopping environment, where cues compete with search, reviews and multi-step navigation. "Non-human" is a contrast with the persuasion literature, not a matched human baseline. Scarcity was tested in one formulation, so its low PE means low for that sentence, not immunity. Each condition has only 6 product pairs, both categories are consumer-electronics durables, and services such as travel or lodging are untested. The models are a convenience sample, so claims hold for these models only. The authors also acknowledge the tool is dual-use: it serves governance and auditing as well as agent-facing content optimization. Their line is truthfulness, with every cue a sentence plausibly true of the product, and they release logs, stimuli and code but not a turnkey library of optimized manipulative descriptions.

For teams building shopping agents or running e-commerce platforms, three takeaways stand out. First, when checking whether an agent is being steered, do not look only at choice rates; add a price axis, or strong cues all pile up at the ceiling. Second, position bias is itself a platform-owned, priceable channel, and ranking policies deserve the same audit as cues. Third, do not carry an audit from one model to another; measure its price sensitivity first. The code and data are open, so the protocol can be re-run directly on your own models and categories.
