AI Search — Paper Daily, 2026-11-03

Today's AI search track is about whether the evaluation itself can be trusted. One paper finds that the stronger the retriever, the less reliable the shortcut of judging answerability from relevance labels; another shows that attack success rate hides the new errors RAG defenses introduce; a third maps what visual document retrieval benchmarks actually test. On methods, DecisionRank teaches a single-pass reranker to rank like a generative teacher at a fraction of the latency, and another paper takes apart how BERT cross-encoders decide relevance step by step.

6. Prevented is Not Protected: What Attack Success Rate Hides about RAG

RAG poisoning defenses are usually judged by one number: how much the attack success rate drops once the defense is on. Attack success rate is the share of answers in which the model repeats the attacker's answer. The authors store each query's answer under four conditions, clean or poisoned retrieval, each with and without the defense, and compare them one by one. Defenses do block some attacks, but they also make other queries that were not fooled start giving the attacker's answer, and the success rate nets this out. Retrieval-score detectors have a blind spot too: with one poisoned passage in context they stop 59 of 60 poisoned answers, and with five poisoned passages they stop none. A defense report should show both what it prevented and what it introduced.

7. Pix2Evidence: A Multimodal RAG System for Text and Visual Evidence

Screenshot-based RAG keeps images and layout, but written facts inside screenshots, such as names, dates and numbers, are hard to retrieve. Pix2Evidence encodes each screenshot tile together with its transcription into one vector, so layout and text count together at retrieval time. During training, a vision-language model writes, for each training question, a description of the screenshot content that supports the answer. Besides standard contrastive learning, the retriever is trained so that the tile ranking produced by the question matches the ranking produced by that description. Without reranking, its answer accuracy beats the text-extraction and screenshot-only baselines on four of five Wikipedia QA benchmarks, and on MMSearch it reaches 26.0%, 4.4 points above the screenshot-only PixelRAG.

8. Search When Needed, Proceed When Reliable: Metacognitive Reliability Supervision for Efficient Agentic Search

At every step an agentic searcher must choose: continue with what it already knows, or search first. Rewarding only final answers cannot tell a bad search choice from bad retrieval or reasoning. This paper scores each step on its own. The model rewrites the step's intermediate conclusion several times, the mean negative log-likelihood of the generated tokens is taken as uncertainty, and a curve fitted before training converts it into a probability of being right, the reliability. A no-search step scores its reliability minus the average reliability on a calibration set. A search step keeps the same query, rewrites the conclusion with and without the returned passages, and scores the gain in reliability. Trained with GRPO, its average CEM (whether the gold answer appears in the response) across seven QA benchmarks beats HiPRAG by 2.1 points with 6.8% fewer searches.

9. Context as Infrastructure: Knowledge Compilation for Retrieval in Agentic Systems with Harnesses

With only BM25 and keyword lookup as retrieval tools, plus a search loop that rewrites queries and decides when to stop, an LLM answers 85% of BrowseComp-Plus. The authors argue accuracy is no longer the main problem; context is. The loop repeats the same searches, rereads the same documents and fills the context window with passages it never uses. RAGFlow's answer is to have an LLM split documents at ingestion into single claims, each carrying its verbatim evidence sentence. At query time the loop checks these claims first, and when a gate judges the evidence sufficient it reads only those sentences instead of whole passages. Compared with the same loop reading raw passages, total tokens per query drop by 91% when that evidence suffices and rise only 1.9% when it does not, for a 38% average saving on FRAMES. Graphs, wiki pages and similar structures show no measurable accuracy gain.

10. Defining Page-Centric Visual Document Retrieval and Evaluating Existing Benchmarks

Word files, PDFs and slide decks are organised by page, yet most visual document retrieval benchmarks test one thing: given a question, find a single page. This paper first separates the task by granularity, either finding pages or finding whole documents. It then separates it by mode, which can mean ranking items one by one, finding a set of pages that only together form the evidence, or searching over several rounds. Finally it checks 42 existing benchmarks against eight criteria. Most cover only single-page, one-by-one retrieval. Questions from real user needs and graded relevance labels are rare, and whether labels cover the whole corpus varies widely. Before picking a benchmark, check that it tests the ability the application actually needs.
