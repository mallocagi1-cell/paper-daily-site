1. How Language Models Learn Taste: Brand Preference in LLMs

Asked to continue the sentence “If you want a good web browser, I recommend”, the base Tulu model, before any assistant training, says Firefox in 13 of 20 samples. Given the sentence “A well-known web browser is”, it most often says Chrome. In other words, the lean toward Firefox when asked for advice exists before the model learns to act as an assistant. Across 102 models, the authors compare two ways of asking about the same category, one asking the model to name a well-known brand and the other asking it to recommend and pick one. In 26 of 42 categories the picked brand differs from the named one, and different models switch to the same brand, such as Nike becoming Brooks and Chrome becoming Firefox. The authors also compare checkpoints saved at different training stages and search the training data for explicit brand recommendations to see whether they explain the shift. What a model recommends differs from what it knows is famous, and brand preference starts forming in pretraining.

2. A Cue's Hidden Price: Auditing Seller Influence on AI Shoppers

The researchers prepared six pairs of headphones, each pair identical in price, star rating, review count and specs. One listing in each pair got the extra sentence “Our best-selling model, chosen by over 10,000 customers.” Asked to pick one, GPT-4o chose the listing with that sentence in 144 of 144 trials. The authors then raised that product's price step by step, and only at about 13% more, roughly 17 dollars on a 130-dollar item, did GPT-4o pick it half the time and the other one half the time. Running the same test with a plain product sentence instead, the product was also chosen almost every time at equal price, but a premium of about 2% was enough to bring its share down to half. Judged by how often they win at equal price the two sentences look almost the same, but judged by what they are worth they differ sixfold. This paper from Adelaide University and Melbourne Business School puts such a price on every sentence a seller writes. Win rates lump strong and weak seller claims together; converting them into a price tells them apart.

LEAD STORY · LinkedIn

1. PAIR: Preference-Aligned Intent Retrieval for Premium People Recommendation at LinkedIn

LinkedIn

A software engineer who starts job hunting stops clicking fellow engineers on LinkedIn and starts clicking recruiters, hiring managers and talent acquisition staff, so recommending people by profile similarity alone gets it wrong. In an A/B test in the module that recommends people to Premium members, LinkedIn's new method raised click-through rate by 7.5% and Premium retention by 2.1% relative to the control, with page load latency nearly unchanged. It trains a profile encoder to rank people a user clicked above people shown but not clicked. It then combines the user's own profile vector with a weighted mix of recently clicked profiles to represent current interests. Clicked profiles are encoded in advance, so updating interests takes only vector arithmetic, with no need to rerun the language model. People recommendation should follow a user's recent clicks, because when someone's goal changes, so do the people they want to meet.

2. Ranking Bandits for Carousel Interfaces with Observable Browsing Depth

Using 3,477 movie-selection records from the RecGaze eye-tracking dataset, the authors simulate a horizontally scrolling movie carousel and look only at the first 5 slots. Their algorithm learns each round how far the user scrolled before stopping, while the comparison algorithm gets only the clicks but is handed the true probability that each slot is seen. At the end, their OD-TS has a cumulative regret of 856.1 against 876.9 for the comparison, with overlapping confidence intervals, so it is effectively a tie. The approach is simple. Only movies the user is confirmed to have scrolled to and seen update the click estimates, and movies never reached do not count as dislikes. In a setting where most users never reach the end and slot 5 is seen with probability 0.08, the two still end up close. Recording how deep users scroll a carousel is worth as much as knowing each slot's exposure probability in advance.

LEAD STORY · Adobe

1. When Agentic Retrieval Fails Silently: Framework and Production Evidence from a Creative AI Assistant

Adobe

Adobe compared gallery search in Adobe Express with its chat assistant, using the same retrieval system and the same users, and found that 44.4% of chat users reformulated their requests against 33.1% in gallery search. But most chat reformulations were requests like “make it bluer” or like “try a bolder font”, which refine the generated output rather than signal a failed search. In another case a user starts an export from chat and then leaves the chat screen, so the export is logged under a different session and chat abandonment shows as 59.5%, even though the user did export the work. The paper therefore suggests splitting reformulations into a new search for the same need and a correction of generated output, and carrying the full retrieved list and retrieval identifiers through to later actions. Retrieval quality, whether assets get used and whether the task succeeds should be measured separately. Once search becomes a chat assistant, old metrics like reformulation and abandonment rates change meaning, and reusing them as is misjudges retrieval quality.

2. AutoSim: A Self-Improving User Simulator for Conversational Search Evaluation

On the harder iKAT 2024 conversational search data, the authors ran the same BM25 retrieval system on real users' questions and on questions generated by a simulated user. The default simulator made the system look like it scored 0.15 nDCG, well above an illustrative launch threshold of 0.05, while real users actually got 0.02. The gap comes from the simulator not talking like a person. Even shown short real questions as examples, its messages matched human length but a classifier still told them apart with an AUC of 0.94. The authors train a small language model by repeatedly generating candidate questions, keeping those the classifier finds most human, and fine-tuning on them. With this simulator the classifier's AUC falls to 0.45, close to guessing, and its retrieval score of 0.03 is close to the real users' 0.02. Before using a simulated user to evaluate conversational search, check that it talks like a person, or a failing system may look ready to ship.

LEAD STORY · Amazon

1. ATTUNE: Agentic Throughput Tuning for LLM Training

Amazon

Amazon engineers trained MiniMax-M2 on 64 B200 GPUs, starting from a recipe ported from a reinforcement-learning setup written for 128 GPUs that kept optimizer state in CPU memory. A coding agent asked to tune it turned that offload off and deepened the pipeline, and across three tuning runs throughput rose by 164.2% on average. Run for 1,000 steps, the tuned recipe stayed 2.66 times as fast as the public one, with loss differences within normal run-to-run noise. The agent reads framework code and run logs, finds the bottleneck, changes one setting or one group of related parallelism settings at a time, and keeps measuring within a fixed GPU-hour budget. Separate fixed checks block changes that alter the training objective and compare the first-step loss. On Amazon's real post-training jobs the tuned recipes were 1.7 times faster on average and up to 3.9 times faster. Training recipes hide a lot of untuned speed, and letting an agent search for it under strict correctness checks works.

2. uGDS: A User-Space Development Kit for High-Performance GPU Direct Storage

Running Qwen3-0.6B with vLLM and LMCache on an A100 GPU, the authors turned off in-memory prefix caching so reusable attention caches could only be read back from a Samsung SSD. For an already cached prompt of 32,768 tokens, NVIDIA's own GDS took 1915 ms to produce the first token; the paper's uGDS took 715 ms. GDS is the technology that lets an SSD send data straight into GPU memory without passing through host memory. After a one-time privileged setup, uGDS has the CPU issue storage commands and handle completions directly in user space, cutting out the middle layers. In a test with small 4 KiB reads and writes, its read bandwidth was 3302 MiB/s against 276 MiB/s for GDS. When LLM serving keeps caches on SSD, the software path for reading them back can itself be the biggest source of delay.
