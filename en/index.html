LEAD STORY · Alibaba / Nankai University

1. ReSID: Rethinking Long-Tail Retrieval in Generative Recommendation through Item Reachability

AlibabaNankai University

Generative recommendation encodes each item as a string of Semantic ID tokens and generates them one position at a time with beam search, and cold long-tail items are hard to retrieve this way. The authors take apart the whole chain from encoding through decoding to matching and find that tail items are mostly lost early in decoding: on Amazon Industrial, 62.5% of tail targets have already fallen out of the beam after just two tokens. They also find that parallel SIDs, which split the vector into segments and quantize each separately, treat the tail better than sequential SIDs that quantize residuals layer by layer. The reason is that an error at one position does not spill into later ones, so a partly wrong sequence still keeps useful positions. ReSID therefore damps popularity bias and adds a user-semantic score inside beam search so tail prefixes survive, then allows a match even when some positions disagree. It now serves main traffic, raising ad revenue by 1.3% and the long-tail exposure share by 4.0%. Tail items are often pruned during decoding, and looser matching afterwards cannot bring them back.

2. TriGen: A Generative Paradigm for Trigger-Induced Recommendation

AlibabaNankai University

E-commerce has a common kind of recommendation: a user clicks an item in the main feed and lands at once in a feed built around that item. The recommendations must follow the intent of that click without throwing away the user's long-term taste. Generative recommendation by default just continues the user's history with more SIDs, so one click is easily diluted in a long history, while traditional methods rely on discriminative scoring and cannot steer token-by-token generation. Alibaba International's TriGen appends several copies of the clicked item to the end of the history so it mainly reshapes recent behavior. It then uses a gate to decide per request whether to follow the click or the history more, and keeps injecting the click signal at every decoding position. In online experiments in six Southeast Asian countries, revenue rose 3.27% and GMV 0.90%, and it is now fully deployed. In click-triggered feeds, the hard part is how much the click should override history, and that changes every time.

1. When Does a Recommendation Spark Curiosity? Theory, Measurement, and Field Evidence

Recommenders are often asked to break users out of filter bubbles, yet a user's curiosity has never had a definition a system can compute. Existing proxies such as unexpectedness and variety-seeking only look at how far an item sits from the user's taste. Picture two videos equally far from that taste: the user has watched many items similar to the first and none similar to the second. Distance scores them the same, but curiosity needs partial knowledge, so only the first makes the user aware of something missing. The authors turn the information-gap theory from psychology into five axioms and derive a curiosity score computed from consumption records alone, which matches self-reported curiosity in a survey. With this score added to the ranking objective next to relevance, an online experiment on a large Asian video platform raised click-through by 2.75 percentage points and watch time by 0.71 minutes per recommendation, and the method is now deployed. Curiosity depends on how much the user already knows, and distance alone cannot measure it.

2. Serving-Aligned Entire-Space Conversion Modeling via Click-Through Rate Knowledge Distillation

Arizona State UniversityReddit

Ranking often multiplies a click-through prediction by a conversion prediction to get the probability of a click followed by a purchase. Many conversion models are trained with ESMM, which carries its own auxiliary click-through tower trained together with the conversion tower, but only the conversion tower is deployed and multiplied by a separately trained production click-through model. The trouble is that the conversion tower was trained alongside the auxiliary tower's click rates, while in serving it is multiplied by the production model's click rates, so the product drifts when the two disagree. The Reddit authors leave the production click-through model alone and distill ESMM's auxiliary tower from the production click-through scores already stored in the logs, so the tower matches the production model during training. On Ali-CCP, the best setting raises PR-AUC of the product actually used in serving by 9.9%, and the conversion tower itself improves too. The gain survives when only 5% of negatives are kept. In joint multi-tower training, the auxiliary tower should learn the model it is actually paired with in serving.

LEAD STORY · Elastic

1. Too much of a good thing: Why 20 candidates beat 200 when reranking search results

Elastic

Is it always better to give a reranker more candidates? Jina's team at Elastic tested its own listwise reranker on more than two dozen retrieval benchmarks, and the answer is no. With BM25 as the first stage, reranking the top 100 candidates works best, cutting about a fifth of the error on average, and adding more makes it worse. With semantic search as the first stage, the best point moves down to 20 candidates and the gain is much smaller, and sending 200 is on average worse than not reranking at all. The reason is that a listwise model reads the whole candidate list at once, so with too many candidates its attention is spread thin and noise accumulates. A model that scores candidates one by one does not have this problem, since an extra candidate never changes the order of the others. The worse the first stage, the more candidates reranking needs and the more room it has to fix things. Size the candidate pool by the quality of the first stage, because sending too many hurts

2. CURATOR: Scalable Fine-grained Evidence Selection for Retrieval-Augmented Generation using Decision Models

Amazon

RAG usually stuffs whole retrieved passages into the LLM, though often only a few sentences in them can answer the question. Picking those sentences is not easy: a sentence can look irrelevant on its own, and whether to keep it may depend on other retrieved text. Judging each sentence with the full context is too expensive. Decision models can score many candidate sentences in one pass, with each judgment seeing the surrounding sentences. But the authors find that the more judgments they must make at once, the worse they get at telling useful evidence apart, and one retrieval step often brings hundreds of candidates. CURATOR trains a decision model specifically to pick a variable number of supporting sentences from a large pool while keeping the efficiency of scoring in one pass. Under a 128-token context budget, it raises evidence recall by 17.0% on average on unseen datasets and domains, and downstream answer quality by 17.4%. The tighter the context, the more it matters to pick the right evidence rather than add more passages

1. Authenticated Approximate Nearest Neighbor Search in Outsourced Vector Databases

Once a vector database is outsourced to a third-party cloud, the provider may skip neighbors to save compute or even tamper with results, so the client needs a way to verify that a nearest-neighbor search is genuine. Existing methods have the cloud send back the traversal path on the graph index along with every raw vector visited on the way, and the client recomputes the distances itself. Embeddings often have thousands of dimensions, so this proof becomes enormous. VIDA sends vector commitments instead, whose size does not depend on the dimension, and uses one aggregated inner product proof to verify many distance computations at once. A matching authentication structure makes the proof paths follow the graph traversal, and distance bounds from quantization prune a batch of points that then need no cryptographic check. Against the best existing methods, end-to-end latency is up to 6.6 times faster and data transfer time drops by up to 99.2%. Most of the verification cost is shipping raw high-dimensional vectors, and commitments remove most of it.

2. Benchmarking tiered KV cache offloading on H100 GPUs in OpenShift AI 3.5

Red Hat

Long-context agent requests resend the same prefix again and again, so the KV cache could be reused, but once GPU memory fills up it is thrown away and recomputed next time. Red Hat offloads the KV cache in OpenShift AI 3.5 to CPU memory, local NVMe, or shared CephFS, and gives a rule for judging a tier: it is worth using only when its p99 restore latency is below the prefill time a cache hit saves. On agent workloads averaging about 50 thousand input tokens, Nemotron-Ultra-253B throughput rose by up to 5.8 times. The difference depends on whether the working set fits in the CPU tier. When it does not, offloading to CPU memory alone gives only 3.3 times, and adding NVMe or CephFS nearly doubles that. When it fits, the CPU tier alone captures most of the gain. With multiple replicas under high concurrency, shared CephFS lets replicas reuse the same cache. Size the working set first, then decide whether a second storage tier is needed

1. What Makes a VLA Reactive? A Study of Execution Strategies for Dynamic Manipulation

A VLA model predicts a short chunk of future actions at a time, and when objects move, that chunk goes stale before it finishes executing. The authors use a pretrained π0.5 on two dynamic simulation benchmarks and three real-robot tasks. They systematically compare how far to predict, how many steps to execute each time, and synchronous or asynchronous inference. The best prediction length is about one second, asynchronous inference helps more when the execution horizon is short, and methods that keep successive trajectories continuous beat those that only reduce latency. The biggest surprise is the conveyor picking task. Fine-tuning the policy with reinforcement learning fixes targeted failures, but the same checkpoint with no training at all reaches 71% success just by setting the execution horizon to 5, as good as after training. How fast a robot reacts depends on how it executes as much as on the policy, so evaluate the two together

2. Scaling VLA Serving Under Robotic Execution Constraints

The authors' company already has hundreds of VLA-driven robots working in real environments, and robots must share GPUs to keep inference costs down. Robots keep executing old actions while waiting for new ones. So among requests of the same size, some robots are about to run out and need a reply now, while others can wait. Admitting one more request on the GPU also slows the requests already running, and once a robot's action queue empties it must stop and wait. XInfer breaks VLA computation into stages that proceed as soon as their dependencies are ready, and its scheduler uses each robot's current urgency to decide what to compute first and when a new request may join. On three VLA models with production-derived workloads, the number of robots one GPU can serve under real-time requirements is 1.26 to 3.75 times that of the best existing systems. When serving a robot fleet, a request's urgency depends on how many actions the robot has left
