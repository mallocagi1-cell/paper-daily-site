1. How Language Models Learn Taste: Brand Preference in LLMs

Ask a model to name a running-shoe brand and it says Nike; ask it to recommend one and it says Brooks. Across 102 models and 42 categories, the recommended brand disagrees with the named brand in 26 categories. Named brands are mostly market leaders; picked brands are mostly niche specialists. Across three open training pipelines, pretraining already attaches the recommended brand to advice-style prompts, SFT mostly leaves the pick unchanged, and DPO moves it by 11–24 points in every pipeline. The preference data contains almost no brand comparisons, yet DPO still shifts judgments—even sauce choices from ketchup to pesto. Recommendation taste forms mainly in preference optimization, not only from what the model “knows” about brands.

2. A Cue's Hidden Price: Auditing Seller Influence on AI Shoppers

When an LLM shops for a user, seller cues such as “best-seller,” endorsements, or scarcity can steer its pick. Choice-rate audits hit a ceiling once a cue already wins near 100% of the time. Price-equivalent (PE) auditing raises the cued product’s price in a counterbalanced forced choice until it is chosen only half the time, and reports that premium as the cue’s PE. Across 113,472 trials, seven models, and two categories, cues that look equally strong by choice rate can differ several-fold in PE. The decision-native model Jev resists most cues yet still bends to the strongest social-proof claims. Pricing seller content in money makes different cues comparable.

LEAD STORY · LinkedIn

1. PAIR: Preference-Aligned Intent Retrieval for Premium People Recommendation at LinkedIn

LinkedIn

LinkedIn finds that for Premium people-browsemap retrieval, the production member embedding Holistic-171 only reaches MAR@100 0.288, and an off-the-shelf 0.6B LLM embedding scores 0.192—neither was trained under the exposure condition of who the viewer already saw. PAIR treats exposure as the conditioning for preference learning; a same-scale 0.6B model lifts MAR@100 to 0.450 (56% relative over Holistic, 2.3× over the base encoder). Online A/B on Premium Browsemaps: CTR +7.5%, Premium retention +2.1%, no measurable latency regression. Who you already saw is preference signal, not noise: condition the objective on exposure and a small model can beat a broad member vector.

2. Ranking Bandits for Carousel Interfaces with Observable Browsing Depth

In carousels and feeds users stop at a browsing depth; positions below that depth are neither clicked nor exposed, yet classical position-based models (PBM) still treat them as “seen but not clicked.” This paper proposes OD-UCB / OD-TS / OD-DMED under observable depth: update item stats only from truly exposed positions, with an instance-dependent log upper bound for OD-UCB. On synthetic shallow/deep environments and RecGaze-parameterized runs, OD-TS matches PBM-TS on final mean regret while all three beat PBM-UCB. When browse depth is observed, do not treat unseen slots as negative feedback.

LEAD STORY · Adobe

1. When Agentic Retrieval Fails Silently: Framework and Production Evidence from a Creative AI Assistant

Adobe

CTR, MRR and behavioral NDCG assume the user sees and acts on ranked results. In an agentic assistant a generation layer consumes retrieval first, so attribution breaks on who saw the results, when the user acted, and what caused the outcome—Adobe calls this the attribution gap. LARA (Layered Agentic Retrieval Assessment) measures retrieval quality, engagement and downstream outcomes by layer; production retrieval rate on assistant prompts is only 0.31%, and chat users reformulate more than gallery users (44.4% vs 33.1%). Do not evaluate agent retrieval as if the user clicked a SERP—attribute by layer.

2. AutoSim: A Self-Improving User Simulator for Conversational Search Evaluation

Conversational search needs trustworthy user simulators for scale. AutoSim’s relevance judgments rank a controlled pool of retrieval systems in agreement with NIST across three TREC iKAT years (Kendall τ 0.66 / 0.91 / 0.91, all p≤0.002), and that agreement survives removing its persona. Self-improving against held-out real-user signal, weight-level self-improvement drives real-vs-sim AUC from ~0.85 down to 0.45, lifts retrieval +0.136 nDCG, and returns a relevant passage on 57% of turns. A simulator is trustworthy when it ranks systems like NIST, not when it merely sounds human.

LEAD STORY · Amazon

1. ATTUNE: Agentic Throughput Tuning for LLM Training

Amazon

LLM training throughput hinges on the recipe—sharding, memory–compute trade-offs, kernels and overlap—and that recipe changes with every model, accelerator and framework. Fixed-knob tuners only search a preset space and check that a job runs, so they miss levers outside that space and can silently change training semantics. Amazon’s ATTUNE is a coding agent that retunes any working recipe unattended inside a GPU-hour budget. On H100/H200/B200 it lifts throughput 1.7× on average and beats four of NVIDIA AutoModel’s published recipes by 6–17%; on held-out models it improves recipes written for another setting by up to 2.6× (MiniMax-M2). Throughput gaps often live outside the fixed knob space—letting a coding agent edit the recipe beats grid search.

2. uGDS: A User-Space Development Kit for High-Performance GPU Direct Storage

NVIDIA GDS moves NVMe↔GPU over PCIe P2P DMA, but still routes every I/O control path through the kernel, imposes total order on the stream path, and ships a closed user-space engine. uGDS is a user-space kit: on a Samsung 990 PRO it reaches up to 12.12× GDS batch-read and 30.69× write bandwidth, and cuts 4-KiB sync write latency 84.1% with two issuing threads. On one stream GDS keeps only 40.8% of its sync 16-MiB read bandwidth; submission calls can eat 99.6% of wall time. GPU Direct Storage bottlenecks are often control-path and ordering semantics, not just DMA bandwidth.
