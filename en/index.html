1. How Language Models Learn Taste: Brand Preference in LLMs

Ask a language model to name a running-shoe brand and it often says Nike; ask it to recommend one and it often says Brooks. The authors confirm this across 102 models and 42 categories: in 26 categories the recommended brand differs from the named one, with named brands mostly category leaders and recommended brands mostly niche ones. Taking apart three open training pipelines shows that pretraining already links recommendation sentences to certain brands, SFT mainly teaches the model how to answer, and DPO is what actually moves the pick, even though the preference data contains almost no brand comparisons. Which brand a model recommends is shaped mainly in preference optimization, not just by which brands it knows.

2. A Cue's Hidden Price: Auditing Seller Influence on AI Shoppers

When a language model shops for someone, seller cues such as bestseller badges, expert endorsements or low-stock warnings change which product it picks. This influence used to be measured by choice rate, but a strong cue pushes the choice rate close to 100 percent, after which strong and stronger cues look the same. The authors propose price-equivalent auditing: the model chooses between two products, each pair is shown in both orders to cancel position effects, and the price of the cued product is raised until it is picked only half the time. That premium is what the cue is worth. Across 7 models, cues with similar choice rates can differ several-fold once priced. Only by converting a seller cue's effect into money can different cues be compared

LEAD STORY · LinkedIn

1. PAIR: Preference-Aligned Intent Retrieval for Premium People Recommendation at LinkedIn

LinkedIn

When LinkedIn recommends people for Premium members to browse, whom a member has already been shown is itself a preference signal, but neither the general member embedding nor an off-the-shelf LLM embedding has learned it. PAIR treats people who were shown and opened as positives and people who were shown but skipped as negatives, and trains a small 0.6B model contrastively so that exposure enters the training objective directly. Offline MAR@100 (how many opened people land in the model's top 100, averaged over users) rises from 0.288 with the general member embedding to 0.450, and online click-through also rises, with no notable latency change. Once the objective knows who was shown but skipped, a small model beats a general member embedding

2. Ranking Bandits for Carousel Interfaces with Observable Browsing Depth

In carousels and feeds, users usually stop at some depth, and the positions below it are never seen. The classic position-based model (PBM) still treats those positions as seen but not clicked, and counts them as negative feedback. This paper assumes browsing depth can be observed and builds a version of each of three common multi-armed bandit strategies, including upper confidence bound (UCB) and Thompson sampling (TS). All of them update their statistics only with positions the user actually saw, and the paper proves that the regret of the UCB version grows logarithmically. In simulations, the TS version matches PBM-based Thompson sampling that is told how likely each position is to be seen, and all three new methods beat PBM-based UCB. If you can see how far a user browsed, unseen positions should not count as negative feedback

LEAD STORY · Adobe

1. When Agentic Retrieval Fails Silently: Framework and Production Evidence from a Creative AI Assistant

Adobe

Retrieval metrics such as CTR and MRR assume users see and click the ranked results, but in an agent assistant the generator reads the results first and users see the generated answer. Adobe points out that attribution then breaks in three places, namely who saw the results, when the user acted, and whether the outcome came from retrieval or from the model's own knowledge. They propose LARA, which separately measures whether retrieval ranked well, whether the returned assets were used, and whether the user finished the task. Each retrieval logs an ID and its list of returned assets, and when the user uses an asset, the log records that asset and the retrieval it came from, which links later actions back to that retrieval. In one production assistant, only 0.31% of prompts triggered retrieval. Evaluating agent retrieval can no longer assume users clicked the results; attribution has to be traced layer by layer

2. AutoSim: A Self-Improving User Simulator for Conversational Search Evaluation

Conversational search wants to replace real users with simulated ones for large-scale evaluation, which only works if the simulator's judgments can be trusted. The system rankings AutoSim produces agree closely with NIST's human assessments in three years of TREC iKAT, but it talks very differently from real people. The authors measure improvement against real conversations held out from training. Tuning only the prompt cannot push the AUC of a classifier that tells humans from the simulator below 0.85, while fine-tuning the model weights brings it down to 0.45, where the simulator can no longer be told apart. Sounding human also changes evaluation results, because a prompt-only simulator is too optimistic about cases where real users struggle to find what they need. A simulator must also talk like real users for its conclusions to hold; ranking systems correctly is not enough.

LEAD STORY · Amazon

1. ATTUNE: Agentic Throughput Tuning for LLM Training

Amazon

LLM training throughput depends on a whole recipe of model sharding, memory-compute trade-offs and overlap of communication with compute, and it has to be retuned for every new model, accelerator or framework. Existing auto-tuners search a fixed grid of knobs and only check that the job runs, so they miss gains outside the grid and can silently change training results. Amazon's ATTUNE is a coding agent that edits the training recipe code on its own within a GPU-hour budget, with a correctness check that keeps training semantics unchanged. On H100, H200 and B200 it raises throughput 1.7 times on average. Throughput gains often lie outside the preset knobs, so letting an agent edit the recipe works better than grid search.

2. uGDS: A User-Space Development Kit for High-Performance GPU Direct Storage

NVIDIA's GPU Direct Storage (GDS) moves data from NVMe drives straight to the GPU. But the control operations of every read and write still go through the kernel, requests in one stream must run in strict order, and the user-space engine is closed source and hard to debug. The authors measure that in the worst case 99.6% of total run time is spent waiting for GDS submission calls to return. uGDS is a user-space development kit that moves the whole control path into user space and drops unnecessary strict ordering. On a Samsung 990 PRO drive, its batched read bandwidth reaches up to 12 times that of GDS. The bottleneck of GPU direct storage is often the control path and ordering rules, not data transfer bandwidth
