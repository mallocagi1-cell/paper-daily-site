LEAD STORY · Pinterest

1. PinGER: Token-Efficient Generative Retrieval with Semantic IDs

Pinterest

Generative retrieval encodes each item as a multi-level SID and generates it level by level, but on the input side every history item costs several tokens. During training, Pinterest's PinGER places one dense-vector token after each item's SID and requires that any token reading another item may see only that item's dense token, never its SID. At serving time all SIDs in the history can be dropped, each item costs 1 token, and the output is unchanged. It turns input compression into a structural constraint at training time. Offline it matches TIGER's 1,024-token recall with 170 tokens, and after launch on Related Pins saves rose 0.62%.

2. SLIDE: Search-to-List Item Distillation for Single-Rollout Generative Retrieval

Generative retrieval usually produces candidates online with beam search, and the beams never see each other; writing a whole list in one go by greedy decoding is the most direct alternative but works much worse. Taobao's SLIDE (a training method that distills offline search results into lists) moves search offline: a teacher runs beam search for every training request, concatenates the retrieved items in score order into one long sequence, and a student learns it, decoding greedily once online. It trades online search cost for offline labeling cost. With a 7B teacher, the 0.5B student reaches HR@10 of 0.4578, beating the 0.4498 of beam search at the same size.

1. Unified Heterogeneous Feature Interaction via Effective-Rank Scaling for Recommender Systems

AlibabaRenmin University of ChinaCAS

Most production ranking models squeeze the user's behavior sequence into one fixed vector before it reaches the feature-interaction module, so later interaction layers can no longer see which clicks or add-to-carts actually happened. Alibaba Taobao's HiRank keeps the behavior sequence alive through every layer of the backbone, and builds the retrieval query over long-term history from the user's recent behavior rather than from static profile fields such as age and gender. On Taobao homepage CVR prediction it lifts GAUC from 0.7615 to 0.7801 over the strongest baseline at the same parameter count; in an online A/B test, orders per user rose 2.27% and GMV per user rose 2.86%.

2. Towards a Unified Framework of Optimal Input-Side Dimensional Configuration for Deep CTR Prediction

There are three common ways to trim the input side of a CTR model: drop low-quality training samples, switch off useless features, and prune useless embedding dimensions. Earlier methods do one at a time. This paper from Shenzhen University and collaborators argues that all three are the same operation, multiplying one embedding tensor by a mask at different granularities. They also interact: once a feature is switched off entirely, pruning its dimensions no longer matters. On Avazu with a plain DNN, any single selection raises AUC by only about 0.001. Learning all three together in one model, with penalties that make the levels' decisions fit each other, takes AUC from 0.7893 to 0.7930. Between samples and features, for example, the penalty sums each feature embedding's cosine distance to the sample's average embedding, weighted by the feature gates, and multiplies it by the sample weight. A heavily weighted sample that opens wide gates for outlying features is penalized hard.

1. CA-HNM: Constraint-Aware Hard Negative Mining for Dense Retrieval with Structured Domain Models

Dense retrievers usually take hard negatives from top-ranked documents that are not labeled positive, but ranking high only shows a document resembles the query, not why it should be excluded. CA-HNM checks each candidate against a course knowledge graph, wrong concept, wrong scope or wrong difficulty level, and keeps only those with a clear conflict as negatives. It beats a rank-matched control without the graph in most settings, yet randomly shuffling the graph on one dataset raises NDCG@10 from 0.0311 to 0.1174, so how much the graph helps is still open.

2. Retriever-Free Retrieval: Navigating Documents with Decision Models

Most RAG systems still retrieve with BM25 or vector similarity, and evidence the retriever misses cannot be recovered by any generator. DecisionRAG drops the retriever and has a decision model browse like a person in a library, from topics to pages to passages, judging each level's candidates together in one context. Over 2.68 million Wikipedia passages its Hit@1 is 17.4 points above Qwen3-Embedding-8B retrieval plus a reranker, at about 1.7 seconds per query.

1. HiLO: Towards Product-Level Graceful Degradation with a Quality-Aware, Load-Aware Control Framework

NSDI 2027Microsoft

During traffic spikes, each service degrades on its own, so every service survives while the product's quality can collapse. Microsoft's HiLO moves degradation from the service level to the product level. It predefines a few validated product states. Each state sets a level for every service, such as dropping some features, reducing fan-out or using a cheaper model. At runtime services report load and a central controller picks one state. The unit of degradation should be the product, not the service. It went through 9 real traffic surges. The largest hit 2.38 times baseline peak, and Bing's relevance metric OneDCG, a normalized ranking-quality score, dropped at most 0.425 points, inside the 0.5-point operating limit.

2. Cross-Modal Data Co-Locality for Heterogeneous Storage Systems

A multimodal query first searches text vectors and then fetches the matching image vectors. Both nearest-neighbor searches are fast. The slow part is moving data when the two vectors sit in different storage tiers. CoLoc changes neither the vectors nor the index. It only decides which tier holds what, keeping vectors that are queried together in the same tier. It optimizes where data lives, not where to search. On a four-tier setup, cross-tier queries fell from 73.04% to 0, and end-to-end latency fell 24.8% at a half-multimodal mix, with recall unchanged.

1. Zero-HLM: Generalizable Humanoid Loco-Manipulation without Real Robot Data

Teaching a humanoid a long task such as walking to a table, picking up a banana and putting it in a basket usually takes a hundred or more teleoperated real-robot demonstrations, and moving the table often means collecting them again. Zero-HLM builds all of its data in simulation instead. It scans a real room with a smartphone, rebuilds it as a simulated scene that looks like a photo and supports physical interaction, and rewrites a few teleoperated demonstrations into a thousand or more new ones. Each new demonstration randomly moves the robot's starting point and the positions of the object and placement target, the hand trajectory moves along with the target, and only those that succeed in simulation are kept. A policy trained only on this data and deployed on the real robot averages 66.7% success over three tasks, above the 46.7% from training on real demonstrations. At positions the real demonstrations never covered, success rises from 23% to 69%.

2. PlayPolicy: Dexterity from Supervised Play

To learn to push objects or turn an object in hand to any pose, a dexterous hand usually needs either demonstrations for each task or reinforcement learning in simulation followed by transfer to the real robot. PlayPolicy, from Amazon FAR and collaborators, has a person teleoperate the hand to fiddle freely with objects for a few minutes, and uses behavior cloning to learn a play policy tied to no task. The robot then plays on its own for hours, and this data trains a world model that predicts what actions will do. For a specific goal, the play policy proposes candidate actions, the world model predicts each outcome, and the one closest to the goal is executed. On the real robot its success rate is 69.6% higher than behavior cloning with goals relabeled afterwards, and it matches reinforcement learning with about 490 times less interaction data.
