Embodied AI — Paper Daily, 2026-11-11

Today's embodied track centers on where robot policies inherit their abilities from and how those abilities connect to real control. Kimodo-Policy uses a frozen motion generator as its backbone, SWAM pretrains a world action model on large-scale video and human demonstrations, and DASH takes the slow backbone off the control loop. Another group focuses on data and signals. CTR retimes two-armed demonstrations so policies do not learn operator habits as rules, the tactile paper shows that finger-mounted sensors can stand in for wrist cameras, and AnchorQ refines a frozen policy with a small set of meaningful candidate actions.

4.69

1. Practice Makes Precise: At CoRL, NVIDIA and Partners Reveal How Robots Rehearse Delicate Work in Simulation

Simulation used to be mostly for teaching robots to walk, and the work NVIDIA showed at CoRL pushes it toward fine jobs like aligning gears and connecting cables. AlphaNIST shows the shift best. A single policy learns every task on the NIST assembly board, handling millimeter-scale parts with nothing but a yes-or-no success signal. Its training success tops 98%, against 81% for separate per-task policies, and it uses roughly 39 times less GPU time. The sampling method behind it is plain: practice concentrates on tasks the policy succeeds at about half the time. Training budget should go to what is still unlearned, the same instinct as training on hard samples in recommendation.

2. Learn the Task, Not the Schedule: Reducing Spurious Cross-Arm Coupling in Bimanual Imitation Learning

In two-armed robot demonstrations, which hand moves first is often just the operator's habit, yet imitation learning turns it into a rule, so pausing one arm makes the other wait too.

3. Kimodo-Policy: Learning Kinematic Action Models for Humanoid Loco-Manipulation

A humanoid that walks while picking things up needs whole-body coordination, yet current models mostly inherit semantic or video pretraining and still have to learn how the body moves downstream. This paper changes the pretraining target: it takes a model that generates whole-body motion from text, freezes it as the backbone, and trains only a small branch that feeds in camera images plus the hand control. With the same structure randomly initialized and then trained, success on object-interaction tasks falls from 51.67% to 18.33%. Across eight real-robot tasks it averages 60% success, while its lead on simulation leaderboards is small. With motion itself as the pretraining target, the downstream work is only connecting it to images, much like freezing a pretrained model and training only an adapter layer in recommendation.

4. SimDuoReal: Object-Centric Bimanual Policy for Emergent Dexterity

Getting two dexterous hands to cooperate has usually meant writing a separate reward for each behavior, spelling out which hand moves and when to hand over. This paper uses one reward that only checks whether the object reached its goal pose, and changes the training environments instead, for example by placing the goal in the other hand's workspace so the object must be passed across. Grasping, in-hand adjustment and handover all appear in the trained policies. After three expert policies are distilled into one, it succeeds 76.3% of the time on real hardware across four tasks with unseen objects and no task-specific fine-tuning. In simulated barcode scanning it reaches 85.0%, while two single-arm policies without handover reach 60.4%. The engineering effort moves from writing rewards to designing the training distribution, the same trade-off recommendation engineers face when tuning sample distributions.

5. Tactile Sensors Are an Effective Substitute for Wrist Cameras in Contact-Rich or Occluded Manipulation

Wrist cameras are almost standard on robot-arm policies, yet once the fingers close, the actual contact is hidden from them too. The authors attach two low-cost tactile arrays inside the gripper and feed the readings as two extra images into the same ACT policy, with demonstrations and training settings unchanged. On retrieving an item from an opaque bag, inserting a plug and wiping a whiteboard, the policy with touch and no wrist camera beats the vision-only policy with a wrist camera every time, significantly so on two tasks, with 81.7% against 50.0% on retrieval. Adding both does not reliably help. Signals worth adding are the ones that do not overlap existing inputs, which also holds when adding features to a ranking model.

6. Compass 1: Towards Open-World and Persistent Embodied Navigation from Monocular RGB Videos

Navigation models usually can use only the last few frames, so returning to a target that went out of sight long ago is close to impossible. Compass 1 draws its past trajectory as a 2D map labeled with keyframe indices and headings, and feeds it with sparse keyframes as ordinary images, leaving the model architecture unchanged. The map alone does nothing because the model ignores it, so training adds question-answering supervision on which frame shows the target and which direction to go now. Overall real-world success is 79.4%, against 17.8% for the strongest baseline. On returning to an out-of-sight target every baseline fails, while Compass 1 reaches 58%. History has to be compressed, and supervision has to force the model to use it, which is also the sticking point in long-term behavior sequence modeling.

7. Gemini Robotics 2.5 accelerates real-world general dexterity

DeepMind has released Gemini Robotics 2.5 and calls it its most capable vision-language-action model, a kind of model that takes in images and text instructions and outputs robot actions directly. The upgrade is pitched on dexterity, reliability and safety, and the title stresses general dexterity in the real world. Putting reliability and safety among the headline claims reflects the same concern as not judging a production system by offline metrics alone.

8. SWAM: Scaling World Action Models with Cross-Modal Alignment

World action models predict future video and robot actions together. Existing work often assumes the text-to-video alignment from video pretraining simply survives. Using about 47,000 hours of general video, egocentric video, handheld-gripper demonstrations and robot data, this paper runs item-by-item controls and finds that text annotation quality directly affects control. With the same training budget, moving subtask descriptions from a short verb-object phrase to a step-by-step account, then adding scene and lighting, lifts success on simulated tasks with changed conditions such as background and lighting from 47.20% to 55.04%. Mixing egocentric video with handheld-gripper demonstrations also beats using either one alone. More detailed text descriptions end up as better actions, so annotation quality deserves a check first in multimodal pretraining.

4.37

9. Decoupled Asynchronous System for Humanoids (DASH): Latency-Agnostic Scaling of Vision-Language-Action Models for Humanoid Manipulation

The backbone of a vision-language-action model is slow and the robot does not stop to wait, so actions are always computed from stale images, and a bigger backbone makes it worse. DASH splits the slow backbone and the fast action head into concurrent processes, so the action head alone sets the control rate. From the smallest backbone to the largest, control holds at 20 Hz, while the synchronous pipeline falls from 9.7 Hz to 4.1 Hz. Splitting alone is not enough, since an asynchronous version trained on aligned data does no better than synchronous inference, and success only passes it once training deliberately pairs stale semantic features with fresh images. The time offset in training samples has to match deployment, just as training and serving feature freshness must match in recommendation.

4.20

10. Sample-Efficient Reinforcement Learning for Policy Improvement via Flow-Reversed Action Anchors

To make a frozen general-purpose robot policy better at a specific task, a common approach uses reinforcement learning to search the initial noise it generates actions from, but directions in noise space map to behaviors with no clear pattern, so exploration is inefficient. AnchorQ first reverses coarse actions, such as translating in six directions, back into noise, which gives a small set of candidates the paper calls anchors. A value network picks among them and samples refinements nearby. With the same 500,000 interaction steps, one set of simulated tasks reaches 95.5% success, against 48.3% for DSRL, which searches the noise directly. Mark out meaningful candidates first and then refine locally, which reads just like retrieval followed by ranking in search.
