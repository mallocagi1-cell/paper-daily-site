5. Slim Foundation Models for Latency-Critical Recommendation Serving

Putting an LLM in the online CTR path is too expensive, so existing methods mostly compute LLM outputs offline and store them, and must regenerate them whenever the item or feature catalog changes. SlimFM tries to run the LLM directly online, first applying structured pruning to LLaMA3 to remove 44% of the parameters. LoRA fine-tuning then makes the model produce one embedding per feature field, such as MovieID, and similarity between fields reweights the feature interactions of the CTR model. On FM, the pruned model keeps 99.77% of the full model's mean AUC, and inference is up to 3.4 times faster than with existing LLM-enhanced methods. It holds no clear accuracy lead, reaching the highest AUC among the LLM-enhanced methods in fewer than half of the model and dataset combinations, so its main value lies in latency.

6. CHO-Bench: How Do Context Hints Affect Ad Rankings in LLM Advertising?

ChatGPT Ads lets advertisers write natural-language hints about what a product is, who it suits, and when it helps, and these hints feed ad ranking separately from the ad card shown to users. How should such hints be written? The authors split them into seven styles from marketing frameworks, add one that mixes all seven, and run over 1.19 million paired comparisons with and without hints. Adding hints moves an ad up by 0.56 to 1.16 positions on average, but the seven styles differ little and the mix has no consistent advantage. Measured by rank gain, the difference shows up in the ranker: embedding rankers favor hints about usage situations, while LLM rankers favor hints about target audiences. Adding more hints generally improves rank further, with diminishing marginal gains. In these experiments only the evaluated ad receives hints and the rankers are offline, so the effect when competitors also write hints remains a separate question.

7. Not Yet in the Profile: Uncertainty-Aware Dynamic User Profiling by Learning from Future Interactions

When an LLM maintains a user profile, new behavior often has several readings: a fantasy fan rating a fantasy drawing guide 3 out of 5 may be tiring of fantasy, or may simply dislike drawing guides. Existing methods must either write it into the profile at once or discard it, and a wrong entry keeps misleading later predictions. UAPM adds two stores outside the profile that the downstream model cannot read, one holding unresolved readings with their evidence and one recording what evidence this user needs before a conclusion. In training, a Teacher that sees the user's future behavior guides each decision of a Student, and at deployment the Student uses only current information. Under the same profile length, it beats the best baseline by up to 5%, and removing either store worsens almost every metric. Held entries rarely move straight into the profile and mainly serve as evidence for later profile edits.

8. Your Algorithm: Giving People More Ways to Personalize Facebook Recommendations

Facebook recommendations have long learned from implicit behavior, which adapts slowly and captures little nuance. Your Algorithm lets people write in their own words what they want and do not want to see, and the hard part is making those words actually change recommendations. Each wanted interest gets four dedicated retrieval sources, and without dedicated retrieval, content for a new interest might never enter ranking. Candidates that pass a relevance check get a boost in late-stage ranking, and reranking then controls spacing and a daily cap so one interest does not flood the feed. Unwanted interests are blocked in layers: embedding comparison filters during ranking, a fine-tuned LLM reviews the final list, and the device drops matching cached content. The team found that a small model fine-tuned to return only the items to remove was faster and more reliable than a general-purpose model judging each item.

9. An Adaptive, Context-Aware, and Stacked Attention Network-Based Recommendation System to Capture Users&#39; Temporal Preference

User tastes change over time, and sequence models tend to focus on the latest clicks and lose long-term interests. This paper, published in TKDE in 2023, handles this with two attention modules. The first uses user embeddings to weight the items in a recent time window and summarizes them into the current context. The second projects user embeddings into several spaces, each representing one aspect of taste, and the current context sets the weight of each aspect. The paper reports a 3.25% gain in HR@10 over the strongest baseline, but the gain is uneven, and on Pinterest it beats SASRec by only 0.32%. Ablations show the structure must be tuned per dataset: stacking more layers has little effect, and adding positional encoding is slightly worse on Pinterest. Evaluation ranks each test item only against randomly sampled negatives, which is worth remembering when reading the numbers.

3.15

10. UCB-min: Combining Bandits Under Variance Heterogeneity (Extended Version)

When multi-objective recommendation weights metrics such as relevance and disagreement into one reward, variance can differ widely across candidates, so whether variance-independent exploration like UCB1 or variance-aware exploration like UCB-V wins depends on the data. UCB-min is simple: in every round it computes both exploration bonuses for each candidate and keeps the smaller one. On MovieLens group recommendation, variance is large, UCB-V2 over-explores and ends with cumulative regret of 406, while UCB-min follows UCB1 and stops at 233. On low-variance Steam, it instead keeps up with UCB-V2. It needs no prior knowledge of the variance regime and no reward normalization, only one extra bonus. Note that UCB-minV2, the variant used in most experiments, currently has no theoretical guarantee, and UCB1-Tuned reaches lower regret on Steam.
