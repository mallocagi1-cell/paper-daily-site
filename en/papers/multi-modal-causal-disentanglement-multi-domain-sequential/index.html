Train the domains together, and a sparse domain can end up worse than going it alone

A single listening session often spans a song, a saved playlist, and a podcast episode. Training one shared model across all three domains is supposed to let them pool signal. In production logs, though, the volume skew is steep. On NetEase Cloud Music, roughly 3.56 million users who are active in all three domains generate 297.2 million song interactions and 166.4 million playlist interactions, against just 75.5 million podcast interactions. With a skew that sharp, gradients on shared parameters get dominated by the high-frequency song domain, and the sparse domain takes the hit.

The offline benchmark makes that failure mode concrete. Across nine baselines on this industrial dataset, AutoCDSR and CGRec manage only 0.1539 and 0.1536 Hit@5 on podcasts—well behind the strongest single-domain model, Mamba4Rec, at 0.1887. Joint multi-domain training does not guarantee a free lunch. Leave negative transfer unchecked, and a sparse domain can end up worse off than a model trained in isolation.

To address this, researchers from Hangzhou Dianzi University and NetEase Cloud Music introduce CAMAR, a multi-domain audio recommendation framework. The core intuition is clean: user behavior reflects two preferences operating at the same time. One is a fairly stable cross-domain taste—say, "healing" or "company on the commute." The other is specific to each format, like timbre and rhythm in songs or narrative pacing in podcasts. CAMAR accordingly splits each domain's user representation into an invariant component and a specific component, then applies three constraints designed to curb negative transfer: keeping the two components orthogonal, training the invariant component with a domain-adversarial objective, and allowing cross-domain interaction only inside the invariant subspace. Alongside that, it quantizes text, cover art, and audio into trainable Semantic IDs to link cross-domain items that rarely co-occur in user logs but sit close in content semantics. When a user has no history in a given domain, a completion module called TransNet fills in the missing representation.

Offline, CAMAR leads on every target domain and every metric across both the industrial logs and Amazon Reviews, with the widest margin on the sparse domain. On the industrial dataset, podcast Hit@5 climbs from 0.2022 for the best baseline, MOSAIC—which already uses orthogonality and adversarial constraints—to 0.2622, a 29.7% relative lift. In May 2026, the team also ran a two-week online A/B test on NetEase Cloud Music. Control was the existing per-domain retrieval stack; treatment left ranking and reranking untouched and added one extra CAMAR retrieval channel. Song play duration went up 1.0%, podcast play duration went up 1.6%, and playlist like rate—the share of impressions subsequently added to favorites—went up 3.0%. The paper reports all three gains as statistically significant. The online numbers come from a comparison that adds an extra retrieval channel; they are not from replacing the existing per-domain retrieval with CAMAR.

Negative transfer is pinned down to three harmful paths

Before you can stop a high-volume domain from distorting shared parameters, you need to trace where negative transfer enters the model. CAMAR frames preference generation with a structural causal graph. The solid edges mark paths we want to keep: domain attributes shape a domain's specific preference, which feeds into that domain's retrieval score, while a shared invariant preference influences scores across all domains. The dashed edges mark the three interference paths the method aims to cut.

Source: paper Figure 2. Each dashed arrow represents a distinct leak: blue Path 1 is invariant preference bleeding into a domain's residual; orange Path 2 is domain attributes leaking back into the invariant representation; green Path 3 is one domain's specific signal spilling into another domain's scoring.

To sever Path 1, CAMAR splits first and enforces orthogonality. Each domain's user representation passes through two single-layer linear projections to produce an invariant vector z_inv and a specific vector z_spec, and the loss penalizes their squared inner product:

L_ortho = average_d E[ <z_inv, z_spec>² ]

The two branches do not differ in structure; they differ in training objective. Only z_inv receives the adversarial loss, pushing it toward a subspace where domain identity cannot be recovered. Meanwhile, z_spec skips cross-domain interaction altogether and absorbs whatever is left for that domain. The orthogonality penalty pushes the two branches toward a zero inner product. The authors intend z_inv to carry reusable cross-domain factors like topic, mood, and style, and z_spec to carry domain-bound residuals such as timbre composition or narrative pacing.

Path 2 is handled via domain-adversarial training. Left alone, domain attributes leak into z_inv and skew it toward high-traffic domains. Before z_inv enters cross-domain self-attention, it passes through a gradient reversal layer (GRL) into a shared domain classifier. The classifier tries to predict which domain the vector came from, while the encoder is trained to foil it, scrubbing identifiable domain cues out of z_inv. During training, the reversal coefficient λ ramps linearly from 0 to 1. At equilibrium, the distribution of z_inv should be roughly the same under each domain's condition.

Path 3 gets a simpler architectural wall: z_spec never leaves its home branch, and only z_inv enters the shared self-attention layer. Bidirectional attention runs over the active domains and averages their outputs into a shared cross-domain preference z_share. One subtle design detail matters here: if TransNet had to impute z_inv for a domain where the user has no history, that imputed vector is excluded from the average so synthetic representations cannot contaminate z_share. A learned gate then blends z_share with the domain's own z_spec to produce the scoring vector:

r = ℓ2_normalize( g ⊙ z_share + (1−g) ⊙ z_spec )

g = sigmoid( W [z_share ; z_spec] + b )

Retrieval scoring is a dot product, s(i) = r · e_i, trained with sampled-softmax. The total objective sums the retrieval loss, TransNet supervision, and the two causal regularizers. Loss weights α, β, and γ are tuned on the validation set within [0, 1]. Training runs in three stages: optimize retrieval alone first, add orthogonality second, and turn on the adversarial loss and TransNet's task-level supervision last. At serving time, the domain classifier and both causal losses are discarded, leaving only TransNet active to fill in missing domains.

Put in concrete listening terms, the separation is easy to picture. Suppose a user frequently plays mellow tracks, saves a "bedtime light music" playlist, and occasionally tunes into emotion-focused podcasts. The underlying "healing, sleep-aid" taste carries across all three and belongs in z_inv. Song timbre and rhythm, or podcast narrative pacing, belong strictly in each domain's z_spec. Leave Path 3 open, and song timbre preferences bleed straight into podcast scoring. Leave Path 2 open, and domain attributes pull z_inv toward the high-traffic song distribution, dragging podcast quality down with it.

Items that don't line up in behavior are aligned with Semantic IDs

Blocking negative transfer only solves half the problem—you still have to align items across domains. Most multi-domain recommenders lean on collaborative signal from item IDs, which quietly assumes plenty of users overlap across domains. The paper points out that multi-domain active users make up only a small fraction of a real platform, though it does not report an exact share. When cross-domain ID co-occurrence is thin, collaborative signals simply fail to connect items that share semantics but rarely appear in the same user's history.

CAMAR bridges that gap by discretizing pretrained multimodal features into Semantic IDs. Titles, descriptions, and tags are encoded with Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B; cover art or posters go through a Vision Transformer; song and podcast clips go through the public audio model MERT. The three vectors are concatenated and fed into an RQ-VAE trained jointly over items from all domains, producing offline Semantic IDs of length 3 with 256 codewords per layer. The recommender reads only these discrete codes through a shared, trainable cross-domain codebook, concatenates them with each item's in-domain ID embedding, and fuses the pair via a domain-specific FFN. Putting all domains onto one shared codebook means items with similar topic, mood, or style land in the same semantic space right at the input layer.

On the public Amazon Reviews benchmark, only item text is used—no vision or audio—mainly to check whether the framework still works outside an audio setting.

The other practical headache is missing domain history. If a user has zero interactions in a domain, that domain's sequence encoder has nothing to encode. TransNet addresses this with a set of completion networks indexed by ordered domain pairs, predicting a missing domain's representation from whichever domains are available. If domain d already has history, the model uses that domain's own h_u^d; if it is missing, the predictions hat h_u^{d_s → d} from all active source domains d_s ∈ D_u^+ are combined by arithmetic mean, (1/|D_u^+|) ∑_{d_s ∈ D_u^+} hat h_u^{d_s → d}. Each ordered domain pair still has its own completion network. To stop completion gradients from distorting already-learned source encoders, stop-gradient is placed on both ends—the source-domain input and the target-domain alignment target. Worth noting: the main industrial dataset filters to users active in all three domains, so inference for Table II never actually triggers this completion path. To evaluate completion cleanly, the authors run a dedicated cross-domain cold-start test: the test set is divided evenly into three folds, each fold picks one target domain, wipes out all user history in that domain, and ranks across the full target item catalog using only the other two domains. In Figure 3, CAMAR sits above all compared multi-domain baselines across all three target domains, with the widest visual gap on podcasts.

The offline contrast is sharpest on the sparse domain, and dropping multimodal hurts most

The main offline benchmark covers users active across all three domains under a 19:1 train/test split. For each domain, the earliest interaction after the history cutoff serves as the retrieval target, evaluated with Hit@5/10 and NDCG@5/10. Interaction definitions vary by domain: likes for songs; likes, completions, and favorites for playlists; playback and favorites for podcasts. Catalog scale and history length also diverge sharply—podcasts have 480,000 items and an average sequence length of 21.2, compared with 3.39 million items and an average length of 83.4 for songs. That much smaller candidate pool and shorter sequence length partly explain why raw Hit numbers on podcasts look much higher than on songs.

Look at how the relative lifts scale with sparsity: +5.9% over CGRec on songs, +11.5% over SyNCRec on playlists, and +29.7% over MOSAIC on podcasts. Against MOSAIC on podcasts, Hit@10, NDCG@5, and NDCG@10 also post relative lifts of 26.9%, 29.2%, and 27.9%. Meanwhile, AutoCDSR and CGRec actually trail the single-domain Mamba4Rec baseline on podcasts—confirming that negative transfer shows up right in the data, not just as a post-hoc story.

On Amazon Reviews (Books, Kindle_Store, and Movies&TV), the pattern holds at a smaller scale. On the sparsest category, Movies&TV, Hit@5 improves by 10.7% over the strongest baseline (from 0.0224 to 0.0248), whereas Books moves by only 3.4%.

Ablations on the industrial dataset peel off one piece at a time. Dropping either orthogonality or the adversarial loss degrades all three domains; dropping both causal constraints pulls podcast Hit@5 down from 0.2622 to 0.2281. The steepest drop, however, comes from stripping out all multimodal features and Semantic IDs so the model relies solely on in-domain ID embeddings: podcast Hit@5 falls to 0.2063—barely above MOSAIC's 0.2022—and song Hit@5 slides to 0.0331, below CGRec's 0.0355. For a sparse domain whose content spans very different formats, multimodal semantic alignment does the heaviest lifting. There is no "remove TransNet" row in the ablation, which makes sense given that the main test set only contains users active in all three domains.

The paper reports this configuration: the hidden dimension and both embedding types are 64; each per-domain Transformer has 2 layers and 1 head, and the shared invariant Transformer has a single layer. Sequences are capped at length 100 within a domain and 200 for the cross-domain mix, with 4,095 negatives sampled per user. Training uses Adam at an initial learning rate of 1×10⁻³ on an NVIDIA A100.

Online, it is an extra channel, and all three domains' main scenarios see a lift

The May 2026 online A/B tests ran on the main-traffic scenarios of each domain on NetEase Cloud Music, which serves song, playlist, and podcast recommendations to tens of millions of users daily. Again, treatment did not swap out the existing per-domain retrieval stack. That stack remained the control, ranking and reranking stayed fixed, and CAMAR was wired in as an additional retrieval channel. Core metrics matched each scenario's primary objective: average play duration per session for songs and podcasts, and like rate—the share of impressions added to favorites—for playlists.

DomainMetricLift vs. control
SongPlay duration+1.0%
PlaylistLike rate+3.0%
PodcastPlay duration+1.6%

The paper reports all three lifts as statistically significant. On play duration, the podcast lift (+1.6%) is larger than the song lift (+1.0%); the playlist result is a like-rate lift of +3.0% and is not comparable in magnitude to the play-duration figures. The offline industrial benchmark was restricted to users active in all three domains. Offline song labels were also built from likes, whereas the online song metric is play duration. Given those differences, offline relative gains should not be read as one-to-one predictors of online business impact.
