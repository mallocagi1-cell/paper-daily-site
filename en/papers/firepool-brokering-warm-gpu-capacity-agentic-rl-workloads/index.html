Firepool: Brokering Warm GPU Capacity for Agentic and RL Workloads at LinkedIn

In the Firepool paper, what makes GPU jobs lasting a few seconds run fast is keeping the cold start off the critical path, more than making the cold start itself faster.

Start with a two-agent tool loop that LinkedIn measured: an H200 node, a cap of two GPUs, and steps that each run for only a second or two. When every job gets a fresh session tag, one repeat takes 185 s. When each agent reuses its own warm pod, the same repeat finishes in 53 s, and the medians over 10 paired repeats are 186.4 s and 51.8 s.

In the top panel the dark gray running segments are only thin slivers, and almost all the time goes to waiting. Each agent has an orange wait of more than a minute, and its later steps get held up behind it.

In production, over a sampled 14-hour peak period, 94.7% of 3,206 pod acquisitions were served from warm or standby capacity. Getting there requires answering three questions at once: can a warm pod serve the next job, how far can state travel once it does, and can we afford to keep it warm.

With the image cached, twenty-odd seconds remain

On an H200 node with the 12.2 GB image already cached, a cold start still takes 22.1 s at the median and 34.7 s at p90, which shows that a long chain of steps exists beyond pulling the image. The largest piece is the platform init containers at 14.0 s, including 5.0 s to fetch credentials, and caching images cannot save this time. When 8 pods start at once on one node, create-to-Ready doubles from about 20 s to about 48 s. When the image is not on the node, the fleet-wide average is 1.3–7.5 minutes.

Existing fixes each cover one part. Caching images on nodes saves only the network pull, and with a 10–30 GB image per experiment, a node's disk holds only a few. Persistent workers such as Ray or a static pool remove the wait but hold GPUs through think-time pauses and off-peak hours. Kueue and Volcano manage queueing and quotas but do not keep reusable warm pods across jobs.

So three goals pull against each other: fast starts, few idle GPUs, and state isolation between jobs. The first thing to solve among them is usability: however many warm pods exist, they are wasted if the next job cannot use them.

For a warm pod to serve the next job, code and topology must bind late

If every image, dependency set or topology needed its own pool, warm capacity would splinter. Firepool's late binding pushes everything it can to job start: each hardware pool (SKU) uses just one shared base image holding the heavy, slow-changing layers. User code is a content-addressed zip unpacked at job start; extra Python dependencies are installed the first time the caller uses that pod, and are kept. Hoot says▾

The topology of a multi-pod job is likewise deferred to job start: the scheduler claims N generic pods in one step, injects the torchrun rendezvous and rank variables, and resets the pods back to the pool when the job ends. The dividing line is easy to remember: native builds and framework versions go in the image, while prompts, rewards, verifiers and experiment logic go in the code package, where an edit takes effect on the next job.

Late binding has two costs. Delivering the code package, unpacking it and starting the job takes 0.40–0.54 s at the median, with p99 at most 1.45 s on H200. A caller's first dependency install on a pod costs a further 0.11–4.3 s, and because dependencies are kept, later jobs pay 0 when they do not change. What it buys is warm capacity that does not fragment: in the simulator with 64 environments, one pool per environment that may grow to the budget holds 2.15 times the capacity of an always-on pool, while a shared pool needs only 1.06 times. Splitting the budget across environments instead pushes p90 start latency to 400 s.

Once a pod can serve the next job, state propagation must be limited

With pods interchangeable, reuse can carry files, shared memory or credentials to the next job. The first rule is narrow: in production, a used pod is reused only for the caller that used it last, and by default never across callers. After a job ends, the pod also stays reserved for that caller for an owner hold, so the next step of the loop gets it back first.

The second rule governs session boundaries. A caller marks a session with a reuse tag, and the first job of a new session may run once on the old pod to avoid waiting, after which the old pod is retired. So state from one session reaches at most one job of the same caller's next session, and scenarios that need kernel-level isolation should add a microVM.

Between jobs, the in-pod agent kills the entire process tree and verifies against /proc, then empties the code directory and /dev/shm, and if it cannot confirm this the pod is deleted. This step is cheap, with a reset taking only 9.3 ms. What is expensive is the boundary policy: when the tag is treated as a retiring boundary, only about half of multi-step jobs stay warm.

The gap left by isolation goes to a standby on the same card

Retiring the old pod leaves a gap. When the next job comes from another caller or crosses a reuse tag, the old pod must be held or retired, and on a capacity-capped pool the new job waits 22–200 s.

Standby overlaps the preparation of the next pod with the current job. One physical GPU carries 1 active pod and up to 3 standbys, with HAMi plus Volcano allocating fractional shares. The share is only a scheduler slot: a standby runs nothing but the idle pod agent, creates no CUDA context and takes no GPU memory. When a job runs, the agent raises the memory limit to the whole card in the job process's environment, so the job can still use all 141 GB of the H200.

At handover, a per-GPU gate ensures at most one pod runs a job at a time. Once the active pod's job processes are confirmed gone, the gate promotes the ready standby, and the controller later fills the open slot with a new one. The group counts as just one physical GPU in the budget.

The benchmark has one caller run 30 sequential jobs, each with a fresh tag, over 10 paired repeats. Standby served 86% of handovers and the median handover wait fell from 51.0 s to 0.94 s, which shows most boundary-crossing jobs no longer wait for a new pod. The loop therefore fell from 1,023 s to 337 s, and standby won all 10 pairs. Its limits are that it refills at pod-creation speed and that multi-GPU groups still need group-wide promotion.

The bill for warmth is kept by queue and by physical GPU

Warm pods hold GPU time, so every pool has a budget. Scaling follows the queue, and the authors' reasoning is that warm pods are idle by design, so scaling on utilization would shrink the pool just when it is needed most. Two accounting details matter: starting pods count as supply, otherwise each pass would create another wave; owner-held pods count toward the floor and ceiling but are not supply for other callers.

Operators have four knobs: floor, idle cap, ceiling and keep-alive τ. Floor and idle cap at 0 with τ is serverless TTL (reclaim after an idle timeout), while floor at peak with τ infinite is a static cluster, so common policies all become points in the same space.

So how long should τ be? Simulating a 22 s pod start, a 60–120 s keep-alive matched to the loop's pauses holds 0.48–0.68 times the capacity of an always-on pool and adds only 0.01–0.27 s at the median, which means saving about 30–50% of capacity with start latency almost unchanged. A long 900 s keep-alive actually holds 1.15 times, as measured. On H200, warm reuse takes 15% more GPU time per job (12.3 vs 10.7 GPU-seconds), in exchange for a loop that is 3.6 times faster.

How much faster depends on the baseline

The paper's multiples come from three baselines, and they mean very different things:

New pod per jobH200, two-GPU cap, W-Agent119.3 s vs 1,554.2 s13.0×

Standby off, fresh tag per jobH200, 30 sequential jobs337 s vs 1,023 s3.0×

The first two rows have the most extreme conditions: short fixed-duration jobs, a median queue of about 90 s with a new pod per job, and multi-step agent loops taking 22–32 minutes, against 0.6–2.5 minutes with reuse.

The third row says more about reuse itself, because standby stays on there. Standby and recycled pods served 137 of 190 jobs, median start is 1.91 s vs 2.29 s, and the CI of the paired difference spans 0. The gap is entirely in the tail: p90 falls from 59.1 s to 3.52 s, and the 20 jobs that waited for a new pod had a median of 82 s. On CPU, which has no standby, removing reuse makes RL 4.5 times slower and agents 2.2 times slower.

As jobs get longer, the gain narrows. In the CPU-calibrated simulator, the loop speedup falls below 1.1 times beyond about 200 s of job body for RL and about 100 s for agents, and it vanishes when think time exceeds the keep-alive. Jobs that bring their own image do not benefit either, and they dominate the 8-GPU pools.

In production data, once a job has a warm or standby pod, its code starts in 0.3–0.5 s at the median and at most 0.7 s at p90, which shows the pod step no longer costs time. Yet during a burst of about 2,500 jobs, warm-reuse jobs still waited a median of 53 s from submission to start, and this number matches the 53 s of the opening loop only by coincidence.

During a burst jobs still wait, but what they wait for is now quota.

The authors' explanation is that callers had used up their own GPU quota and queued behind their own jobs. This also answers the three opening questions: once pods can serve the next job, state propagation is limited and warmth is affordable, cold starts still exist, but about 95% of acquisitions never meet one, and the queueing constraint has moved to quota.
