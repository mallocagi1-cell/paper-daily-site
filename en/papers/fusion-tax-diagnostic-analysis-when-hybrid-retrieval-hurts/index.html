The Fusion Tax: A Diagnostic Analysis of When Hybrid Retrieval Hurts

Aaditya Chauhan, Arun Agarwal
30 minread4.75scoreNo. 110-24 AI Search issue
Original paper ↗
SHARE

Hybrid retrieval fuses the results of term-matching sparse retrieval such as BM25 with those of dense vector retrieval into a single ranking. The documentation of Elasticsearch, Azure AI Search and LangChain all list reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) as the recommended or only fusion method, on the basis of gains in average metrics. This paper keeps the books differently: it compares nDCG@10 before and after fusion query by query, and treats "no fusion" as a legitimate option. The authors test on 7 BEIR datasets with standard Anserini BM25 and the BGE-small dense model. The best fusion method is score interpolation with a 0.7 weight on the dense score, which beats dense-only retrieval in mean nDCG@10 on every dataset, by 0.008 to 0.051; yet in the same setting 8–21% of queries rank worse than with dense-only retrieval, and 13–48% rank worse than the better of the two retrievers on that query. The authors call this per-query loss the "fusion tax". The losses concentrate on queries where only one retriever finds a relevant document, and the other retriever then contributes only noise. Switching to a larger dense encoder, replacing BM25 with SPLADE, or adding a cross-encoder reranker after fusion does not make this tax disappear.

1. The average went up, so why look query by query

A higher mean nDCG@10 only says that more queries were helped than hurt; it does not say that no query was hurt. For a RAG system the difference has practical consequences: if fusion pushes a query's relevant document out of the top 10, the context given to the generator no longer contains the answer, and the answer quality for that query drops directly, while the average does not show it.

The authors therefore keep two accounts for every query. The first uses the "per-query best" as reference: whichever of BM25 and dense retrieval does better on a given query is compared with the fused result. This reference is only known after the fact and measures whether fusion drags down the better retriever. The second uses "always dense-only" as reference, an alternative that can actually be deployed, and answers whether adding BM25 is worth it at all.

2. The accounting: one formula, four quadrants

The fusion tax of a single query is defined as the nDCG@10 of the better of the two retrievers minus the nDCG@10 after fusion:
τ(q)=max(NDCGs​(q),NDCGd​(q))−NDCGf​(q)
Here s is sparse retrieval (BM25), d is dense retrieval and f is the fused result. τ(q)>0 means fusion is worse than the better retriever on that query. The share of queries with τ>0 is called the "oracle tax"; the share of queries where fusion falls below dense-only retrieval is called the "deployable tax".

Three fusion methods are compared. RRF sums the reciprocals of the ranks, with the constant set to 60. Score interpolation first min-max normalizes the scores of each retriever's top 100 (best score 1, worst 0), then adds them as sf​=αs~d​+(1−α)s~s​ with dense weight α=0.7. The paper does not say how a document found in only one of the two top-100 lists gets its missing score; the usual choice is 0. CombMNZ sums the normalized scores and multiplies by the number of retrievers that returned the document.

To find where the losses come from, the authors split queries into four quadrants by whether each retriever puts at least one relevant document in its top 10: both retrievers hit (Both-Win), only dense retrieval hits (Dense-Only), only BM25 hits (Sparse-Only), and neither hits (Both-Fail).

The sparse side uses BM25 with Anserini default parameters (k1=0.9, b=0.4, title and body indexed as separate fields), which the authors confirm reproduces the published BEIR results to 4 decimal places; this rules out the explanation that "BM25 drags fusion down because it was badly tuned". The main experiments use BGE-small (33M parameters) on the dense side, with MiniLM (22M), BGE-base (110M), BGE-large (335M) and Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B (596M) as alternatives, all searched exactly with FAISS. The 7 datasets are SciFact (scientific claim verification), FiQA (financial QA), NFCorpus (medical nutrition retrieval), TREC-COVID (COVID literature retrieval), ArguAna (finding counter-arguments), SciDocs (citation recommendation) and Quora (duplicate question retrieval), with query counts ranging from 50 for TREC-COVID to 10,000 for Quora.

3. Experiments

3.1 Averages: interpolation does win

Score interpolation beats dense-only retrieval on all 7 datasets. RRF beats dense-only retrieval only on NFCorpus and TREC-COVID, where BM25 and dense retrieval are at similar levels (0.656 vs 0.666 on TREC-COVID); on the other 5 datasets RRF is below dense-only retrieval. RRF looks only at ranks and not at scores, so even when dense retrieval is clearly stronger it treats BM25's ranks as equally trustworthy, which dilutes the dense side's advantage.

Take ArguAna: interpolation makes 19.1% of queries worse, the worsened queries drop by 0.213 on average, almost as much as the 0.221 average gain of the improved queries, and the mean net change is only +0.0077. The small rise in the average is the difference left after large gains and large losses across many queries cancel out.

One might suspect that the 0.7 weight was picked on the test data. The authors re-select the weight with leave-one-dataset-out cross-validation: choose α on the other 6 datasets and apply it to the 7th. The resulting deployable tax is 11–25%, slightly higher than with a fixed 0.7, which shows the tax is not caused by tuning on the test set. The risk-sensitive test TRisk works query by query on the nDCG@10 change of fusion relative to dense-only retrieval: gains count as they are, losses are multiplied by (1+α) (the authors use α=5), and the test asks whether this weighted mean is significantly below zero. By this standard, interpolation is significantly riskier than dense-only retrieval on 6 of the 7 datasets, even though its mean score is higher.

3.3 Which quadrant the losses come from

This experiment checks which kind of query the losses come from. The authors split interpolation's per-query gains and losses by the four quadrants of Section 2 (paper Table VII). Result: Sparse-Only queries make up no more than 7% of the total, but 87–95% of them are hurt by fusion, with mean net losses of 0.14 to 0.37 within the quadrant. The reason is that dense retrieval has no relevant document in its top 10 on these queries, and the 0.7 dense weight pushes down the relevant documents BM25 found. Dense-Only queries are the mirror case: BM25 becomes the noise source and 38–65% are hurt. Among Both-Win queries, 10–48% are reordered, with help and harm nearly cancelling and a quadrant net change between −0.04 and +0.01; because this quadrant holds the most queries, it contributes 38–72% of the worsened queries.

These losses come mainly from rank changes, not from relevant documents going missing. Queries whose top-10 recall actually drops are only 1–7%; in most cases the relevant documents are still in the top 10, just moved down a few places. Across all datasets, only 77 queries have all their relevant documents pushed out of the top ten, so the fusion tax is mainly worse ordering, not worse recall.

3.4 A bigger encoder does not remove the tax

This experiment checks whether the fusion tax exists only because the dense model is weak. The authors step the dense encoder up from 22M to 596M, then redo the experiment with BM25 replaced by the learned sparse retriever SPLADE. Result: with Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B, interpolation still puts 14–43% of queries below the per-query best; with SPLADE it is 14–41% on 6 datasets and 60% on TREC-COVID. A stronger encoder only changes the balance between the two retrievers, and that balance decides which retriever becomes noise on each query, so the share of hurt queries does not decline as the encoder grows.

3.5 Can we tell in advance which queries should not be fused

If this could be predicted, queries could be routed: fuse where fusion helps and use dense-only retrieval elsewhere. This experiment checks whether that is feasible. The authors extract 17 features available at inference time for each query (query length, IDF statistics of its terms, the score gap between the first and second result of each retriever, the overlap of the two top-10 lists, and so on) and train gradient-boosted trees to predict whether fusion will fail. "Failure" means that on this query, fusion's nDCG@10 is more than 0.01 below the better of the two retrievers. Result: AUC is 0.63 within a dataset, falls to 0.59 across datasets, and is as low as 0.49, close to random; 11 of the 17 features flip the direction of their association with failure across datasets. A router built on these features does not beat fixed interpolation on any dataset. A concrete example is the dense side's score lead, the similarity gap between dense retrieval's first and second results: on datasets where dense retrieval is stronger it signals that fusion is safe, but on TREC-COVID it signals that fusion will fail.

3.6 Can reranking repair the damage

This experiment checks whether a cross-encoder reranker after fusion can repair the losses fusion causes. The authors rerank the top 30 with BGE-reranker-v2-m3. After reranking, the reference also becomes reranked dense-only retrieval, and the tax is the share of queries where fusion falls below it. This deployable tax falls by a third only on SciFact, and not at all on FiQA or TREC-COVID, where 16% and 22% of queries still finish below reranked dense-only. The reason is that reranking repairs old losses while creating new ones. Take FiQA: of the 95 queries fusion had hurt, reranking repairs 75, but newly hurts 81, so it gains nothing. After reranking, nDCG@10 barely differs (at most 0.011) whether the candidates come from interpolation or from dense-only retrieval, which means that with a reranker, the fusion step can largely be dropped. The cost is latency: cross-encoder reranking of 30 candidates takes about 1.6 s, while BM25 needs only a few milliseconds per query.

4. Limitations

The authors list five limitations: only retrieval ranking is evaluated, not the final answer generation; only one reranker and one reranking depth are tried; TREC-COVID has only 50 queries, so its percentages fluctuate a lot; latency is measured on one machine only; and the prediction experiment uses only cheap features, without trying LLM judgments.

Two further points deserve attention. First, all 7 datasets are English BEIR datasets whose queries are mostly natural-language questions, and dense retrieval is stronger than BM25 on all 7 of them; in keyword-heavy settings such as e-commerce or code search, Sparse-Only queries may be far more than 7%, and both the quadrant shares and the recommended weight need to be re-measured. Second, Section 3.3 shows that most of the loss is relevant documents moving down within the top 10; if the downstream system feeds all 10 documents into the generator's context, this loss affects the answer much less, which is exactly the gap left by not evaluating generation.

5. Practical implications

First, before adopting hybrid retrieval, measure the nDCG@10 of each retriever on the target corpus. The authors' advice is that if dense retrieval leads by more than 0.04, use dense-leaning score interpolation (dense weight 0.6–0.7); only when the two are close use RRF, CombMNZ or equal-weight interpolation. This call does not need much labeling; 200–400 queries are enough. The weights also carry over to other datasets: a weight chosen on the other 6 datasets scores at most 0.014 nDCG@10 below the weight tuned directly on that dataset (TREC-COVID is the exception, at 0.031), so there is no need to retune for every corpus.

Second, in online evaluation, report "the share of queries worse than dense-only retrieval" and "the mean loss when worse" alongside the average. These two numbers show directly whether a higher average comes from most queries gaining a little, or from some queries gaining a lot and cancelling out large losses on others.
