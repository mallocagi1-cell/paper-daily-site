1. HandUMI: A Modular Hand-Worn Parallel-Jaw Interface for Robot-Free Bimanual Data Capture

MMu RoboticsMcGillMilaTUMUUPC📖 Deep dive →

Bimanual demonstrations usually occupy a robot during collection, and the motions are hard to move to a new body. A hand-worn parallel-jaw interface uses swappable fingertips to match the robot gripper, tracks the hands with headset controllers, and measures aperture with encoders.

2. Continually Evolving Skill Knowledge in Vision Language Action Model

NeurIPS 2026Shanghai Jiao Tong UniversitySShanghai Innovation InstituteUniversity of CambridgeNTUMMIT SMARTAAgiBot

Vision-language-action models can accumulate knowledge in pretraining, but continual learning remains hard. Existing continual imitation methods often add parameters or modules, which does not scale well for large models. A knowledge-driven framework with no parameter growth jointly optimizes task representations and a self-evolving knowledge space, and routes experts using a knowledge posterior over relations and semantic embeddings for task specialization. It has two forms: one that is task-centric, and one that models reusable skills across tasks in a hierarchy. On LIBERO, the method stays strong against vision-language-action and continual-imitation baselines with only 1% replay, and dual-arm real-world experiments validate knowledge transfer across embodiments and scenes. The hierarchical variant is stronger at hierarchical manipulation, and visualizations show knowledge retention and task discovery. For robot models that must keep adding skills, a knowledge space can evolve without growing parameters to remember old tasks.

3. Hidden Variables Behind Real-Robot Data Quality: Lessons from Hundreds of Thousands of Hours

AAgiBot

As real-robot data collection scales, workspace limits, control-interface friction, and latency can write pauses, detours, and corrections into demonstrations that still count as successful. One study looks at these hidden variables across embodiment, teleoperation control, and data consistency, combining a kinematic study of 25 dual-arm robots, controlled teleoperation experiments, and real-robot evaluation of fine-tuned policies. Parallelizing body-part control cuts task completion time by 43% to 53%, and compliant control improves success while reducing peak contact forces on contact-rich tasks. Policies also keep about nine tenths of the extra path length in detour-contaminated demonstrations, so inefficiencies in the collection system are remembered by the model. Task success alone does not reveal demonstration quality. Better real-robot data starts with seeing what the workspace and the interface actually write into the trajectory.

4. From Temperature-Limited to Battery-Limited Legged Locomotion

Woven by Toyota

Sustained walking on legged robots often ends in thermal shutdown of a few joint motors, long before the battery is empty. One method trains thermally aware locomotion policies with reinforcement learning without ever observing temperature, and demonstrates this on a Unitree Go2 quadruped. A lumped thermal network, fitted on runs of a single robot and calibrated to measured electrical power, predicts all twelve motor temperatures with a mean error below 1 degree Celsius across robots, gaits, payloads, and speeds. Under load, a policy trained without thermal awareness hits thermal shutdown in about 19 minutes, while the thermally aware policy drains the battery after 174 minutes, still with headroom at the hottest motor. Sustained walking should reward lower predicted asymptotes of the hottest joints, not only whether the current gait can move.

5. Body Schema Policy: Body as the Vision-Action Interface for Efficient Robot Loco-Manipulation

MBZUAI

Whole-body loco-manipulation can be learned by scale: the strongest policies have billions of parameters and still need task-specific fine-tuning. Humans have limited processing capacity, yet organize what they see and how they move through a body schema. An imitation-learning policy writes such a schema into the vision-action interface. Its latent body schema places joint and link tokens on the kinematic tree, and learned screw motions carry features between links. Each body part then retrieves its own visual evidence to condition the denoising of its actions. With this inductive bias, the policy returns an action chunk faster than any policy compared in the study. A model of about 35 million parameters matches a much larger baseline's success rate with 75 times fewer parameters, and nearly doubles Diffusion Policy's success. On an RTX 6000 Ada, a 16-step chunk takes only 13.9 milliseconds.

6. TCP4D: A Unified Tool-Center-Point Tracking and Generation Model for Robot Manipulation

HKUSTBBITNUSPolyUSun Yat-sen UniversityZhejiang University

Recovering robot actions and gripper states from manipulation videos is still hard, especially after the embodiment changes. A unified model uses an embodiment-agnostic tool-center-point pose as the action space, and a shared four-dimensional scene representation both tracks observed tool-center-point motion and generates future trajectories. Given a monocular video and initial two-dimensional queries, the tracker recovers metric poses and gripper states, along with scene geometry and camera parameters. The generator samples local features from the same representation along the recovered tracks, then combines them with robot observations and language instructions to predict future states. Tracking absolute trajectory error is 7.60 millimeters on DROID and 16.07 millimeters on RoboLab; as a manipulation policy, mean success on RoboTwin is 69.19%. Cross-embodiment augmentation can also turn existing videos into demonstrations for other robots.

7. ALOHA Lightning: Learning Fast and Precise Manipulation

Stanford

Learning from human demonstrations has given robots many manipulation skills, but the learned policies usually run far slower than ordinary people. The gap comes mainly from the lack of an interface for collecting demonstrations at high speed, and from the difficulty of training policies that can execute fast motions robustly. A system for fast, precise manipulation collects near-human-speed trajectories by kinesthetic teaching on a backdrivable bimanual platform, smooths actions at test time, and uses masking to align the visual distribution between collection and deployment. With 50 demonstrations per task, it autonomously completes bimanual tasks such as tossing and catching a ball, folding shorts, inserting a battery, and bussing a table, at over 80% success and at speeds close to a human. Execution time can also be cut by more than half compared with teleoperation. Fast manipulation needs a near-human-speed demonstration interface first, then alignment of actions and vision at deployment.

8. Zero-Shot Compositional Generalization for Embodied Manipulation

Peking UniversityDD-RoboticsUESTCCUHK

Robot demonstrations are collected on different embodiments, so many robot-skill combinations are never observed. Pooling the data shares visual knowledge, but joint labels stay tied to the kinematic chain that produced the demonstration. A geometry-supervised training framework treats a demonstrated command as a tool-space target for every robot that can realize it. Calibrated forward kinematics then supplies this supervision to another robot's native action interface, without joint labels for that skill on the foreign body. Native reconstruction keeps demonstrated configurations, and reachability checks select targets for cross-robot supervision. A visual policy learns to predict shared actions and executes them through the target robot's native decoder. Across three backbones and two simulators, cross-robot supervision raises success on held-out combinations by 9.5 and 8.6 percentage points over source-only supervision.

9. Gemini 4 Hydrogen: a faster model for general purpose robotics

Google

General-purpose robots need spatial and embodied reasoning, but they also need to be fast and inexpensive enough to keep adjusting on a real arm until the job is done. Gemini 4 Hydrogen folds specialized skills from stronger embodied-reasoning models into a faster generalist: given a goal in plain language and a camera view, it plans the task, writes and runs control code, and keeps adjusting until the work is finished. On the Artificial Analysis Robotics 0.1 benchmark, it controls a real six-axis arm through eight everyday tabletop tasks, from pouring a drink to packing blocks into a box, and scores 58, similar to the stronger Argon model at a lower cost per token. A robotics plugin also gathers hardware onboarding, simulation, and control into one workbench, so a developer can go from a robot in a box to a working application in an afternoon. For anyone deploying embodied agents, speed and reasoning no longer have to live in two separate models.

10. Build Training-Ready Physical AI Datasets with Two Prompts in NVIDIA Cosmos

NVIDIA

Training physical AI often stalls on data: curation, augmentation, generation, and labeling live in different tools, the engineering pipeline is long, and a coding agent can rarely orchestrate the whole chain at once. A path on NVIDIA Cosmos folds these steps into a flow that a coding agent can orchestrate, and starts it with two prompts, with the aim of producing training-ready physical AI datasets. The point is not another isolated generative model, but to connect curation, augmentation, generation, and labeling inside one orchestration, shortening the path from raw observation to data a policy can train on. For teams training robots or physical AI, data preparation itself can be agent-driven, rather than beginning as a manual pipeline that only human operators can run. How far two prompts will go still depends on the task and the source data, but the direction is to hand the data factory to an orchestratable agent.
