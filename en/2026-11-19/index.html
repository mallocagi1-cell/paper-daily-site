Generative — Paper Daily, 2026-11-19

Today's generative recommendation track asks whether the objective and the list structure catch up after a model starts generating the next item directly. Reinforcement learning can lift clicks and revenue, but it can also turn the model into a better ranker and a worse retriever, with candidate recall falling on all five datasets studied. Some work replaces a scalar reward with verbalized feedback, some writes query evidence into identifier learning and decoding, and some uses hyperbolic geometry or full-sequence diffusion to settle codebook hierarchy and the entire page of results. Large-model sequential recommenders start to keep long histories and multiple interests together, and raise pass-through rate online; collaborative graph supervision can be written into training without becoming inference cost. A shopping-agent evaluation adds a caution: longer thinking raises stated confidence without necessarily choosing better products.

1. Stable and Granular Policy Optimization for Generative Recommendation

NeurIPS 2026

Generative recommenders decode sequential semantic IDs for items, and because core metrics are scored at the sequence level, reinforcement learning is a natural fit. Off-the-shelf group-based algorithms struggle here: token-level methods become much more unstable, while sequence-level methods weight every token equally and ignore their different roles. A group-based method built for this setting keeps a sequence-level ratio for stability while injecting token-wise advantages. It upweights high-confidence tokens on positive trajectories to reinforce successful retrieval, strictly penalizes them on negative ones to encourage exploration, and uses an adaptive scale to bound the variance of the reshaped advantages. On two public benchmarks and a billion-scale industrial dataset, it consistently beats six strong reinforcement-learning baselines. In an online test on a commercial platform, click-through rate rose 2.36% and advertising revenue rose 1.95%.

2. Better Rankers, Worse Retrievers: The Hidden Cost of RL Post-Training in Generative Recommendation

HKUSTTrip.com

Post-training generative recommenders with reinforcement learning after supervised fine-tuning is now standard, but gains are usually reported on the first ten results, while deployed models often hand hundreds of candidates to a downstream ranker. A systematic measurement shows that reinforcement learning improves accuracy at the very top, yet on all five datasets studied it lowers recall of the candidate list: the model becomes a better ranker and a worse retriever. The cause is that only one next item is logged per user, so the most plausible alternatives in the rollout are treated as wrong and given negative advantages, and items that similar users would choose next drop out of those lists. An out-of-rollout anchoring term that preserves the supervised model's ordering of items outside the rollout keeps the gain at the top and restores recall to the supervised level on all five datasets. On Office, hit@500 falls from 52.84% to 47.69%, which shows how large this hidden cost can be.

3. HyMiRec: A Hybrid Multi-interest Learning Framework for LLM-based Sequential Recommendation

WWW 2026Fudan UniversityXiaohongshuPeking University

When large models are used for sequential recommendation, latency and feature-bandwidth limits often force the system to truncate behavior sequences to recent interactions, dropping long-range preferences; predicting a single embedding also struggles to cover a user's many interests. A hybrid multi-interest framework uses a lightweight recommender to extract coarse interests from long sequences and a large-model recommender to refine them, and a residual codebook based on cosine similarity compresses and reuses history embeddings to cut feature-fetch cost. A disentangled multi-interest module uses multiple queries to learn different facets of intent. On public benchmarks and industrial data it improves over existing large-model methods, with an average gain of 24.53% on PixelRec and 8.9% on MovieLens-1M. In an online test, pass-through rate on image-and-text traffic rose from 26.46% to 30.93%, showing that long sequences and multiple interests can fit an online budget.

4. More Than What Was Chosen: LLM-based Explainable Recommendation Beyond Noisy User Preferences

ICLR 2026SK TelecomKAIST

Recommenders usually assume that observed choices faithfully reflect underlying interests, but real-world choices are often noisy and inconsistent, so even large-model recommenders with reasoning may miss genuine preferences and produce unconvincing rationales. One approach complements revealed preference with a coherent-preference signal that favors items logically and causally consistent with interaction history, and jointly optimizes both in a unified ranking. The influence of each signal is adjusted depending on whether the two agree or conflict, so the model can learn from chosen items without being tied to a single noisy click. On Amazon review data, the method consistently outperforms about 20 strong baselines in both recommendation performance and rationale quality, and it achieved a 1.65 times relative improvement in click-through rate after deployment. For teams that want large models to recommend and explain, clicked items should not be treated as the whole of user interest.

5. IntentGR: Enhancing Intent for End-to-End Personalized Generative Retrieval in Search

WSDM 2027AlibabaZhejiang University

Personalized generative retrieval in search needs to satisfy both the current query intent and longer-term preference. Existing end-to-end methods often treat the query as just another input feature, without using it to guide identifier learning or sequence generation, so the generated semantic IDs drift toward frequent token patterns rather than what the query is looking for. An intent-enhanced framework adds query-related evidence at four stages of the retrieval pipeline: it brings in query-level behavioral evidence as a new input, then applies semantic alignment, long-tail query enrichment, and query-conditioned attention at the optimization, training-distribution, and decoding stages. On the public AOL4PS benchmark and a large-scale industrial e-commerce dataset, it raises Recall@10 by 8.11% and 11.96% over the strongest baseline, with especially clear gains on long-tail queries. The implication is that end-to-end generative search should write query evidence into identifier learning and decoding, rather than treating the query as a mere prefix.

6. Hyperbolic RQ-VAE enhanced Generative Recommendation with Differential-Length Codebook Strategy

ICML 2026

When large models are joined with generative recommendation, most methods still run residual quantization in Euclidean space and only implicitly model hierarchy across codebook layers, which can distort a tree-like structure and leave codebook utilization low. A hyperbolic residual-quantization method embeds the discrete latent representations in hyperbolic space to model that hierarchy explicitly. Motivated by the exponential volume growth of hyperbolic space, it also uses a pyramidal, differential-length codebook, which aligns with the tree structure and compresses codebook size. After the geometry and the codebook hierarchy are aligned, collision rates are lower, codebook usage is more uniform, and training takes less time than existing methods. Across multiple benchmarks, the approach consistently reaches the strongest reported generative-recommendation results. For teams that assign semantic IDs to items, hierarchy is worth expressing directly in the geometry, not only approximating it layer by layer with residuals.

7. More Than a Score: Reinforcement Learning with Verbalized Feedback for Generative Recommendation

TencentNUSCUHK

Generative recommenders are increasingly aligned with recommendation objectives through reinforcement learning, but most methods use only a scalar reward, which does not say which aspects of a candidate conflict with a user's preferences or how the next candidate should differ. A verbalized-feedback framework lets a user simulator write personalized comments from the user's history and the candidate profile. The policy then produces refined recommendations, and the original and refined candidates form an expanded group that is optimized together with group-relative policy optimization. The simulator is used only in training and is removed at inference. On three Amazon review categories and two generative backbones, the method significantly improves hit rate and ranking quality; on Cell Phones, hit@20 rises from 0.0671 for the strongest baseline to 0.0730. Analyses also show that verbalized guidance encourages more diverse candidates and helps the policy find more rewarding recommendations during training.

8. Full-Sequence Masked Diffusion for Generative Recommendation

NeurIPS 2026

Autoregressive large models have dominated generative recommendation by predicting the next item in order, but this one-way generation propagates earlier errors and has a limited ability to optimize an entire recommendation list jointly. A full-sequence masked diffusion method treats user history and the top recommendations as one diffusion process: the forward pass randomly masks tokens across the full sequence, and the reverse pass denoises every masked position at once with bidirectional context. The entire list can therefore be generated in parallel, and dependencies among items are captured inside a coherent global structure. To align generation with ranking, training combines a masked-diffusion likelihood with a listwise ranking loss. On multiple public benchmarks, the method consistently outperforms the strongest autoregressive and item-level diffusion generative recommenders on both accuracy and list quality. For systems that must return a whole page of recommendations, items on the list should be generated and scored together, not guessed one after another.

9. R-GRec: Relation-Guided Generative Recommendation via Collaborative Graph Supervision

NeurIPS 2026Microsoft

Semantic-ID generative recommenders mostly learn from history-to-next-item supervision, so item-item collaborative topology is only captured indirectly through sparse user sequences. A relation-guided framework builds an item-item graph from interactions and adds three complementary auxiliary objectives: neighbor prediction, topological contrast, and link prediction, so collaborative structure is supervised explicitly during generative training. On two public Amazon datasets, the method outperforms representative traditional, generative, and large-model recommenders. Ablations show that each graph-derived objective contributes, that collaborative topology is complementary supervision for semantic-ID generation, and that no graph computation is added at inference time. For teams adopting generative recommendation, item-item collaboration can be written into training without becoming extra cost at serving time.

10. Thinking Harder Doesn't Lead to Better Choices: Reasoning Effort Increases the Confidence but Not the Correctness of LLM Shopping Agents

UUniversity of Chicago

Large models are increasingly used as autonomous shopping agents that read multimodal product listings and recommend purchases, and it is natural to assume that thinking harder will yield better choices. A benchmark of ten frontier models on three product-choice suites built from WebShop, Amazon SQID, and Wayfair WANDS tests that belief. Across all three benchmarks, raising thinking effort does not improve decision quality in a statistically meaningful way, but it does raise stated confidence at a statistically significant level, so extra thinking buys confidence rather than correctness. Most models are also poorly calibrated at every effort level. Errors are strongly shared: when several models all err on a task, more than 80% of them pick the same most popular wrong product. For anyone deploying shopping agents, a longer thinking trace is not a guarantee of better product choices.
