Embodied AI — Paper Daily, 2026-11-10

Today's embodied AI track asks where a robot policy should get the information it really needs. The headline paper lets a generalist AI model act as an agent that controls the arm directly, beating specially trained VLA policies without any demonstrations. Several studies of vision-language-action (VLA) models question a default choice: whether the action expert should pass gradients back to the vision-language model, whether images must first go through a separate vision encoder, and whether spatial understanding should wait for fine-tuning. On world models, one paper adds depth to handheld demonstrations and another quickly generates shopping-bag manipulation data from a contact model. A humanoid climbing stairs, Atlas on wheels, gravity compensation for a robot arm and a full day of work in a convenience store come from real deployments.

2. STEP: Spatiotemporal Training with Explicit Prediction for Perceptive Humanoid Locomotion

A humanoid climbing stairs or crossing gaps reads the terrain through a depth camera, but which part of the terrain matters depends on the robot's own body state and on how both change after each action. Existing methods either fuse visual and body-sensing features or predict one signal from the other at the same moment, and neither directly constrains how the state changes after an action. STEP adds two prediction tasks during training. It hides one signal and predicts its features from the other, and it predicts the next fused state from the current state and the executed action. On a real robot it succeeds in 98 of 100 trials across stairs, a high platform, a wide gap, stepping stones and walking backward on stairs, where it cannot see the terrain behind it. When camera input drops out or lags, both prediction tasks also keep the policy steadier.

3. Spatial-UMI-Dream: Scaling World Action Models with Metric Geometry for UMI Manipulation

Collecting demonstrations with a handheld gripper, the UMI approach, is cheap and independent of any particular robot, but the gripper camera is close to the objects and moves with the hand. The image cannot tell camera motion from object motion, so the real distances and sizes that precise manipulation needs must be guessed indirectly from RGB. Spatial-UMI-Dream pretrains a world action model on UMI data with depth, jointly predicting future images, metric depth and actions. The authors also build a simulation benchmark that tests changes of robot and environment. When both the robot and the environment change, it reaches 72.15% success, 10.30 percentage points above the strongest baseline, and it averages 85.0% success on six real tasks. Adding depth makes handheld demonstrations transfer more easily to different robots.

4. Should the Action Expert Train the VLM? Action-Token Supervision and Gradient Routing in Mixture-of-Transformer VLAs

Popular VLA training recipes block the gradients from the action expert to the vision-language model, and this paper finds that this default is not always right. Such models pair a pretrained vision-language model with an action expert that outputs continuous actions. Blocking gradients is meant to protect the vision-language model's existing abilities, and the recipes also train it to predict discrete tokens encoding the same actions. The authors vary these two choices separately and find that blocking gradients looks harmless mainly because the action-token supervision fills the gap. In open-loop driving, blocking or not makes little difference when that supervision is present. Without it, letting gradients through cuts trajectory prediction error by about 25%. When both parts are trained together from the start, letting gradients through raises closed-loop driving route progress by about 17%, or about 40% without action-token supervision, and robot manipulation improves as well. Blocking gradients should not be the default recipe.

3.95

5. From Pixels to Actions -- Towards Native Vision-Language-Action Policies

VLA models usually compress an image into features with a vision encoder before reasoning about actions, and this paper finds that this step loses information control needs, so removing the separate encoder gives better and steadier control. Manipulation often depends on exactly the details most easily lost in compression, such as local geometry, edges, depth and subtle state changes. NEO-VLA removes the separate vision encoder. Images pass through light convolutional patch layers and then travel with the language through the whole backbone, so the action expert can draw instruction-aware visual representations from different depths. In controlled comparisons, this native design consistently beats encoder-based versions, and neither full fine-tuning nor scale alone closes the gap. A control policy should not see only the vision encoder's final output.

3.87

6. WorldContact: A Contact-Centric World Model for Scalable Robot Learning

Adapting robots to new objects and tasks takes a lot of interaction data, and physics simulation of deformable objects such as shopping bags is slow because the simulator needs tiny time steps to resolve fast motion and keep objects from passing through each other. WorldContact trains a contact-centric world model from a small set of high-quality trajectories and predicts object motion with larger time steps, rolling out states 10 times faster than the source simulator, excluding rendering and disk time. After an existing VLA policy is fine-tuned on data expanded with it and deployed directly on a real robot, single-attempt success at lifting a bag rises from 65% with simulation data alone to 95%. For deformable-object manipulation, a learned world model can be a cheap source of data.

7. SLA: Scaling Spatial-Language-Action Model via Continual Pre-training

VLA models mostly inherit their visual representations from 2D vision-language pretraining, and spatial understanding is usually added only during task-specific fine-tuning, so it must be redone for each task. SLA moves spatial learning into continual pretraining. Starting from a pretrained π0.5 policy, it learns to predict actions while aligning intermediate visual features with spatial representations from a frozen 3D perception model. The authors also unify robot data from many sources, and during training they randomly drop the robot-state input so the model relies more on images. These extra parts are used only in training, and the inference architecture is unchanged. Under eight real-world deployment conditions, it has the highest success rate among the compared methods in every one.

8. Boston Dynamics adds wheels to Atlas humanoid to test whole-body control

Boston Dynamics has put wheels on the feet of its Atlas humanoid as a testbed for whole-body control, and it says plainly that this is not a product. The only new hardware is a custom adapter that mounts an off-the-shelf scooter rim on the existing ankle actuator, and in software each wheel is simply modeled as an ankle that can spin continuously. Classical model-based motor control keeps the robot stable, while a whole-body reinforcement learning policy adjusts pose and force in real time from body sensing and velocity commands to handle unmodeled disturbances and poor contact. All behaviors were learned in simulation and run directly on the real robot, so Atlas can roll fast on flat ground and switch back to stepping over obstacles. The engineer's takeaway is that combining the two kinds of control is more agile than either alone.

9. Make a Robot Arm Feel Weightless with NVIDIA Holoscan 5

When you guide a robot arm by hand and its motors supply just enough torque to hold up its own weight, you can move it with little effort. This is gravity compensation, the starting point for learning from demonstration and working alongside people. It requires computing inverse dynamics from the current joint state continuously and with low jitter. This NVIDIA tutorial builds a three-node control graph in Holoscan that reads joint state at a high rate, computes inverse dynamics with Pinocchio and sends torque commands to the arm. The key is to run the control nodes on one thread to avoid cross-thread handoffs, with queues that keep only the newest sample. Pinning the control thread to one CPU core then cuts the latency of the slowest few runs in ten thousand to about one fifth in the C++ version and by about 9.6 times in the Python version. Jitter in real-time control often comes from runtime scheduling rather than from the algorithm itself.

10. AI WAVE Wrap-Up: AgiBot Reports Results of Its 24-Hour Convenience-Store Livestream, Moving Physical AI Into Real Settings

AAgiBot

Whether robots can keep working in a real store that is open for business is harder to prove than whether they do a single task well. AgiBot kept 6 Genie G2 robots on duty in a convenience store for a full day under a live stream, completing 1,730 tasks in total, and 4 of them passed the assessment and stayed on. The stream closed AgiBot's AI WAVE series of releases, which also included a new dataset release, a simulation platform, an embodied agent framework and a foundation model among seven results. The public account gives only the total number of tasks and the number of robots kept, without success rates by task type, but long, continuous tests in real settings like this are a step embodied AI must pass to move from demos to real jobs.
