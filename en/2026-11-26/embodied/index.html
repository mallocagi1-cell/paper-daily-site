Embodied AI — Paper Daily, 2026-11-26

Today's embodied papers ask how skills can move across robots with less real-robot data, and how contact and coordination can be made more reliable. Some rebuild a verifiable world from a single real demonstration, revise skill code, and deploy without calling a language model at runtime. Some add low-level behavioral coverage in midtraining, so a policy can make better use of longer rollouts and online updates. Others let one policy condition on the robot's kinematics in the current scene, or turn egocentric human video into humanoid training data. Bimanual policies begin to give the two arms separate action tokens at the same timestep, and geometry can be supplied so the policy learns only execution. World models and visual latents approach transfer through cross-embodiment motion and through how a scene evolves. Tactile simulation makes normal and tangential forces more consistent, and a small walking robot can add obstacle avoidance as a correction to the velocity commands of a fixed walking policy.

1. RESET: One Demonstration, One Verified World, Self-Improved Skills Across Embodiments

LLM coding agents can iteratively improve robot manipulation skills in simulation benchmarks, but real-world tasks offer neither a ready success scorer nor a resettable practice environment. A single real demonstration already specifies scene geometry, the terminal success criterion, and a physical reference trajectory for checking whether the reconstructed world is faithful, all without depending on the source robot's kinematics. RESET rebuilds a contact-rich world and a frozen, read-only criterion from that one demonstration, checks the world by open-loop replay and silhouette consistency, then revises modular task graphs and skill code from per-node failures, and deploys the frozen code with no runtime language-model calls. On eight manipulation tasks, average success on unseen layouts in simulation is 97%, compared with 27% for zero-shot code. The resulting programs run on four real robots and succeed in 80% of 280 trials. Without target-robot demonstrations, a robot with similar arms can reuse most of the skill functions.

2. Exploratory Midtraining for Robot Policies

CMUUniversity of WashingtonToyota Research InstitutePrinceton University

Robot policies are mostly trained on human demonstrations of competent behavior, so they often lack the low-level coverage needed for later autonomous improvement. A form of midtraining can add those behaviors, reconnecting difficult states of the robot and the environment to states from which progress is still possible. The data come from adversarial teleoperation: one person reversibly perturbs the environment while another keeps going to completion without resetting, and trajectory segments are relabeled with local outcomes in natural language. In simulation and on three real-robot platforms, the resulting policies scale more favorably with data, generalize better zero-shot, and make better use of extra rollout time than policies trained on standard demonstrations. In a simulated packing setting, success rises from 54.7% with standard demonstrations to 69.7%. With 2.6 hours of this data, success already reaches 60.3%, comparable to twice as many hours of standard demonstrations.

3. OmniAct: One Policy, One Million Embodiments

University of WashingtonAllen Institute for AIUUNC Chapel HillNational University of Singapore

A single policy that controls many kinds of robots must transfer task knowledge and also adapt its behavior to what each robot can do in the current scene. Shared action representations help transfer, but they do not necessarily say how differences in morphology should change the manipulation strategy. OmniAct conditions directly on a kinematic graph of the robot in that scene, including joint positions, motion axes, and their connection to the visual observation. A flow-matching policy then predicts relative joint motions for each node, so it can cover different degrees of freedom and configurations. The authors procedurally design and filter one million physically feasible embodiments, generate trajectories for them in simulation, and train across diverse manipulation tasks. On 2,000 evaluation episodes with held-out embodiments and scenes, success is 63.0%, compared with 17.8% for pi0.5. On the task families used to evaluate GPT-6 Astra, it reaches 64.7%, compared with 41.1% for that model. On a previously unseen real robot, success is nearly twice that of the next-best method.

4. EgoLoAF: Learning Humanoid Loco-Manipulation from Egocentric Demonstrations with Hand-Object Affordances

XXi’an Jiaotong UniversityHKUST (Guangzhou)Xbotics

Training humanoid vision-language-action models usually depends on costly real-robot teleoperation. Converting egocentric human videos into training data often still requires wearable motion trackers, leaves hand-object cues implicit, and many methods still mix in real-robot teleoperation for post-training. EgoLoAF derives all of its training data from egocentric demonstrations, without wearable trackers and without real-robot teleoperation during post-training, and uses explicit hand-object relations to guide action generation. It estimates torso motion from camera poses, reconstructs both hand trajectories from video, represents hand-object relations by each hand's current pose relative to the object, and injects those cues into the action expert with cross-attention while freezing the vision-language backbone. The authors also release more than one hundred thousand egocentric episodes, spanning a thousand hours and more than a thousand tasks. Across four real-world tasks, the mean task progress score is 73.1, close to 74.4 for teleoperation, and data-collection efficiency improves by about 4.03 times.

5. Duo: Towards Spatiotemporal Action Coordination in Bimanual Policies

Shanghai Jiao Tong UniversityQQuicrobotZhejiang UniversityTsinghua UniversityTU MunichETH ZurichHKUST

Bimanual manipulation asks each arm to follow its own motion while still coordinating with the other. Common flow-based robot policies pack both arms' actions at a future timestep into one token, so attention can tell timesteps apart but cannot directly tell the arms apart. Duo models spatial and temporal dependencies inside one shared action head by splitting each timestep into two action tokens, so the arms can interact through cross-arm attention during flow matching. On RoboTwin 2.0, it raises overall success for all four base policies, from about 0.45 billion to 3.62 billion parameters, including a gain of 12.55 points for FLOWER and 9.02 points for pi0.5. Despite its compact size, FLOWER-Duo also posts the best randomized-generalization, precision, and memory scores. In real-world tasks, it outperforms pi0.5 in both success rate and execution speed, and it continues to improve as the training data grow. Systematic ablations show that cross-arm attention is what drives the gains.

6. Supply Geometry, Learn Execution: Screw Attention for Zero-Shot Policy Transfer in Robot Manipulation

University of Nottingham

Manipulation policies trained at scale often have to learn geometric structure as well, and then need further adaptation when the scene or the robot changes. Screw Attention supplies task geometry explicitly and learns only how to execute. It operates on object poses, relative pose transforms from perception, and joint screw axes from the robot model, and it has only about thirty thousand learned parameters. Trained entirely in simulation on a single robot arm, the policy generalizes to new layouts, transfers zero-shot to other embodiments, and deploys directly on a real robot, without adaptation or fine-tuning. Success is 96% on LIBERO, 85.8% under layout changes, and 80.2% across five unseen robot arms, and it succeeds in twenty-one of twenty-four real-robot trials. Much of what large manipulation policies learn may therefore be geometric structure that can instead be supplied.

7. DexLatentWorld: 3D Cross-Embodiment Latent Action World Models for Zero-Shot Robotic Manipulation

Imperial College London

World models let robots anticipate the consequences of their actions, but they usually depend on action-labeled interaction data that is costly to scale, and they are often tied to the embodiment they were trained on. A new 3D latent-action world model learns continuous action representations from point clouds of retargeted human demonstrations, and a contrastive objective keeps the same motion consistent across embodiments. Predicted geometric motion can be turned into executable commands without an action decoder for each robot. After task-agnostic adaptation, success is 90% for reaching, 92% for grasping, and 60% for placing, clearly above a prior world model. The latent actions also transfer to unseen gripper geometries. On an unseen dexterous hand, success is 86% across fifty trials on five everyday tasks of pushing, grasping, and lifting.

8. ViTacLab: Physically Consistent Visuo-Tactile Simulation, Benchmarking, and Representation Learning for Robotic Manipulation

Contact-rich manipulation needs touch, but visuo-tactile simulators often trade physical fidelity for scale, and benchmarks mostly focus on two-finger grippers. ViTacLab is a stack of simulation, benchmarks, and policies. The simulator uses three tactile primitives, anchors them to a PhysX contact solver, corrects inconsistent normals, and reconstructs tangential forces under separate slip and stick regimes, while keeping GPU parallelism. The benchmarks cover parallel grippers, dexterous hands, and bimanual systems, from vision-dominant to tactile-dominant tasks. The policy aggregates a variable number of heterogeneous tactile inputs with spatial pose embeddings and a Transformer. In controlled Hertzian indentation and real-sensor sliding tests, normal-force root-mean-square error falls from 2.31 N to 0.42 N, and tangential error from 1.85 N to 0.53 N. The tactile pipeline can exceed two thousand frames per second at two hundred fifty-six parallel environments. Contact-rich tasks improve, but absolute success on purely tactile control remains low.

9. More Than Picturing the Future: Motion-Oriented Latent Reasoning for Generalizable Robot Manipulation

HUSTUESTCA*STARSingapore Management University

Latent reasoning lets vision-language-action models think ahead at a fraction of the cost of explicit chain-of-thought, and it is indispensable for predicting actions. The usual recipe is inherited from vision-language models: each latent is aligned with an isolated future frame, so the policy is bound to the appearance of its training scenes and degrades sharply when the scene changes. Visual latents for action should capture not what a future frame looks like, but how the scene evolves toward the goal. The new method first uses semantic latents to establish what to accomplish and how, then lets motion-oriented latents anticipate each future step from coarse to fine: from the global scene, to the interaction region between the object and its destination, to the robot state. On an AgileX Piper dual-arm platform, mean success in training scenes rises from 60.8% to 90.8%. Under environmental changes, it rises from 6.7% to 37.5%. In-distribution and out-of-distribution success in simulation rise as well.

10. Microduck Obstacle Avoidance: A Sim2Real Workflow with Isaac Lab and Newton

NVIDIA

Adding local obstacle avoidance to a small walking robot often means leaving an already trained walking policy unchanged, so the gait itself is not retrained and possibly degraded. This tutorial keeps Microduck's walking policy fixed and trains a separate controller that only adjusts gamepad velocity commands so the robot can move around obstacles. Varied courses are built from reusable simulation assets, time-of-flight sensing is modeled with Newton's raycaster, and the controller is trained in Isaac Lab. The workflow covers simulation training, evaluation, export, and real-robot deployment, giving a path from simulation to a physical robot. Because the walking policy stays fixed, avoidance is expressed as a correction to velocity commands, rather than as a newly trained walking model. Avoidance can therefore sit as a separate control layer on top of a walking policy that already works, without tying gait and obstacle avoidance together in one retraining.
