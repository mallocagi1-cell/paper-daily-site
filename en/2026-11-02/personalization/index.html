Personalization — Paper Daily, 2026-11-02

Today's personalization track is about better inputs for ranking models and more trustworthy offline evaluation. Two long-sequence papers from Meta stand out: one gives history embeddings the user's own perspective, the other lets reading more history and stacking more layers be tuned separately. A third asks how to pick the best model offline in ad systems where impression probabilities are never logged. Also in the issue are a traffic simulator for supporting new creators, an online experiment on allocating advertiser subsidies, and a reminder that classical PageRank can match newer models on temporal link prediction.

1. PRISM: Personalized Item Representations in User History

The same cooking video is a pastime for a casual viewer and source material for a food creator. Yet a recommender gives it the same starting embedding in both users' history sequences. Read the deep dive →

2. Read More, Reason Deeper: Latent Workspaces for Long-Sequence Recommendation

This Meta paper gives long-sequence ranking a new structure in which reading more history and stacking more layers become two separately tunable knobs: with 10K history events, its NE beats HSTU by 0.19% while using 69.6% less forward compute. Current models tie the two together. Read the deep dive →

3. Beyond IPS: Reliable Counterfactual Evaluation in Multi-Stage Ad Systems without Logged Propensities

Before an ad system launches a new ranking model, it must pick the best candidate offline from logs. In multi-stage delivery, impressions pass through auctions, filters and caching, and their selection probabilities are never logged, so IPS cannot be used. Read the deep dive →

4. Floor It, Then Fill It: Simulating Creator Ecosystems for Fair Video-Content Recommendation Systems at Billion User Scale

Recommenders allocate traffic by engagement, so big creators keep growing and newcomers struggle to be seen. Testing traffic policies that support newcomers directly in production is costly, so the authors build a simulator of about 42K creators whose makeup and overall metrics are calibrated to the real platform, and compare traffic allocation methods inside it. The best one gives creators a weekly guaranteed view target, and the allocation algorithm uses how far a creator still falls short of it as its reward, so the small creators furthest behind get supported traffic first. With it, 90% of the guaranteed targets are met each week, and small creators' organic reach grows by about 33% per week. The authors also discuss when fairness conclusions drawn from a simulator can be trusted.

3.65

5. Revisiting Temporal Link Prediction through Structural Proximity

Temporal link prediction guesses who will interact with whom next. Recent methods mostly learn ever more complex temporal representations, yet a classical method can match or beat them. The authors run Personalized PageRank directly on the historical interaction graph, adding only a sliding window or a time decay that shrinks the weight of old edges, with no training at all. Across 14 datasets, the time-decay version ranks first on 9 and second on 4, even on datasets with few repeated edges. The reason is that paths in the history graph leading to new destinations already carry a strong signal. Current benchmarks may overstate the value of complex temporal models, and new methods should be compared against classical baselines like this one.

3.50

6. CASH: A Capability-Aware Subsidy Harness for Advertiser Subsidy Allocation

Ad platforms give advertisers billing discounts, usually set by operators through tier-based rules. Those rules cannot capture how each account responds to different discount levels. CASH needs to predict how much revenue each account brings at each discount level, and it does so in two ways at once: an LLM that carries business knowledge, and a numerical model learned from historical data. It measures each one's errors on accounts whose outcomes are already known, for each kind of account and discount level, and only a side whose error is small enough is licensed to predict for that level. Each account then uses one side's predictions and only that side's licensed levels. When both sides have licensed levels, a scorer trained on which side erred less in the past picks one; when neither does, the account gets no discount. The system then picks every account's discount level together under the total budget to maximize total revenue. In a one-month online experiment covering 60% of the platform's traffic, with the subsidy rate essentially unchanged, the platform's revenue per account rises by 15.35%, and longer-term metrics such as retention improve as well.

7. Bridging Semantics and Structure: A Theoretically Grounded Framework for Link Prediction on TAGs

Link prediction on text-attributed graphs, such as predicting whether two papers will cite each other, is usually done by combining a language model with a GNN, but how best to fuse the two is still unsettled. Bridge-LP takes a simple route. It lets the language model's text embeddings bypass the GNN layers and join the final node embeddings used for scoring, so the loss gradient flows straight back to the language model and training is more stable. And during training it re-encodes text only for the nodes in the current batch of edges, using cached embeddings for all other nodes, which saves a lot of compute. Compared with 40 existing models on 9 datasets, it beats the strongest baseline on Cora by 43.06% in MRR. Neither design is expensive, so teams working on graph-based recommendation can try each one separately.

3.40

8. DeepCommerceRec: a graph- and sequence-aware deep learning recommender system with cold-start adaptation for large-scale e-commerce platforms

E-commerce recommendation has to handle three things at once: long-term collaborative preferences, short-term purchase intent, and new users and items with almost no interactions. DeepCommerceRec learns them with two encoders. One learns structural user-item relations on a heterogeneous graph, and the other learns how interests change over time from behavior sequences. A small MLP reads both vectors plus signals such as interaction count and cold-start flags, and outputs a weight between 0 and 1 for each dimension to mix the two. For a new user, the sequence representation is replaced by a weighted combination of their few most recent items, with more recent ones weighted higher. On three public datasets, Amazon, YooChoose and RetailRocket, the authors report better HR@10, NDCG@10 and MRR than a range of baselines. The method mostly combines existing parts, and the most useful idea for real systems is adjusting the fusion weights by how cold a user or item is.

3.25

9. Hidden Disparities in Ranking: Category-Aware Fairness via Distributional Stability

A common fairness check for rankings asks whether different user groups get the same average utility, but this can hide problems. The authors simulate a job platform where the historical data miss women's interest in managerial jobs and men's interest in administrative jobs. Women are then under-served on managerial jobs and men on administrative ones, yet the two gaps cancel out and the overall gap stays near zero. The authors therefore require equal utility across groups within each job category. They prove this is equivalent to the average utility in each category staying the same when only the share of each group changes and each group's own makeup stays fixed. They then search mixtures of existing ranking policies for the one with the highest utility that meets the constraint, validating it on MovieLens-1M. Looking only at overall fairness metrics can miss bias inside categories.

3.05

10. Reinforcement Learning Under Runtime Confounding: Policy Learning and Off-Policy Evaluation

Variables visible in training logs are not always usable at deployment. A clinical decision tool, for example, may be trained with patients' socioeconomic information but barred from using it as an input when deployed, to prevent discrimination. The authors call this case, complete logs but restricted deployment, runtime confounding. In reinforcement learning it is harder, because earlier decisions shape later states and actions, so the bias accumulates over time. Their approach estimates the logging policy's action probabilities and a return model with all variables, while the new policy sees only deployment-time variables. The new policy's probability of the logged action divided by the logging policy's reweights the logs, and the return model corrects the result. Both policy learning and off-policy evaluation build on this estimate, and the evaluation gives valid confidence intervals. They also give a test that measures how much return is lost by not being allowed to use those variables. Features that recommendation and ad systems log but cannot use online for privacy or compliance reasons pose the same kind of problem.
