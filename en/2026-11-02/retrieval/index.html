AI Search — Paper Daily, 2026-11-02

Today's AI search theme is finding all of the evidence. The answer to a question is often spread over several chunks and documents, and finding only the chunk that states it is not enough. Perplexity's contextual embeddings stop accepting a single answer chunk and also rank the chunks that supply context. A chain retriever is trained on its own mistaken retrieval histories and learns to recover from wrong passages. DeFT-RAG asks one sub-question at a time with every entity named and reuses answers it already has. Coverage-aware selection picks documents that complement each other by sub-question. The other theme is serving one model fewer, as Glance lets the generator rank candidate passages itself instead of a separate reranker.

5. xPRT: Cross-Passage Retrieval Pretraining

📖 Deep dive →

Which passages of the same web page talk about related things is, by itself, enough to train a retriever. xPRT first takes passages whose vectors are close enough to the page's mean vector as anchor passages, then, by vector similarity and word overlap, finds for each anchor the four most related passages of the same document.

6. Glance: The Generator Is Its Own Reranker

📖 Deep dive →

RAG systems usually serve a reranker and a generator side by side, and the candidate passages are read once by each model. Glance starts from the idea that the generator reads these passages anyway, so it may as well rank them.

7. How Specific Do I Need to Be? Effects of User Query Underspecification on Deep Research Agents

When users ask a deep research agent a question, they often leave out the time range, use the wrong technical term, or skip their preferences. The authors remove or blur each kind of information in fully specified questions and compare the answers of three deep research agents. Without the scope or the terminology, the answers cover the user's real need much less well, yet citations stay accurate and there is no more hallucination. The search traces suggest why: with a vague question the agent searches more broadly, and fewer of its search terms come from the user's words while more come from what the model learned in training. Agents trained specifically for deep research, which score higher on complete questions, lose more. Deep research agents need to learn to ask first when a question is unclear, instead of guessing.

8. Relevance-Guided Residual Calibration for Spectral Compression of Dense Retrieval Embeddings

Projecting retrieval embeddings onto a few principal directions is a common way to cut storage and scoring cost, but how much weight each direction keeps follows only an overall trend from corpus statistics, not how much that direction helps separate relevant from irrelevant documents. RRC uses relevance labels to re-estimate each direction's weight: the larger and steadier a direction's contribution to the score gap between relevant and irrelevant documents, the higher its weight. It then fits a power-law curve to the overall trend of these weights and adds only each direction's departure from that curve to the existing weights, so the original trend of high weights on strong directions and low weights on weak ones stays. It only changes the linear transform shared by queries and documents, with no model training and no extra inference step. On five datasets of the reasoning-heavy BRIGHT benchmark, compressed to 768 dimensions, nDCG@10 is on average 2.34 points higher than the original compressor SpecTemp, and the gain holds after quantization.

9. Quantum-Inspired Pairwise Ranking Aggregation via Active LLM Feedback

When fusing results from several retrievers, unsupervised methods such as RRF are cheap but do not learn, while listwise LLM reranking works well but needs hundreds of calls per query. LLM-IRA takes a middle path. It picks only document pairs that the retrievers order in opposite ways (BM25 prefers A over B while dense retrieval prefers B over A) and asks an LLM which one is more relevant. These judgments are used to learn the fusion weights of the retrievers, so the final ranking is still a simple weighted sum of scores. With an open 8B instruction model as the judge, it matches or beats the best static fusion on most benchmarks; on two years of TREC DL it already passes the best unsupervised fusion with about 5 LLM calls per query. It needs no training data and no fine-tuning, which suits settings that want to spend only a small LLM budget at the fusion step.

10. Feedback-Assisted Trust Propagation over Document Relation Graphs for Retrieval-Augmented Generation

RAG corpora often contain outdated, contradictory or unreliable documents, and labeling which ones are trustworthy by hand is expensive, so labels cover only a small part. This paper treats each document as a node in a graph. Two documents from the same source, or that support each other, get a support edge, and two that contradict each other get a contradiction edge. These relations can be judged by an NLI model. Humans mark only a few documents as reliable or not. It then solves an optimization problem. The trust scores at the two ends of a support edge should be close. The two scores across a contradiction edge should add up to about 1, so at most one is trusted. The scores should also fit the human feedback. That is how trust spreads from the few labeled documents to the whole corpus. Both retrieval and answer generation use the score. The authors report better retrieval quality and EM than the baselines, and stable results when feedback is sparse or noisy.
