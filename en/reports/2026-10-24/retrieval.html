AI Search — Paper Daily, 2026-10-24

Today's 10 papers cover several stages of a question-answering pipeline. On retrieval, one paper analyzes the queries where hybrid search does worse than vector search alone, Lean Finder V2 uses reinforcement learning to generate short queries for training theorem search, and SurveyGraph retrieves papers for surveys along a graph of method relations. In the RAG pipeline, one paper measures how many chunk embeddings must be recomputed after a knowledge base is revised, and CANOPY trims multimodal results to the granularity needed. Another paper replaces sequential decoding in hierarchical RAG with one forward pass, and GBOR-RAG pulls evidence subgraphs from a gene graph. On the answering side, JCA has the model check each condition in the question. On the industry side come Alibaba Cloud's full-stack AI Search optimization and Tencent Cloud TokenHub's web search API.

1. The Fusion Tax: A Diagnostic Analysis of When Hybrid Retrieval Hurts

Semantic Retrieval

On Quora's duplicate-question dataset, the authors merged BM25 keyword search and BGE-small vector search by weighted scores, with the weight favoring vector search. Even on this dataset, where vector search is already accurate, about 13% of queries ended up with relevant documents ranked lower after merging than in the better of the two retrievers. For every query the authors run both retrievers, merge them with several common fusion methods, and compare the rank of relevant documents before and after, counting both gains and losses. The cost persists on other datasets and with larger vector models. Adding a cross-encoder reranker on SciFact cut the share of queries where the merged result trails the better retriever from 20% to 13%, but did not remove it. A higher average score for hybrid search does not mean every query improves; check per query whom it hurts before shipping.

2. Chunk Recomputation under Real Document Revisions: Measuring Update Cost and Retrieval Quality of Fixed, Recursive, and Content-Defined Chunking

RAG Pipeline

An English Wikipedia article changed by just 0.13%, yet fixed-length chunking had to recompute embeddings for input amounting to about 49% of the article, while paragraph-first chunking needed only 8%. The reason is that with fixed-length chunks one extra character near the start shifts the boundary of every later chunk, so each chunk's content changes and its old embedding can no longer be used. Using real document revision histories, the authors compare fixed-length chunks, chunks split by paragraph first and then subdivided, and content-defined chunks, whose boundaries are set by a fingerprint of the few nearby words, so text inserted elsewhere does not move them. An old embedding is reused only when the new chunk's input matches it byte for byte, and the authors then count the tokens that must be recomputed. They also run retrieval with the same encoder and token budget to see whether the saved work hurts search, and recall differs by only a few percentage points. When a knowledge base changes often, the chunking method decides how many embeddings each update must recompute.

3. Lean Finder V2: Semantic Search for Mathlib That Disambiguates User Queries

Query Understanding & Rewriting

A user wants a theorem in Mathlib, Lean's math library, saying that a constant function is unchanged when its input is negated, and the target is Function.Even.const. If the query spells out even function, the theorem is the 1st result, but the user's short query leaves that out and it drops to 7th place. Lean Finder V2 is trained for exactly such short queries with implicit concepts. The authors first train a query writer to produce short queries that are faithful to a theorem but easy for the current search model to get wrong, such as describing a constant function without saying even. They train the search model on these query and theorem pairs, then let the improved search model give feedback to the query writer, and repeat for several rounds. On the human-curated MathlibQR, its Recall@10 is 14% higher than the previous best semantic search. When Claude Code solved 100 algebra problems, its proof success rate with this tool was 39.3%, compared with 36.0% for the next-best search tool. The hard part of math search is the concept the user never says, so training data should be built from exactly those queries.

4. CANOPY: Adaptive-Granularity Evidence Compression for Multimodal RAG

RAG Pipeline

The question asks in which year the sport of David Douillet, a UNESCO Champion for Sport, was invented. The first retrieval found a table showing that he does judo, and the system kept only 3 of its 13 rows before searching for when judo was invented. The second retrieval found an introduction to judo, kept only 2 of its 8 sentences and answered 1882, while plain RAG, which gives the model the first results in full, got it wrong. CANOPY organizes each retrieved item into nested regions, such as a whole table, a group of rows and a single row, and a trained encoder scores how relevant each region is to the question. It switches to the smaller regions when they score at least as high as the larger one, and otherwise keeps the larger context. A separate LLM checks which fact is still missing and triggers another search, whose results are compressed the same way. With Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, the evidence tokens given to the model fall by 14.2% to 27.7% with similar accuracy. Compressing evidence means deciding per question how big a piece to keep, and sometimes a whole row beats a single sentence.

5. From VDBBench to the Top of MMEB: Full-Stack Optimization of Alibaba Cloud AI Search, from Engine to Model

Multimodal MatchingAlibaba

Alibaba Cloud says its AI Search ranks first on the MMEB multimodal document retrieval benchmark and has made breakthroughs on VDBBench for vector search and on Big5 and Tantivy for traditional search. The article credits these results to optimization across the whole stack, from its in-house Elasticsearch engine to its multimodal embedding models. Its starting point is that once agents go into production, search is no longer only about finding relevant documents but about continuously supplying the model with high-quality context. AI Search therefore covers the whole chain of query understanding, vector and full-text recall, hybrid retrieval, fused reranking and engine execution. We could read only the article's summary because the body requires a login, and the summary gives no scores or experimental details, so these results cannot yet be checked. Search that feeds context to agents needs the model and the engine optimized together, not just a better vector database.

6. Web Search on TokenHub, Tencent Cloud's Model Serving Platform

QA & Deep ResearchTencent

The example in Tencent Cloud's TokenHub documentation calls the Chat API to ask hy3 what the weather is like in Shenzhen today, with web search switched on, the lite version selected and the user's location given as Shenzhen, Guangdong. Web search is limited to 5 QPS, and usage can be checked in the usage monitor. With search on, the model decides by itself whether to look things up, writes its own search terms, such as Shenzhen weather today, and then builds the answer from the results with the sources marked. Developers can choose the lite or standard version and adjust how much context each search round injects, but the model decides how many searches to run based on the question. This is product API documentation with no reported evaluation, and the sample answer is only a placeholder. Making web search a tool the model calls itself leaves developers to manage only the search version and the context size.

7. Joint Constraint Aggregation for Qualifier-Sensitive Retrieval-Augmented QA

QA & Deep ResearchCMU

On AmbigQA, the retrieved passages sometimes mention a wrong answer more often than the right one, and on such hard questions Qwen3-30B answers 86.4% correctly with ordinary step-by-step reasoning, compared with 93.5% for the authors' method. The paper's example asks which actress said a famous catchphrase on Saturday Night Live, with Gilda Radner and Emily Litella, the character she played, as candidates. The authors first turn the question into conditions every candidate must meet, such as being an actress rather than a character. They then check each candidate against the retrieved passages and write down whether it meets each condition and why. In this example both candidates receive identical verdicts, but the written reasons keep the difference between actress and character, and a final step that compares all the reports picks Gilda Radner. On the full AmbigQA set, accuracy rises from 90.7% to 94.0%. When a question carries qualifiers, checking each candidate condition by condition and writing down why, then comparing, beats answering directly.

8. Compiling Autoregressive LLM Control into Policy-Exact Parallel Inference for Hierarchical RAG

RAG Pipeline

On QASPER's questions about scientific papers, a Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct controller picks which sections to retrieve, and on a MetaX C500 decoding token by token takes 0.63 seconds per question, compared with 0.37 seconds with the authors' compiled approach. To choose 3 sections out of 20 candidates, for example, serial decoding emits 9 tokens one after another, each waiting for the previous one. The authors process the prompt and settle the first section. The prefixes that can follow are limited, such as the first section plus some candidate second section, so the authors join all of them into one input of a few hundred tokens, where each part sees only the prompt and its own earlier part. A single forward pass then gives the scores of every section for each prefix's next step. The scores after the first-section prefix pick the second section, and the scores after the prefix with those two sections pick the third, instead of writing out JSON token by token. On most questions it picks exactly the same sections as serial decoding, differing only occasionally when scores are nearly tied. When a controller has only a few possible choices, scoring them all at once beats generating its answer word by word.

9. SurveyGraph: Generating Academic Surveys via Methods-Entity Knowledge Graphs

EMNLP 2026Semantic Retrieval

The authors built a graph from 30 papers on LLM function calling, with 74 typed relations such as which method extends which, and had Qwen write a survey from it. Compared with a version using the same papers and outline but without the graph relations, the share of citations backed by evidence was 2.8 percentage points higher. Relations in the graph can be wrong. One candidate relation claimed that a paper on scientific tool use extends API-Bank, and the audit rejected it because API-Bank is a benchmark, not an earlier method. SurveyGraph links methods, tasks, datasets and other entities in a graph and organizes sections with a standard survey outline plus themes found in the papers. For each section it follows graph relations to find related papers, then generates and revises the text, and relations with weak evidence can be filtered out before they are used for retrieval. The hard part of a survey is tracing which paper builds on which; a graph helps, but its relations need checking too.

10. Automated Enrichment Analysis with GraphRAG: Efficient Multi-Database ORA Extraction and Biological Interpretation

RAG Pipeline

Enrichment analysis is a routine step in biology that checks whether a set of strongly changed genes clusters in a known pathway or functional term, and the conventional method runs separately on each gene-set database and returns several long, unconnected lists. The authors do it on one knowledge graph instead, and on 42 real gene-expression queries the median query was 37 times faster. On the 8 of those queries that have reference results, the significance scores of the terms matched the conventional method with a mean Pearson correlation of 0.997, so the results are essentially the same. The authors build one graph, once, from the biological terms in 7 databases and the genes they annotate, then score up-regulated and down-regulated genes separately to find significantly enriched terms. For the Alzheimer's disease dataset GSE1297, for example, the system pulls out a small subgraph linked by down-regulated genes, centered on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, and hands it to an LLM to write a biological interpretation. Linking several databases into one graph reproduces conventional results faster and shows which genes connect the terms.
