AI Search — Paper Daily, 2026-10-25

Today's AI search theme is a fresh look at evaluation. Adobe shows that in agent assistants the model reads retrieval results first, so click-based attribution breaks; AutoSim tests whether simulated users can replace real ones in evaluation; and Cohere's RCP replaces sparse human labels with calibrated LLM judgments. In addition, LMVU picks evidence by which viewpoints are still missing, and ULME has one model do both retrieval and generation well.

1. When Agentic Retrieval Fails Silently: Framework and Production Evidence from a Creative AI Assistant

Adobe

Adobe compared gallery search in Adobe Express with its chat assistant, using the same retrieval system and the same users, and found that 44.4% of chat users reformulated their requests against 33.1% in gallery search. But most chat reformulations were requests like “make it bluer” or like “try a bolder font”, which refine the generated output rather than signal a failed search. In another case a user starts an export from chat and then leaves the chat screen, so the export is logged under a different session and chat abandonment shows as 59.5%, even though the user did export the work. The paper therefore suggests splitting reformulations into a new search for the same need and a correction of generated output, and carrying the full retrieved list and retrieval identifiers through to later actions. Retrieval quality, whether assets get used and whether the task succeeds should be measured separately. Once search becomes a chat assistant, old metrics like reformulation and abandonment rates change meaning, and reusing them as is misjudges retrieval quality.

2. AutoSim: A Self-Improving User Simulator for Conversational Search Evaluation

On the harder iKAT 2024 conversational search data, the authors ran the same BM25 retrieval system on real users' questions and on questions generated by a simulated user. The default simulator made the system look like it scored 0.15 nDCG, well above an illustrative launch threshold of 0.05, while real users actually got 0.02. The gap comes from the simulator not talking like a person. Even shown short real questions as examples, its messages matched human length but a classifier still told them apart with an AUC of 0.94. The authors train a small language model by repeatedly generating candidate questions, keeping those the classifier finds most human, and fine-tuning on them. With this simulator the classifier's AUC falls to 0.45, close to guessing, and its retrieval score of 0.03 is close to the real users' 0.02. Before using a simulated user to evaluate conversational search, check that it talks like a person, or a failing system may look ready to ship.

3. Learning Marginal Viewpoint Utility for Budgeted Evidence Selection in RAG

A European Social Survey question asks whether respondents voted in the last national election, with three answers: yes, no and not eligible to vote. Under the same budget of 1024 tokens, the common MMR method picked 12 responses that all said yes, while this paper's method covered all three answers in its first three picks and only then spent the remaining budget on repeated support. A tiny linear scorer is trained on labeled development data to prefer passages that add viewpoints missing from the evidence already chosen. It scores only relevance to the question, passage length and similarity to chosen evidence, never looks at viewpoint labels at run time, and each time takes the highest-scoring passage that fits the remaining budget. On another question about perceived corruption it covered every viewpoint within 539 tokens. When picking retrieved evidence for an LLM, making sure every viewpoint is present helps more than piling up similar high-relevance passages.

4. ULME: Predictive Coupling for Mutually Reinforcing Embedding and Language Modeling

A team at Tencent WeChat inserted a few learnable placeholder tokens between the question and the answer so that Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct learns retrieval and answering together, and removing them cost 5.2 points on the MTEB-lite embedding benchmark and 2.0 points on MMLU knowledge questions. In other words, the placeholders help both retrieval and generation. One placeholder is trained to match relevant documents, the model learns to generate the answer as usual, and another placeholder at the end of the question learns to move close to the mean of the answer's hidden states in the same forward pass. Training moves only the question side toward the answer side and leaves the answer side unchanged, so the question's embedding picks up information useful for answering. On Qwen3-8B, one set of weights produces both retrieval embeddings and answers, scoring 66.0 on MTEB, 4.1 points above LLM2VEC-GEN on the same backbone. Retrieval embeddings and generation can be trained together in one model and even help each other, with no need to maintain two models.

5. Rubric-Calibrated Preferences: Cross-Query Calibration of LLM Judgments via Item Response Theory

CCohere

Cohere hired 46 paid external annotators to grade the top five results from two search rerankers for the same query. In 185 comparisons, the paper's calibrated metric and the usual nDCG on human labels disagreed, with one matching the annotators and one not; in 72.4% of them the calibrated metric was the one that matched. An LLM repeatedly compares batches of documents for a query, which yields a Bradley–Terry comparison score for each document. It then answers, for each document, five yes or no questions shared across all queries, from whether its topic relates to the query to whether it treats the topic in depth, each harder to pass than the last. For each query the authors find a shift and a stretch so that a higher converted score and an easier question both mean a higher chance of answering yes, with the shift and stretch chosen to make that chance fit the documents' actual answers best. With this metric, the rerankers zerank-2 and zerank-1, ranked 14th and 13th on NanoBEIR, moved to the top two. When an LLM judges relevance, scores from different queries must be calibrated to one scale first, or reranker rankings can flip entirely.

6. Self-Assessment Reinforcement Learning for Search-State Awareness in Retrieval-Augmented Large Language Models

Asked for the birth date of the mother of handball player Emilie Hegh Arntzen, the trained model first searches for Emilie and finds that her mother is Hanne Hegh. It then searches for Hanne, notices that the birth date is still missing, searches for that date specifically, and returns the correct answer, 27 April 1960. This sense of what is still missing is trained in. During training, the same model reviews its finished search and answer in a separate context and points out which requirements have evidence and which missing facts call for more searching. After an assessment the model may search again and re-answer, and the reward looks at how answer correctness changes from before to after, so assessments that turn a wrong answer right score points. Each extra assessment costs more than the last, pushing the model to gather all the evidence before its first answer. At test time there is no separate assessment step, and the model just searches and answers. Replacing the self-assessment with random complete or incomplete feedback gave an average validation accuracy of 41.2, against 55.5 with real assessments. Having a search agent check its own evidence gaps during training makes it go find the missing facts on its own when used.

7. Graph Topology Meets Energy-Driven Pathfinding: Preserving Contextual Consistency in Retrieval-Augmented Generation

EMNLP 2026

A MuSiQue question asks who sang Home Alone Tonight with the singer of Crash My Party. The retrieved passages include an unrelated Eddie Money song, and the system first cuts them into 270 spans that keep the original wording, linking neighbouring spans and the most similar pairs of spans across documents into one graph. The starting spans are those most similar to the question and its entities, scored by similarity. Personalized PageRank then passes each span's score to its neighbours by link strength each round, with part of the score always returning to the start, until the scores settle. Spans whose score falls below a threshold are dropped, and only those still reachable from the starting spans are kept, leaving 9. These spans connect two facts, that Luke Bryan sang Crash My Party and that he sang Home Alone Tonight with Karen Fairchild, so the answer is Karen Fairchild. On HotpotQA, swapping only the usual graph of entity and relation triples for this text-preserving graph, with everything else unchanged, raised exact match from 0.288 to 0.414. Keeping original text spans and their context in graph retrieval loses less of the information reasoning needs than extracting triples alone.

8. ERCLIP: Small-Data Adaptation of CLIP for Free-Length Image-Text Retrieval

The authors trained CLIP for one epoch on one A100 using just 20k image and text pairs, each image paired with a short COCO caption and a long ShareGPT4V description. On four long-description image and text retrieval tests, Recall@1 rose by 6.5 to 11.2 points over the original CLIP. Each long description is split into sentences, each sentence is encoded with CLIP, and the results are averaged or merged with learned weights so the image vector moves close to both the merged description and the short caption. A frozen original CLIP is kept as a reference during training to penalize image and short-caption vectors drifting too far and losing old abilities, which recovers 30% to 42% of the ImageNet accuracy lost during adaptation. At inference the sentence-merging part is removed and text still goes through CLIP's original 77-token window. Teaching CLIP to read long descriptions does not require big data or a new architecture, and one epoch on a little data gives a clear gain.

9. FlexVDR: Learning to Allocate Matryoshka Representation Budgets for Visual Document Retrieval

In the industrial document search test of ViDoRe V3, the webAI retriever had to answer 283 queries over the complete corpus of 5,244 pages. Choosing a representation size separately for each query cut online computation by 20.4% compared with always using the full model, while retrieval quality fell by only 0.86 percentage points. The existing retriever is frozen and small adaptation maps are trained so it can build representations with fewer encoder layers, fewer embedding dimensions and fewer matching vectors. A predictor reads early query features and estimates the retrieval score each representation would reach, then subtracts a penalty based on that representation's compute cost, with an adjustable weight where a larger weight saves more compute. Only the chosen encoding path keeps running, and its vectors are matched against document vectors prepared offline. On ViDoRe V1, ColPali used a smaller representation for 66.60% of queries while its nDCG@5 fell only from 83.90 to 83.16. Not every query needs the largest representation, and allocating compute by query difficulty saves a good deal of computation.

10. Unbalanced Optimal Transport for Efficient Visual Document Retrieval

ECCV 2026

The authors tested document image search on five topics, energy, government, healthcare, artificial intelligence and French environmental reports, each with 1,000 pages and 100 queries and with many similar pages planted to compete. Compressing each page's visual vectors to one fiftieth with Qwen2, the paper's method scored 19.4 points higher in nDCG@5 than Light-ColQwen, which compresses by clustering. Each page's many patch vectors are replaced by a few representative vectors, and queries are matched against these compressed vectors. During compression, similar patches are repeatedly assigned to representatives that are then updated, patches with more model attention get more weight, and each representative may take in roughly the same total patch weight, so a few representatives cannot take over the whole page. On the harder English domain-specific test at the same compression ratio, it beats Light-ColQwen by 23.1 points. When compressing vectors for document image search, weighting by importance and stopping a few representatives from absorbing everything keeps more retrieval quality.
