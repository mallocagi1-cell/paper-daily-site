Generative — Paper Daily, 2026-10-25

All 4 papers today study what influences an LLM when it chooses for users. They ask which training stage shapes its brand taste, how far seller copy can sway a shopping agent, whether agents follow the crowd when they see each other's popularity counts, and whether judge prompts for multilingual evaluation can be copied from English. There are few new papers on generative recommender systems themselves today.

4.05

1. How Language Models Learn Taste: Brand Preference in LLMs

Asked to continue the sentence “If you want a good web browser, I recommend”, the base Tulu model, before any assistant training, says Firefox in 13 of 20 samples. Given the sentence “A well-known web browser is”, it most often says Chrome. In other words, the lean toward Firefox when asked for advice exists before the model learns to act as an assistant. Across 102 models, the authors compare two ways of asking about the same category, one asking the model to name a well-known brand and the other asking it to recommend and pick one. In 26 of 42 categories the picked brand differs from the named one, and different models switch to the same brand, such as Nike becoming Brooks and Chrome becoming Firefox. The authors also compare checkpoints saved at different training stages and search the training data for explicit brand recommendations to see whether they explain the shift. What a model recommends differs from what it knows is famous, and brand preference starts forming in pretraining.

2. A Cue's Hidden Price: Auditing Seller Influence on AI Shoppers

The researchers prepared six pairs of headphones, each pair identical in price, star rating, review count and specs. One listing in each pair got the extra sentence “Our best-selling model, chosen by over 10,000 customers.” Asked to pick one, GPT-4o chose the listing with that sentence in 144 of 144 trials. The authors then raised that product's price step by step, and only at about 13% more, roughly 17 dollars on a 130-dollar item, did GPT-4o pick it half the time and the other one half the time. Running the same test with a plain product sentence instead, the product was also chosen almost every time at equal price, but a premium of about 2% was enough to bring its share down to half. Judged by how often they win at equal price the two sentences look almost the same, but judged by what they are worth they differ sixfold. This paper from Adelaide University and Melbourne Business School puts such a price on every sentence a seller writes. Win rates lump strong and weak seller claims together; converting them into a price tells them apart.

3. LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades

The authors had Gemma 3 4B play a crowd of listeners, each choosing 4 of 48 brand-new songs nobody had heard, reading the lyrics and deciding which to recommend. In the strongest-influence setting, every listener saw how many times earlier listeners had recommended each song, with the list sorted by popularity. The choices of the first 30 listeners almost fully determined later popularity, with a correlation of 0.998, while the songs' own appeal had almost nothing to do with final popularity, with a correlation of 0.118. Appeal was measured with a separate crowd who saw no one's recommendations and chose independently. The authors gave each listener different demographics and music tastes and compared independent recommendation with seeing earlier recommendations. Letting each listener read more songs reduces this herding but does not remove it. When agents can see each other's choices, early chance gets amplified, and the final hits are not necessarily better.

4. Prompting Strategy Effects Depend on the Judge: A Multilingual Study of LLM-as-a-Judge Robustness

With the evaluated content unchanged, the judge's instructions, rubric and score definitions were translated from English into the language of the text being judged, with judges giving an overall score from 1 to 5. This raised Gemma 3's mean score by 0.83 points but lowered Aya Expanse's by 0.32. Aya's biggest drop, 2.16 points, was on Yoruba. Most of the judged texts were human-written references whose quality did not change, so only the judges' scoring habits moved. The authors give different judge models the same input, the same candidate output and the same rubric, change only how the prompt is written, and compare the scores. On a machine-translation dataset with 12,565 human ratings, under the same target-language prompt Gemma agreed most with the human judgments and Aya least. Overall, which judge model you use matters more for the scores than how the prompt is written. The same prompting trick can work in opposite directions for different judges, so it cannot be carried over from one judge to another.
