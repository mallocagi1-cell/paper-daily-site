Generative — Paper Daily, 2026-10-25

All 4 papers today study what influences an LLM when it chooses for users. They ask which training stage shapes its brand taste, how far seller copy can sway a shopping agent, whether agents follow the crowd when they see each other's popularity counts, and whether judge prompts for multilingual evaluation can be copied from English. There are few new papers on generative recommender systems themselves today.

4.05

1. How Language Models Learn Taste: Brand Preference in LLMs

Ask a language model to name a running-shoe brand and it often says Nike; ask it to recommend one and it often says Brooks. The authors confirm this across 102 models and 42 categories: in 26 categories the recommended brand differs from the named one, with named brands mostly category leaders and recommended brands mostly niche ones. Taking apart three open training pipelines shows that pretraining already links recommendation sentences to certain brands, SFT mainly teaches the model how to answer, and DPO is what actually moves the pick, even though the preference data contains almost no brand comparisons. Which brand a model recommends is shaped mainly in preference optimization, not just by which brands it knows.

2. A Cue's Hidden Price: Auditing Seller Influence on AI Shoppers

When a language model shops for someone, seller cues such as bestseller badges, expert endorsements or low-stock warnings change which product it picks. This influence used to be measured by choice rate, but a strong cue pushes the choice rate close to 100 percent, after which strong and stronger cues look the same. The authors propose price-equivalent auditing: the model chooses between two products, each pair is shown in both orders to cancel position effects, and the price of the cued product is raised until it is picked only half the time. That premium is what the cue is worth. Across 7 models, cues with similar choice rates can differ several-fold once priced. Only by converting a seller cue's effect into money can different cues be compared

3. LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades

When several LLM agents recommend unfamiliar songs together, will they follow the crowd if each can see how often the others recommended each song? Following the classic Music Lab experiment from sociology, the authors compare a setting where cumulative recommendation counts are visible with one where they are not. With counts visible, collective recommendations quickly drift away from how good the songs are, end up extreme, and differ from one rerun to the next. Compared with human participants, LLM groups follow the crowd more easily and correct themselves less. Once agents can see each other's popularity counts, feedback loops amplify recommendations instead of converging on song quality.

4. Prompting Strategy Effects Depend on the Judge: A Multilingual Study of LLM-as-a-Judge Robustness

Multilingual tasks increasingly use LLMs as judges, yet prompting strategies such as chain of thought or role-play have almost only been validated on English benchmarks. This paper tests 9 prompting strategies, 4 open judge models and 27 languages, producing over a million scores that are compared with human judgments. Prompting strategies do not transfer: switching the judge model matters more than switching the prompt, the same strategy can even have the opposite effect on different judges, and results vary more on low-resource languages. In multilingual evaluation, pick the judge model first and validate prompts for it, rather than reusing English findings.
