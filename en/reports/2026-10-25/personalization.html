Personalization — Paper Daily, 2026-10-25

Today's personalization theme is making use of what users actually saw. LinkedIn puts people who were shown but not opened into its training objective, a carousel bandit updates only on positions that were really browsed, and Kuaishou has several agents write ad scripts per user. Other papers handle recommendation under hard constraints, including a shared household fridge, medication sets and ad auctions inside assistants.

1. PAIR: Preference-Aligned Intent Retrieval for Premium People Recommendation at LinkedIn

LinkedIn

A software engineer who starts job hunting stops clicking fellow engineers on LinkedIn and starts clicking recruiters, hiring managers and talent acquisition staff, so recommending people by profile similarity alone gets it wrong. In an A/B test in the module that recommends people to Premium members, LinkedIn's new method raised click-through rate by 7.5% and Premium retention by 2.1% relative to the control, with page load latency nearly unchanged. It trains a profile encoder to rank people a user clicked above people shown but not clicked. It then combines the user's own profile vector with a weighted mix of recently clicked profiles to represent current interests. Clicked profiles are encoded in advance, so updating interests takes only vector arithmetic, with no need to rerun the language model. People recommendation should follow a user's recent clicks, because when someone's goal changes, so do the people they want to meet.

2. Ranking Bandits for Carousel Interfaces with Observable Browsing Depth

Using 3,477 movie-selection records from the RecGaze eye-tracking dataset, the authors simulate a horizontally scrolling movie carousel and look only at the first 5 slots. Their algorithm learns each round how far the user scrolled before stopping, while the comparison algorithm gets only the clicks but is handed the true probability that each slot is seen. At the end, their OD-TS has a cumulative regret of 856.1 against 876.9 for the comparison, with overlapping confidence intervals, so it is effectively a tie. The approach is simple. Only movies the user is confirmed to have scrolled to and seen update the click estimates, and movies never reached do not count as dislikes. In a setting where most users never reach the end and slot 5 is seen with probability 0.08, the two still end up close. Recording how deep users scroll a carousel is worth as much as knowing each slot's exposure probability in advance.

3. Bezier-Guided Adaptive Flow for Cross-domain Sequential Recommendation

The paper picks one user in Amazon's sports category and ranks the item he actually bought against 999 random items. Transferring his preferences from another category along a fixed curved path puts the item at rank 13; letting the path correct its direction from his current interests as it goes lifts it to rank 8, inside the top-ten list. The transfer turns his interest vector from the other category step by step into a sports vector. The reference route is a curve joining start and end, with its bend point computed from his interest profile, and the fixed approach just follows it. At each step, this paper also has a small network compute how far to depart from that curve. It uses the current position and his interest profile, trained so that the final vector ranks the item he actually buys next near the top, and the result is combined with interaction-based predictions to rank items. Across the tested domains and metrics it beats the strongest baseline by 3.91% to 16.62%. When moving a user's preferences across categories, adjusting the route to each user along the way is more accurate than a fixed path.

4. Behavior lifetime modeling uses time sensitive behavioral signals to rank candidate items for purchase in E commerce recommendation

JJiangxi University of Software Professional Technology

On Taobao UserBehavior data, the authors deliberately put items the user browsed repeatedly but did not buy, favorited or carted but did not buy, or from the same category into the candidates, ranked together with what the user actually bought. In this harder setting every method got worse, but the paper's BLMRec still beat the strongest baseline, RMBRec, on Recall@20 by 8.8%. In training, each browse, favorite or cart add is checked over five periods starting at the action, within 1 day, within 3 days, and so on up to 30 days. The question is whether the same item moved a step further, such as a browse followed by a cart add or a cart add by a purchase, or whether the user bought a related item. Each period gives its own label. If so the action counts as still valid for that period, otherwise as stale, and from this the model learns how much purchase signal an action keeps as time passes. For each candidate, links between past items and the candidate then decide how much browsing and cart interest counts alongside purchase history. Under the usual sampled evaluation, Recall@20 on Taobao improved by about 6.0%. Browsing and cart signals go stale over time, so ranking purchase candidates should discount them by age.

5. MAPS: A Multi-Agent Personalized Script-Driven Video Advertising System Enhanced by Reinforcement Learning

Kuaishou

Kuaishou ran an online A/B test in one advertising scenario, where the comparison method GRM retrieves creatives from an existing pool while the paper's MAPS generates a personalized video ad for each user on the spot. Click-through rate rose by 2.87%, conversion rate by 2.74% and average revenue per user by 9.85%. The system first infers the user's preferences from viewing history and the video being watched, then writes an ad script using the product details. A video model turns the script and product images into an ad. After each impression, the system uses the user's online feedback to judge which part of the profile was wrong, such as interests, aesthetic taste or buying motive, and revises it, so the next script changes too; the paper gives no exact rule for that judgment. The script writer first learns from human-written scripts, then gets reinforcement-learning rewards for scripts that are well structured, fit the user and describe the product accurately. The offline benchmark uses 250 user and product pairs. Ad creatives no longer have to be picked from a pool but can be generated per person, and the revenue gain is already measurable online.

6. UserArena: Benchmarking Interactive User Simulation for Long-Horizon Shopping Agents

An anonymous shopper opened the shopping feed three times in a row, adding brow mascara and eye cream to the cart on the first visit and viewing makeup primer and leaving on the second. On the third visit the shopper both bought and carted an eyeshadow, then kept browsing product details, 26 actions in all. This paper rebuilds feeds and product pages from real logs like these to make a test bed where simulated users shop over long sessions. To train the simulated shopper, a student model writes its own next action and reply in real page states. A frozen teacher model also sees broad preferences summarized from the shopper's complete records, gives its probability for each token the student wrote, and the student learns to move toward the teacher. The authors test it against a real online A/B test of ranking policies, rerunning both policies with 10,000 simulated users. The online measure was how much the per-page-view click-through rate rose, and the closest simulator measured a rise 0.713 times the real online one, with its gap from 1 being 50.2% smaller than the runner-up's. Simulated users are starting to give credible estimates of ranking-policy effects, though they still fall short of the real online effect.

7. FairFRESH: A Neuro-Symbolic Framework for Fair and Explainable Household Food Recommendation

The paper builds a household from the US NHANES diet survey in which one member has hypertension. When each person gets their own picks, he receives bacon, fried eggs, French dressing, tap water, toasted wheat bread and heavy cream, and none of the 4 members gets both a grain and a legume. Searching first for one dish the whole family shares, the system picks white bread with pinto beans, which fits everyone's dietary rules, and all 4 members get a complete meal. Each person–food pair is first scored, then the limited pantry is split among members. The remaining places on each person's list are filled one food at a time, each going to whoever raises the product of everyone's satisfaction the most. Each member's satisfaction is the sum of the scores of the foods they get. The same food raises a low total by a larger proportion, and so raises the product more, so no one ends up with a much worse meal. On 100 held-out four-person households, this fair allocation shrinks the satisfaction gap between members by 42%. Household food recommendation should not just add up everyone's favorites, and finding a dish the whole family can share works better.

8. Truthful by Omission: Click Thinning for LLM-Native Advertising

Two ads sit in a fixed order inside an LLM answer, and a reader who reaches either one clicks it with probability 50%, but stops reading after the first click. If the platform keeps the first ad with probability 50% and the second with probability two thirds, both end up with an actual click probability of 25%, so the later slot is no longer a disadvantage. This paper's auction works exactly this way. It never changes the order of passages in the answer and instead randomly drops earlier ads more often to offset readers stopping at their first click. Under that rule it picks the ad and passage pairing that exactly maximizes expected click value. Under a VCG-style rule, what each advertiser pays on average is the expected click value his presence costs the others, divided by his click probability to give the price charged on each actual click. This makes reporting his true value per click the best strategy. The paper proves it gets at least half the maximum expected click value that free reordering would allow. Ads inside LLM answers can be made fair and truthful by random omission rather than by moving them around.

9. From Scores to Safe Sets: Exact Safety--Constrained Decoding for Medication Recommendation

On MIMIC-III hospital visits, the same FLAME model's medication scores were held fixed. Each method had to prescribe the same number of drugs per visit, 26.78 on average, while the share of drug pairs in a set flagged as interacting could not exceed 8%, the paper's strictest setting. The best method that first removes conflicting drugs and then searches step by step for replacements matched doctors' actual prescriptions with a Jaccard score of 0.3963, while choosing the whole set together scored 0.4027. The paper does the latter, directly finding the set with the highest total score within the interaction cap instead of editing drugs one at a time. The authors also tightened the allowed interaction rate step by step, and at every checkpoint whole-set selection matched doctors more closely. The stricter the cap, the larger its advantage. Under tight safety constraints, choosing the whole result set at once beats recommending first and patching one item at a time.

10. FAIRGate: Agentic Runtime Policy Selection and Verified Repair for Fairer Recommendation Delivery

For 1000 MovieLens users, a standard BPR-MF recommender first picks 100 candidate movies per person and the final list has 10, with a language model deciding which fairness policy to apply this time. Compared with the original model, long-tail movie coverage rose by 21.52% in relative terms. Applying policies in a fixed order satisfied the chosen policy for only 9.10% of requests, against 60.70% here. The language model reads precomputed summaries of relevance, popularity, genre exposure and the user's past preferences. It then picks from a fixed menu of policies, such as a floor for long-tail movies, balanced exposure across movie groups, or a cap on any one group crowding the list. A deterministic program then swaps suitable movies into the list and checks that the change meets the policy, that the relevance loss stays within a preset budget and that the genre mix does not drift far from the user's history. The final explanation is written only from this verified change record, whereas unconstrained explanations contained unsupported fairness claims every time. Letting a language model pick the fairness policy while a program applies and verifies it is more reliable than letting the model edit the list itself.
