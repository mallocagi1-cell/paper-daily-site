Embodied AI — Paper Daily, 2026-11-27

Today's embodied papers include world-model policy optimization, so a vision-language-action model can learn to correct itself from imagined failures, and methods that take camera motion from in-the-wild videos that never show object manipulation. Others combine visual and force memory so a policy can adapt when contact changes, or steer a frozen action model along a 3D object trajectory so fine-tuning does not wash out old skills. On the data side, one pipeline turns human demonstrations into physically executable whole-robot trajectories, and another attaches video priors to control on a different body. Long-horizon tasks have to keep both demonstration intent and actual execution progress, and mobile manipulation needs navigation and dexterous skill to co-evolve. Other work uses a single human video as context, or treats pathfinding for thousands of warehouse robots as optimal transport of a gas.

1. WMPO: World Model-based Policy Optimization for Vision-Language-Action Models

ICLR 2026HKUSTByteDance

Vision-language-action models mainly learn manipulation from expert demonstrations, so they have little chance to learn from failures or to correct themselves. Running reinforcement learning on a real robot also makes samples very expensive. WMPO no longer has the policy interact with the physical environment: it first imagines trajectories with a pixel-level world model, aligns those imaginations with features pretrained on web-scale images, and then does on-policy optimization rather than the more common off-policy updates. Because the predictions are pixels rather than more abstract latents, the imagined rollouts line up better with the visual features the model already has. In simulation and on a real robot, it uses fewer samples, shows self-correction, and can keep improving. On the real robot, the base policy, a preference-optimization baseline, and this method reach success rates of 53%, 60%, and 70%.

2. CamAct: Learning Robotic Manipulation from In-the-Wild Videos via Camera Motion

Harbin Institute of TechnologyZhejiang UniversityACE Robotics

Vision-language-action models need large, diverse manipulation data, but collecting robot demonstrations is expensive. Prior work uses egocentric human video and usually only the parts that show people interacting with objects. Even when a video contains no object-manipulation demonstration, camera motion recovered from in-the-wild footage can still give a robot policy extra motion supervision, filling in what robot demonstrations do not cover. Those clips were not filmed to train robots. CamAct fine-tunes with this camera-motion supervision together with robot demonstrations. On the already strong LIBERO suite the gain is limited; on the harder RoboCasa suite, success rises from 22.96% to 29.32%. In-the-wild video does not first have to become an object-interaction demonstration: how the camera moves can itself be a signal for learning actions.

3. M$^{2}$AdaptVLA: Multimodal Memory-Augmented Vision-Language-Action Model with Predictive Test-Time Adaptation

Dexterous manipulation needs multimodal memory and has to adapt quickly when contact conditions change. Many policies keep only a visual history, or only a force and torque history, and miss cues from the other stream; even with force memory, a fixed policy can fail when contact changes at deployment. This model combines visual and force memories and adapts predictively at test time. On the visual side, test-time training writes observation history into fast weights; on the force side, a gate decides how much the action expert should use force history, while jointly predicting future actions and forces. Measured forces can also serve as online self-supervision to update low-rank adapters. On a safe-opening task, it completes 69.7% of individual keypresses, clearly ahead of controls that keep only frames or only do test-time training, and whole-task success can reach 90%.

4. Cairn: Object-Centric 3D Trajectory Priors for Steering Frozen VLAs

University of PennsylvaniaAthena RC

Vision-language-action models can produce low-level actions, but where an object should go is often tied to the layouts they were trained on. If objects have been moved, or an obstacle blocks the way, the policy may still carry them to the wrong place or into a collision. Fine-tuning for new layouts needs robot data for each one and can wash out skills the model already has. Cairn keeps the original model frozen and steers it at test time along a 3D trajectory of the held object. A small prior reads point clouds of the scene and the object, the pose at the grasp, and the instruction, then samples trajectories that go around obstacles and turn through gaps. The model's intended motion helps choose and reshape those trajectories, and the chosen one pulls the actions during denoising. On a layout-shift benchmark, success rises from 27.3% to 38.0%. Direct fine-tuning cuts a high success rate on the original suite by a wide margin, while this form of steering largely keeps the original level.

5. DexEngine: Scaling Human Demonstrations into Physically Executable Whole-Robot Data

POSTECHRRLWRLD

To turn human hand-object demonstrations into training data for a whole robot, the retargeted motions must be kinematically feasible and physically executable, and they have to be produced at scale. DexEngine first places each hand-object clip where the arms can reach it, finds an arm motion that follows the human hands, and then corrects the motion in physics simulation with residual reinforcement learning so that it can actually be executed. Starting from nine hand-object datasets, it produces about five thousand two hundred clips for each of four dexterous hands. Even with arms attached, it tracks objects more accurately than earlier floating-hand methods and runs more than ten times as fast as reinforcement-learning-based ones. Policies trained on this data far outperform the same clips without physics correction, or with the arms attached only afterwards. Without that correction, a diffusion policy succeeds in 0% of rollouts on the easier setting, compared with 53% on the corrected data.

6. X-World: Transferring Video Priors to Cross-Embodiment Robot Control

Fudan UniversitySShanghai Innovation Institute

Language-instructed manipulation needs closed-loop policies, but action-labeled robot trajectories are expensive and limited in coverage. Human and robot videos contain spatiotemporal priors that can transfer across embodiments. X-World adapts a pretrained video diffusion model to variable views, compresses hidden states to drive a shared action expert, and uses dataset-specific projections for native states and actions. It first adapts the backbone on large-scale robot and human videos, then freezes it to pretrain the action modules, and finally post-trains on the target platform. At inference it responds at twenty hertz and overlaps updates with execution through asynchronous chunking. It reaches 98.5% average success on LIBERO, and 81.7% average success across six bimanual tasks on a side-mounted dual-arm UR5e. Priors learned from video can be connected to control on different robots.

7. RoboDemo: Reconciling Demonstration Intent and Execution Progress for Long-Horizon Manipulation

Nanjing UniversityJDSCUTCUHK-Shenzhen

Long-horizon manipulation cannot just predict the next action from the current observation. The policy has to keep a procedural prior for how far each stage should have progressed, and also track the progress it has actually made; if those two fall out of line, later steps go wrong. RoboDemo can take a relevant human demonstration video, or a semantically similar robot video retrieved from a simulation corpus, and write task semantics and motion into a stable memory of demonstration intent. At deployment it compresses online observations, robot state, and executed actions into a memory of execution progress. The reference and the execution each update their own fast weights, so changes on one side do not drag the other off course. On a plate-on-rack task, success stays at least 97.5% across three settings; the average success on pouring is 82.9%, compared with 66.3% for a common imitation method.

8. Nav4Manip: From Navigation to Dexterous Mobile Manipulation through Co-Evolution

Peking UniversityGGalbotQwen

Dexterous mobile manipulation in complex environments often fails in two places. Navigation is usually too coarse: manipulation needs a precise base pose and approach heading, not merely arrival in a meter-scale region. Navigation and manipulation are also often trained in isolation, with no feedback loop that lets the two improve together. Nav4Manip couples them so they co-evolve: one side learns long-horizon navigation and refines the policy from whether manipulation succeeds, and the other trains a world-action model for the dexterous skill that follows. Navigation is no longer just about reaching a region; it has to hand over an endpoint the manipulator can use. Under instruction following, end-to-end success reaches 56.31%. Once the approach pose that manipulation needs is part of the navigation objective, the two sides can help each other on long-horizon tasks, instead of being trained apart.

9. Entangle: Scaling Human-Video In-Context World-Action Models across Unseen Tasks and Contexts

In-context learning lets a foundation model do unseen tasks from context without changing its parameters. In robot manipulation, a human video can serve as that context and show how a task should be done. Scaling this to world-action models still hits two problems: human and robot task pairs are not temporally aligned, and an ordinary action loss lets the policy take shortcuts from the initial scene or the task identity. Entangle maps a single human video and the robot's current observations to future visual states and executable actions. It turns recorded robot videos into matched human contexts with video generation, and uses an intra-task contrastive objective so the policy cannot rely on task identity alone. Without task-specific fine-tuning, it reaches an average score of 83.5 out of 100 on six unseen tasks on a real robot, and a 69.5% average success rate on seven unseen simulation tasks, 22.6 percentage points above a comparable baseline.

10. Lazy gas, hot gas, and a thousand robots

Amazon

A warehouse floor often has thousands of robots moving at once, and they must avoid collisions while getting inventory where it needs to go. Multi-agent pathfinding is hard in general, and exact solvers struggle to keep up with real-time dispatch once the fleet grows. Amazon Robotics treats the planning problem as optimal transport: robots are modeled as a lazy, cold gas whose particles spend only the minimum effort to reach their destinations, which yields optimal, minimum-energy paths, though the computation can still be slow. Modeling the fleet as a hot gas that jitters along the way, through an entropic relaxation, is much faster and only slightly off the optimum. On a one-hundred-fifty by one-hundred-fifty grid with one thousand one hundred twenty-five robots, the hot-gas scheme returns a solution in about 67 seconds, within 6.5% of optimal. At fulfillment-center scale, a fast plan that is close enough is often more useful than waiting for an exact one.
