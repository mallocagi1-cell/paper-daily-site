Embodied AI — Paper Daily, 2026-11-24

Today's embodied track asks when a policy should compute finely or skip ahead, how it should recover from a failed grasp, and whether it can still finish an instruction when supervision is incomplete or disturbances appear. Some methods write temporal resolution into the training targets, so the policy leaps over redundant motion and refines at contact. Others give the policy an explicit stage pointer, or turn an image generator itself into an action learner. Chain-of-thought used as an action prefix can accumulate errors at inference, and grasp failures may need force feedback before a retry starts. Missing action labels, unsampled payloads and terrains for a quadruped, and world-action models that recompute nearly the same chunk in closed loop all push systems to handle incomplete signals and redundant compute more carefully. Point tracking also now has a benchmark and training suite aimed at manipulation.

4.84

1. SkiP: When to Skip and When to Refine for Efficient Robot Manipulation

NeurIPS 2026

Imitation-learning policies often predict future actions at every control step, whether the robot is in predictable free-space motion or in a precise, contact-rich phase, so temporal resolution is treated as fixed. A skip policy partitions demonstrations into key segments that need dense refinement and skip segments with temporally redundant motion, then relabels the behavior-cloning target in a skip segment with the action at the entrance of the next key segment. The same network can leap over redundant steps and refine carefully where it matters, without a learned skip planner. The partition comes from Motion Spectrum Keying, which reads the local frequency structure of trajectory velocities and needs no manual annotation. Across 72 simulated manipulation tasks and three real-robot tasks, executed steps fall by 15% to 40%, while success rates match or improve. Temporal resolution can be controlled in the supervision signal, without a separate hierarchy.

2. NS-VLA: Towards Neuro-Symbolic Vision-Language-Action Models

NeurIPS 2026

Vision-language-action models ground natural-language instructions in what the robot sees and then emit actions, but on long-horizon tasks the backbone often cannot see the structure of the instruction, and generalization stays tied to that backbone. A neuro-symbolic approach parses the instruction into a primitive plan tracked by a gated monotone stage pointer, then conditions a chunked policy only on the active sub-instruction. Training first fits the pointer on demonstration segments and then refines the action policy with success-filtered online learning. Even with few demonstrations, it reaches 42.0 on the long-horizon LIBERO-Long suite, 12.7 points above the strongest baseline, and raises real-robot success from 40.0% to 60.0%. Exposing the compositional structure of an instruction lets the policy track stages instead of leaving that work entirely to the backbone.

3. GenPA: Image Generators as Efficient Robot Policies

Fudan UniversityBBeijing Zhongguancun AcademyTsinghua UniversityTU DelftSSunRising AI

A line of work that adapts visual generation models for robot control still treats future-scene prediction as the central link to action learning, even when those future images are no longer generated at inference time. The instruction following, spatial understanding, and visual imagination acquired in image-generation pretraining can support control directly, without a future-visual-prediction stage. One approach fine-tunes an image generation backbone with a lightweight action-regression adapter and reuses the model's native generative objective for perception co-training. At deployment it needs only a single backbone forward pass and action regression, with no visual generation and no iterative action denoising. Without extra large-scale robot-data pretraining, it reaches 99.10% success on LIBERO and 83.79% on the out-of-distribution LIBERO-Plus benchmark. Under compiled inference, one action chunk takes 20.9 ms on a single H100 GPU.

4. Revisiting Embodied Chain-of-Thought for Generalizable Robot Manipulation

NeurIPS 2026

Embodied chain-of-thought aims to connect linguistic reasoning with robot control, but what form that reasoning should take, and how it should be used, has lacked a large-scale test. One study curates the largest embodied chain-of-thought corpus to date, with 978,743 trajectories, and finds that effective traces must ground high-level semantics in concrete linguistic action guidance, such as end-effector motion and image-space trajectories. High-level reasoning alone brings only marginal gains. More importantly, using explicit chain-of-thought as an autoregressive prefix to actions accumulates errors at inference and does not scale reliably. A vision-language-action model is trained with chain-of-thought dropout, then predicts actions directly at inference to bypass that instability. It reaches an 86.9% success rate on LIBERO-Plus and 53.2% on VLABench.

5. FoRe: A Force-Guided Framework for Autonomous Grasp Recovery in Vision-Language-Action Models

Vision-language-action models are already strong at grasp-based manipulation, but after a local grasp fails they often continue into transport instead of retrying, as if proprioception alone were enough to advance the stage and retry supervision were too scarce. Existing recovery methods usually rely on guidance at deployment or on augmenting demonstrations. A force-guided approach uses force feedback to split demonstrations into approach-grasp and transport-placement stages, trains separate policies, and then initializes a dedicated retry policy from the approach-grasp policy and refines it with local reinforcement learning from post-failure states. At inference, a controller combines force signals with grasp-command status to switch stages and invoke retry. On the force-augmented suction benchmark LIBERO-SUCTION, average absolute success gains for two backbones are 18.4% and 6.5%, and the larger real-world gain reaches 25.0%.

6. Learning from Incomplete Actions: Interval Motion Supervision for Vision-Language-Action Policies

Visual labeling of egocentric demonstrations often leaves contiguous gaps in action supervision. If the poses around a gap remain reliable, they still give the net translation and rotation of that interval. One method keeps ordinary flow matching on reliable local actions, and adds a composition objective that constrains the aggregate motion of the missing-action estimates, joining several fixed windows when a gap is longer than the policy horizon. A shared interval branch learns from reliable endpoints and supplies that composition target. On LIBERO, with one fifth of the demonstrations and 70.5% of actions missing, the main method reaches 87.9% mean success over three training runs. On five real-robot tasks, mean success is 59.6%, compared with 48.8% for simply masking the gaps.

7. PACE: Policy Augmentation and Compensation With Explicit Disturbance Estimation for Robust Locomotion

Beihang UniversityNTU

Reinforcement learning for quadruped locomotion often relies on domain randomization, so the policy hedges against every disturbance it might meet. Accurate velocity tracking across changing dynamics, and generalization beyond the sampled range, remain hard to satisfy together. One framework lumps payload changes, center-of-mass shifts, actuator degradation, and external forces into a single joint-space disturbance, estimates it online with a nonlinear observer that does not need joint accelerations, and feeds that estimate into both feedforward torque compensation and the policy observation. Contact forces can be recovered analytically from joint and inertial measurements, without force sensors. Across eleven simulated dynamics conditions, velocity-tracking error falls by 57.0% and 18.1% relative to BASELINE and HIMLOCO. A real robot trained only on flat ground in simulation carries an 11.5 kg payload, nearly its own mass, and clears unseen sixteen-centimeter step-downs.

8. ReActWAM: Action-Consistent Caching for Efficient World-Action Models

University of SydneyHKU

World-action models often generate future visual states alongside robot actions, which makes inference expensive. During closed-loop execution, consecutive cycles often predict similar action sequences, so much of that compute is repeated. Reducing the redundancy requires telling consistent actions apart from changes that need a fresh pass. A training-free method combines action-intent-guided prefix skipping with reuse of predictions inside a chunk. A coarse-to-fine check looks at motion change, visual consistency, and action conflicts, then, for eligible chunks, adapts cached features from first-step differences and resumes at a later denoising step while reusing stable in-chunk predictions. On LIBERO-Long and RoboTwin, it speeds up GPU inference by 1.41 times to 5.44 times across several world-action models, with little drop in task success. On a Franka robot, end-to-end speedup is 1.46 times.

9. SCAP: Stochastic Context for Action Expert Pretraining

Single-modality pretraining of an action expert can limit visual shortcuts, but it may also shut down the vision-language pathway needed for later joint prediction. In a diagnostic model, once visual-action or language-action pretraining disables an initially active joint pathway, later training with complete visual and language inputs cannot restore it, even with unlimited adaptation. A new pretraining stage, used before full-input training, randomly masks visual and text tokens before they enter the vision-language model. In the same diagnostic model, that stochastic context keeps the joint pathway alive, reduces reliance on visual shortcuts, and increases reliance on language instructions. The stage improves four different vision-language-action architectures. In real-robot experiments, average success on three instruction-conditioned manipulation tasks is 76.67%, 25 percentage points above direct full-input pretraining.

10. RoboTrack: Point tracking for real-world robotic manipulation

AAi2

In real-world robot manipulation, a system often needs to track how points on objects or contacts move in the camera view, so it can follow motion through a skill. Turning that point-tracking need into a task that can be trained and compared requires both a benchmark and training data. Ai2 introduces RoboTrack, a benchmark and training data suite for point tracking in robotic manipulation. The suite puts evaluation and training data together, so models can be trained for this task and compared on the same resource. For teams working on manipulation policies and visual feedback, that offers a dedicated public suite for robotic point tracking, with training and evaluation on one shared set of data.
