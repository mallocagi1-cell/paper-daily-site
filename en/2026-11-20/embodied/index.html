Today’s embodied track asks what a policy should look at, what it should remember, and how human experience can become contact a robot can actually use. Future prediction can live in the same visual tokens a vision-language model uses to see the present. Models that look saturated on a standard manipulation benchmark collapse once objects move or instructions are rephrased, and robustness comes from decoupling what to do from training-time positions and wording. Bimanual coordination can be written into the action tokenizer, rather than treating two arms as one arm with twice the joints. Contact-rich work needs touch placed back into three-dimensional space, and where a person would touch an object is not, by itself, where a robot should make contact. 4.48

1. Z-sight: Unifying Vision-Language-Action and Latent World Modeling

Video world-action models jointly generate future frames and actions for control, but pixel-level generation is costly. Recent latent-space policies often align intermediate features or rely on frozen external encoders, separating future prediction from the vision-language model’s own feature space. A unified model instead predicts the future in the same visual token space in which that model perceives the present. A foresight expert and a continuous action expert are coupled with the backbone, jointly denoising future spatial tokens and actions at inference, without pixel reconstruction or an external encoder. It is trained on human video and robot trajectories. Success reaches 81.9% on SIMPLE, 9.6 points above the strongest baseline, and 73.3% across six Unitree G1 real-robot tasks. Future prediction can live in the same visual tokens the model uses to see the present, without a separate pixel-generation path.

4.45

2. RageVLA: Augmentation-Trained Perception and Action for State-of-the-Art Robustness on LIBERO

Vision-language-action models look saturated on LIBERO, yet most of them collapse on LIBERO-PRO as soon as an object moves or the instruction is rephrased. The missing piece is often not scale, but decoupling: what to do is still tied to training-time positions, scenes, and wording. One approach cuts those ties in two places. In the data, objects are moved, tasks are carried into other environments, and instructions are paraphrased by general rules. In the policy, a perception module hands the action model a decoupled list of objects and positions, and a flow-matching model trained from scratch acts on that view. A model of about two hundred million parameters reaches 0.72 success on LIBERO-PRO, against 0.53 for the best published model. When the given object positions are shuffled while the images stay correct, success drops sharply, showing that the policy acts on the layout it perceives. Robustness comes from decoupling what to do from training-time positions and wording.

3. BiCAT: Bimanual Coordination Action Tokenization

Autoregressive vision-language-action models talk to a robot through discrete action tokens, but in bimanual work the two arms share a repertoire of motions and also coordinate through complementary roles. Most existing tokenizers encode both arms as one concatenated vector, so shared motion patterns are not shared and cross-arm dependencies stay tangled inside each token. A bimanual coordination tokenizer shares one motion representation across arms, and uses symmetric cross-arm attention and a joint decoder so each arm’s tokens see its partner. The policy then predicts all tokens of each arm in parallel, generating the left arm first and the right arm conditioned on it. On ten simulation tasks, mean success rises by 17.6 percentage points over the strongest of eight baselines. On six real-world tasks across three embodiments, it succeeds in 113 of 150 trials. The action tokenizer itself can write down bimanual coordination, rather than treating two arms as one arm with twice the joints.

4.04

4. HINT-WAM: Hindsight-Informed Training for World Action Models

World action models couple action generation with predictions of future visual dynamics, but post-training still struggles to turn that into reliable task execution. In offline supervised fine-tuning, a flow-matching action expert is trained only on noised demonstration actions and never sees the intermediate states produced by its own sampler, and task-specific adaptation can also hurt pretrained skills. A hindsight-informed method uses the future video of the demonstration as teacher-only information: the student generates actions along its own denoising trajectory, and the same model, looking also at that future video, supplies a dense action-distribution target. The student still sees only deployment-time inputs, and needs no environmental rewards or simulator interaction. On LIBERO-90, success rises from 78.88% under supervised fine-tuning to 85.18%. An action expert should learn on the states its own sampler visits, not only on noised demonstration actions.

5. 3DTouch: A Tactile-aware VLA Model with 3D Geometric Relations for Dexterous Manipulation

In contact-rich dexterous manipulation, fingers and objects often occlude local geometry, so vision cannot fully capture where contact happens. Visually similar scenes can also hide small differences in relative position, which lead to different contact states. One method expresses scene points and dense five-finger tactile measurements in the robot-base frame, keeping each sensing element’s location and force vector, and then uses hand landmarks and aligned surfaces to describe hand-object and object-object relations explicitly. The action expert can therefore see localized contact forces and relative geometry, and adjust contact and alignment. On five real-world tasks, average success rises by 42 percentage points over the corresponding vision-language-action baseline and by 20 percentage points over the strongest comparison method. Contact-rich manipulation needs touch placed back into 3D space, not just a picture that looks similar.

6. ART: Bridging the Embodiment Gap in 3D Human-to-Robot Affordance Transfer

Three-dimensional affordances are often treated as places on an object that can be interacted with, and human annotations or predictions are used directly as guidance for a robot. Affordance is not a property of the object alone: it is coupled with the agent’s body. Human hands and robot grippers contact and stabilize objects in different ways, so applying human priors directly often fails at execution. A paired dataset annotates every object with both a human prior and simulation-validated robot labels. A transfer network first filters geometrically implausible locations in the human prior, then reconstructs it hierarchically and uses graph diffusion to resolve the topological mismatch into a robot-actionable distribution. On a real 6-degree-of-freedom arm with a parallel-jaw gripper, using the human prior directly yields 58.3% task success, and the transfer raises it to 86.5%. Where a person would touch an object is not, by itself, where a robot should make contact.

7. Allocentric Memory, Egocentric Acting: A Dual-Frame VLA for Urban Aerial Navigation

Language-goal aerial navigation asks a UAV to reach a destination named in natural language, using observations collected in flight. The difficulty is that the egocentric view keeps changing, while the spatial evidence needed for long-range flight is anchored to stable places. A dual-frame model accumulates body-frame observations on a north-up map that starts with geographic and landmark priors, so evidence is preserved when the viewpoint changes. A query over the instruction, current observations, vehicle state, and motion history then retrieves that memory and predicts a map-frame target, and a controller converts it into body-frame commands using the current pose. To reduce target flicker over long horizons, the execution target is fixed to the coordinate-wise median of the first several predictions. On the unseen test set, navigation error falls by 21.2% relative to the best prior result, and path-weighted success rises by 26.1%. A global target can be remembered in world coordinates, while the vehicle only has to execute it from the current pose.

8. A Hierarchical Onboard Vision-Language-Navigation Framework for Aerial Robots in Complex Environments

Aerial robots are expected to do semantic reasoning, decision making, and long-horizon navigation under tight onboard compute, yet existing methods are still limited by scarce 3D data, trajectory feasibility, long-horizon reasoning, and efficiency. A hierarchical framework decouples multimodal reasoning from low-level control, first predicting 3D pose commands and navigation intentions and then turning them into dynamically feasible actions. To ease data scarcity and domain gaps, it uses 3D Gaussian splatting to generate photorealistic flight data automatically, and trains in three stages for grounding, scene reasoning, and complex navigation; a lightweight memory-aware agent then decomposes long-horizon tasks. Onboard token sparsification and model lightweighting speed up inference by 7.1 to 9.4 times on resource-limited UAV platforms while keeping navigation performance. On AerialVLN, success reaches 78.15%, well above end-to-end vision-language-action baselines. Simulation and real-world flights both support this onboard path.

9. Assessing Vision-Language Models for Early Prediction of Unsafe Situations in Robotics

Vision-language models can read images and instructions together, and therefore look well suited to predicting robot failures before they happen, but evidence for that use has been limited. An evaluation studies Qwen, InternVL, and LLaVA-OneVision on three robotic case studies, using video and odometry to classify situations as safe, potentially unsafe, or unsafe, and examines how uncertainty relates to performance. Qwen is strongest overall for classification and prediction reliability, while InternVL shows stronger early-warning ability in several configurations. Whether to add robot-state information, and how much history to keep, depends on the model and the evaluation goal. Qwen’s accuracy can reach 73.37%, yet recall for the unsafe class never exceeds 1.06% in any configuration, because accuracy is inflated by the many safe examples. General-purpose vision-language models can support early safety monitoring, but if the priority is catching already unsafe situations, overall accuracy alone is a too-optimistic conclusion.

10. CoRA-VTLA: Contact-Guided Attention and Queryable Response Memory for Tactile Post-Training

In precision manipulation, a small error in alignment or force can cause jamming or deformation, so contact feedback is essential when adapting a pretrained vision-language-action model to these tasks. A single tactile frame leaves the response to recent actions ambiguous, and fusing touch directly during post-training can disperse the action expert’s visual attention away from contact regions. Lightweight tactile modules retrieve recent contact responses from a queryable action-response memory and combine them with immediate touch, then use contact-guided gaze supervision to concentrate the tactile semantic token’s visual attention on image regions that correspond to fingertip contact. Mean success reaches 73.33% across three simulation tasks, compared with 50% for direct tactile fusion with the same sensor inputs, and 66.7% across three real-robot tasks. Tactile post-training has to remember what the last contact did, and keep the model’s gaze on the place where contact actually happens.
