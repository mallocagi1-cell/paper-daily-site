1. Cap-and-Track: Optimizing LLM Inference Request Dispatch Across Shifting Capacity

Meta

A fixed dispatch rate for language-model inference goes stale as fleets resize and request sizes change. Output length is unknown at admission, and a shared provider pool can also lose capacity. One asynchronous design tracks the current admission ceiling from refusals. A fail-fast check at the saturated resource refuses work, surplus requests sit in durable priority queues, and the dispatcher probes up or backs off from those refusals alone, without predicting output length or retuning a fleet-level rate. In a concurrent same-traffic production A/B, the shaper served a classifier on 43% fewer H100s than a queued, fixed-rate precursor, though lower-priority work waited longer at backfill peaks. In a steady-capacity replay on the real engine, its goodput stayed within 1% of a fixed rate tuned above measured capacity. In its busiest day it handled 1.1 billion requests. Dispatch can follow capacity refusals, without first guessing how long an output will be.

2. FACT: Fixed-Budget Allocation and Cross-Step Training for Speculative Decoding

Speculative decoding speeds up generation by verifying several draft tokens in one pass of the target model, but better draft predictions and longer accepted continuations do not necessarily make generation faster. An experimental framework studies how drafter training, candidate allocation, and execution conditions jointly shape acceptance and latency, using a frozen Llama-3-8B-Instruct target. Backpropagating through predicted-feature rollouts with more weight on early steps raises acceptance by 15% to 24% over uniformly weighted detached training. With a fixed budget and matched widths at each depth, confidence-guided allocation raises the speedup over autoregressive decoding from 1.46× to 2.02× on HumanEval. Even then, changing only host-thread settings can nearly wipe out the speedup while leaving acceptance unchanged. Draft training and candidate allocation have to be judged together with the actual execution conditions.

3. RSIBench-Infra: Can Agents Optimize Their Own Training and Inference Infrastructure at Production Scale?

If an agent is to improve itself, the efficiency of training and inference infrastructure is a practical part of that loop. Existing benchmarks mostly look at a single software layer such as the kernel, and it remains unclear whether agents can deliver end-to-end gains under production workloads. One benchmark offers twenty training and inference tasks across model families and modalities, with deployments of up to thirty-two accelerators. Agents start from runnable official recipes, may change the engine, kernel, and instruction layers, and submit those changes as image layers so the result can be redeployed reproducibly. Independent evaluation then measures end-to-end performance under task-specific quality constraints. Across all task results, 66.2% exceed a validated 1.10× speedup. Current agents can already improve production-scale training and inference, but finding effective strategies and consistently turning local wins into end-to-end gains remains hard. Infrastructure optimization has to be judged end to end, not by whether one layer got faster.

4. LoopCache: Accelerating Long-Context Self-Speculative Decoding for Looped Transformers

Looped Transformers apply a weight-shared block over and over, so a compact model can reach a large effective depth, and each recurrent step keeps its own KV cache. Wave-style self-speculative methods pipeline draft and target tokens across loops in a single step, but they update drafts only at fixed loops and ignore later prediction changes. That wastes work on stale trajectories and loads many loops of KV at once, which inflates memory traffic in long context. One method refines drafts in flight across intermediate loops using output confidence, and it rotates through loop-specific caches so each step’s KV traffic stays in a single loop, while independently retaining caches for a few loop-sensitive shallow layers. In experiments, it is 10.25× faster than WaveFront Decoding and 14.56× faster than LoopSpec on average. Long-context self-speculation has to update drafts and fetch KV only for the loop that is actually needed.

5. NarrowSpec: Draft Hidden Width Need Not Match the Target in Speculative Decoding

In speculative decoding, a small draft model proposes tokens and the target verifies them in one pass. Draft models that read the target’s hidden states usually inherit the same hidden width. This work shows that the draft’s whole residual stream can run at half or even a quarter of that width while acceptance is largely preserved. For a draft that borrows the target’s vocabulary projection, the trained output adapter folds into it, and the narrower projection leaves the logits unchanged up to rounding. At one request and temperature zero, narrowing the draft speeds up end-to-end decoding by 1.13× to 1.44× on a GB10. At larger batches the speedup reaches 1.21× on both GPUs, and no narrow draft is slower than full width. A draft does not have to match the target’s hidden width just to stay aligned with it.

6. DexAttention: Distributed Sparse Attention through Rapid Online Planning and Persistent GPU Execution

Sparse attention cuts computation, but distributed execution may still move unnecessary data and pay a substantial cost on every operation. Mask-aware assignment and scheduling can reduce those costs, yet replanning for a dynamic mask has to be cheaper than the execution time it saves. One system co-designs fast online planning with persistent execution: an assigner uses GPU-accelerated, topology-aware clustering to balance compute and cut communication, a light scheduler orders coarse communication and computation, and a persistent kernel on each GPU enforces fine-grained dependencies and dispatches ready work. On a sixteen-GPU cluster across eleven configurations, the geometric-mean end-to-end training speedup is 1.39× over the fastest applicable baseline. For dynamic video attention, including planning, it is 2.11× to 2.37× faster than ring attention. Planning for distributed sparse attention has to be cheap enough to be worth doing.

7. MoE-Context: Show the Model Only What It Needs

Agents often reread their entire history at every step, which is not how people work: we keep what the moment needs and hold the rest as a short note that can be brought back, without deleting it. One method splits the context into blocks. Before each step, a fast yes-or-no pass decides which blocks the step needs; the rest stay as one-line notes that can be recalled. In the version the authors built, the same model makes the decision and then answers, over a single KV cache, and hidden blocks are masked rather than removed. On a laptop, with Qwen3-1.7B on a synthetic lookup task, showing only the needed block raises accuracy from 93 to 119 of 120, and a masked block is as hidden as a deleted one. The model’s own selection is still the weak part, and simple keyword search picks perfectly on this task. The context need not be reread in full at every step, but the model first has to tell which block the moment actually needs.

8. Vanguard: Knowing When to Trust the Draft in Speculative Vision-Language Agents

Vision-language agents that browse websites, operate phones, or act in homes reason before every action, so every step waits on a large model, a latency and cost that get in the way of deployment. Step-level speculation lets a small model draft each step and calls the large model only when a draft is rejected, but the outcome rests on the judge. A judge that reads only the step’s text cannot tell whether that step fits the screen in view. A training-free judge, with the screenshot visible, assigns each candidate step a probability of being right in one forward pass, keeps it if it clears a threshold, and otherwise lets the large model write the step. On one Mind2Web test set, a 2B draft screened by this judge reaches 66.5% step success, above the 8B target alone at 64.4%. On the other test set the figures are 66.8% and 63.3%. The large model writes only a minority of the steps, and far more closed-loop episodes are completed. Step-level speculation works only if the judge can look at the screen and decide whether the draft can be trusted.

9. eFAIR: Fair LLM Serving under Hourly Electricity Commitments

Datacenters can buy electricity in day-ahead markets by committing to an amount for each hour. If a language-model service runs inside those hourly budgets, raising aggregate throughput alone can still leave some requests waiting, or much slower than desired. Fair serving has to improve service for the worst-served requests and still reserve electricity for later arrivals whose timing and demand are unknown. The paper compares requests by normalized throughput: a request’s actual output rate divided by the product of its standalone throughput and its service entitlement, and under the hourly electricity constraint it prioritizes the requests with the lowest ratio. The scheduler aligns dispatch, budget allocation, and GPU frequency selection to a shared normalized-throughput target. On H20 and H200 configurations with thirty-two replicas each, the tenth percentile of normalized throughput rises 28.0% to 31.0% over the best budget-feasible baseline, while absolute deviations from the hourly commitment stay below 0.26%. Under an hourly electricity cap, fairness cannot be read from aggregate throughput alone.

10. AGoQ: Activation and Gradient Quantization for Memory-Efficient Distributed Training of LLMs

Cutting the GPU memory needed to train large language models often depends on quantization, but current methods struggle with near-four-bit activations and eight-bit gradients, and often slow convergence or lose accuracy. One method allocates activation bit-widths by layer type and pipeline stage so activation storage is close to four bits, stores gradients in eight bits, and uses an accuracy-preserving eight-bit All-Reduce to shorten communication. On two GPU clusters of up to sixty-four GPUs, with LLaMA models from 8B to 32B, memory falls by up to 52%. Training speed rises by up to 1.34× compared with systems such as Megatron-LM, COAT, and DeepSpeed, while pretraining loss still converges and downstream accuracy stays comparable. If activations and gradients are to be quantized more aggressively, bit-widths have to be set per layer, and communication has to keep accuracy at that low width.
