Today’s generative track asks the same question: how generative recommendation can still be computable, credible, and usable under production constraints. One line of work first gets the long-input, wide-beam inference pipeline right, rather than rushing to write a custom kernel. Multimodal Semantic ID diffusion has to ask whether a modality actually narrows the candidates still in the catalog. A flow-network objective written for an online setting cannot be copied onto offline logs, and preference optimization can be hacked on unsampled items. Long histories have to be compressed into an interest sketch first, and automatically evolving a recommender needs feedback that points to the failure and the next change. Medication combinations put safety constraints into inference itself, and medical concepts write hierarchy into the identifier. Whether generation is usable increasingly depends on whether those constraints are written into the generation process.

1. Optimize the Cob, Then the Kernel: Optimizing Beam Search for Generative Recommendation Inference

Generative recommendation inference is unlike ordinary language-model serving: inputs are long, beam search is wide, and only a few output tokens are produced. Existing engines often treat each beam as an independent sequence in a batch, so the shared prompt prefix is reread from the KV cache, and each generation step is a separate engine call, with host-side beam work and runtime cache-block management sitting between steps. This approach concatenates beams along the sequence dimension with Tree Attention to remove those repeated KV reads. It also adds precomputed partial-attention masks, a static KV cache, graph compilation, operator fusion, and vocabulary-constrained decoding. Without custom kernels, end-to-end latency on Ascend NPUs falls by up to 25× relative to vLLM, and generation latency is comparable to a strong baseline that does use custom kernels. The generation pipeline should be optimized as a whole before anyone reaches for a custom kernel.

2. CONCORD-Rec: Catalog-Conditioned Modality Utility for Semantic-ID Diffusion Recommendation

In multimodal generative recommendation with discrete Semantic IDs, a historical image or text does not always reduce ambiguity in the catalog; sometimes it only changes token predictions. When a modality is missing, degraded, or in conflict with another, uniform fusion can also carry misleading content into iterative denoising. One method supervises a modality gate with counterfactual catalog-partition utility. On the same feasible catalog and a partially masked Semantic ID, it compares the loss on the target group with and without a modality, then allocates bounded modality corrections by position and availability, while keeping a behavior-only prediction as fallback. On MicroLens-100K, NDCG@20 rises 4.00% over uniform fusion in the clean setting, and 7.95% when 30% of modalities are missing. Whether a modality helps depends on whether it narrows the candidates still in play.

3. Improving LLM-Based Recommenders with Conservative Generative Flow Networks

Generative flow networks have been used to raise diversity and ease popularity bias in LLM-based recommendation, but most of their objectives were written under online-style assumptions. Offline recommendation can learn only from a fixed logged dataset, so token transitions cover only the prefixes that dataset supports. Applying a sub-trajectory balance objective as if the setting were online becomes non-identifiable, and probability mass can be assigned to regions the data never supports. The paper formalizes this failure and names three sources that drive the learned policy away from the policy implied by the data: flow overestimation, forward mass leakage, and backward compensation. A conservative objective then explicitly penalizes unsupported forward flow and learns the policy under dataset constraints. An offline recommender cannot borrow an online flow-network objective unchanged.

4. Mitigating Reward Hacking in LLM-based Recommendation: A Preference Optimization Approach

When large language models are adapted for recommendation, preference methods such as DPO sharpen pairwise discrimination, but they remain open to reward hacking: the model exploits flaws in the reward signal, training metrics rise, and genuine recommendation quality does not. From a gradient view, pairwise updates barely move the ordering between a positive item and unsampled negatives, creating an insensitive region. Under the Bradley-Terry model, such regions can occupy a substantial share of the preference space, and rankings inevitably drift, because the model never really learns to separate a positive from negatives that never appeared in a pair. The method introduces pseudo-negatives and uses simulated preference optimization to restrain this kind of hacking, so updates can also reach rankings that were almost untouched before. Pairwise preference alone does not adequately constrain how unsampled items are ranked.

5. Token-Efficient Long-Term Interest Sketching and Internalized Reasoning for LLM-based Recommendation

Using a language model to reason about user preference in recommendation runs into two practical problems. Histories are often long and noisy, sometimes spanning hundreds of items, and truncation throws away signals needed for long-term interest. In a decoder-only model, chain-of-thought also has to emit rationale tokens one by one, which makes inference latency hard to accept in production. One framework works on both sides at once. It does not prompt the raw history as a whole. It encodes and clusters item descriptions into a compact, token-bounded interest sketch that keeps persistent preferences and suppresses noise, so the model can still see long-term interest inside a limited token budget. It also internalizes the reasoning so rationale tokens need not be generated at every request. Recommendation reasoning then need not swallow the whole noisy history, nor pay the cost of writing a long rationale for every score. Long-term interest has to be compressed first, and the reasoning need not be written out every time.

6. Self-EvolveRec: Self-Evolving Recommender Systems with LLM-based Directional Feedback

Automatic design of recommenders is often boxed in by a human-defined operator space when it uses neural architecture search, so innovation can happen only among predefined operators. Recent language-model code evolution moves into an open program space, but it still leans on scalar scores such as NDCG or hit rate, which do not say where the model failed or which way to change it. One framework adds directional feedback: a user simulator supplies qualitative critiques, a diagnosis tool checks the model quantitatively from the inside, and the diagnosis criteria evolve with the recommender architecture, so the evaluation ruler does not stay frozen on an old design. Starting from SASRec on the CDs dataset, NDCG@5 rises from 0.3559 to 0.3865, above DeepEvolve from the same seed at 0.3610. Automatically revising a recommender needs feedback that points to the failure and the next change, not only a score.

3.10

7. Steering Diffusion Models Towards Credible Content Recommendation

Diffusion models have entered recommender systems because they can capture complex distributions of item content and user behavior, and their recommendation accuracy is often strong. Existing methods, though, often chase accuracy alone and do not ask whether the recommended content is credible, so they may generate untrustworthy content such as fake news or misinformation, with real social cost. One method tries to steer diffusion toward more credible content recommendation. It rewrites the diffusion objective so generation is encouraged when conditioned on preference-related signals and discouraged when conditioned on untrustworthy content, and it uses a disentangled diffusion process to lessen the influence of that content on generation while aiming to keep recommendation accuracy. Content recommendation cannot be judged by accuracy alone; what is generated has to be credible as well.

3.05

8. RxDiff: Discrete Diffusion for Medication Recommendation with Inference-Time Safety Control

Medication recommendation has to produce drug combinations that are effective and that keep harmful drug-drug interactions low. The difficulty lies in the combinatorics of a prescription: drugs affect one another and cannot be treated as independent labels to be scored one by one. Discriminative methods often predict each drug on its own and miss those dependencies. Autoregressive methods introduce sequential dependence, but they impose an arbitrary generation order and accumulate errors. Both mostly penalize interactions at training time, and have limited ability to regulate them for an individual prescription at inference. This work recasts medication recommendation as discrete diffusion: iterative denoising can revise choices in both directions, and an interaction-aware adjustment at each inference step lets safety constraints be enforced while the prescription is being formed, not only in the training loss. Safety constraints on a drug combination should be enforceable during inference, not only during training.

9. SMI: Ontology-Grounded Semantic IDs for Hierarchy-Aware Medical Concept Representation

Biomedical language models give strong representations for clinical tasks, but they often miss the hierarchy among medical concepts, and the problem is sharper for rare, fine-grained clinical codes, because a hierarchy-agnostic vector becomes less informative as a concept gets rarer. A training-free method defines each diagnosis, procedure, or medication concept’s Semantic ID as the coarse-to-fine path through an expert ontology, then aggregates frozen biomedical embeddings across those levels so coarse clinical meaning can support a fine-grained code. The base version aggregates levels uniformly, and a variant can select level weights on validation data. On medication recommendation in MIMIC-IV, the level-weighted variant reaches an AUPRC of 77.6%, an F-score of 65.3%, and a Jaccard score of 50.5%, significantly better than the strongest baseline. Once hierarchy is written into the identifier itself, rare concepts can borrow meaning from the levels above them.
